A pressure switch that is starting to fail costs $150 to $350 to replace on a scheduled Tuesday. The submersible pump it burns out over the following winter costs $1,800 to $4,500, and it usually quits on a Sunday. That gap is the entire argument for annual service, and it is why well maintenance cost is worth understanding before anything breaks.

Most homeowners on private wells in Chelan, Douglas and Grant counties have never had anyone look at the system. The well was there when they bought the house. It has worked every morning since. So the honest question is not "what does maintenance cost" but "what am I actually buying for that money, and does it pay for itself."

Here is the straight version, with the numbers we quote, what a visit includes, and the cases where we tell people to save their money.

## What an Annual Well Service Visit Actually Includes

There is no license or standard that defines a "well tune-up," so the contents vary by company. Ask before you book. When we come out for a scheduled inspection, the visit is billed at our normal service and diagnosis rate of $150 to $250, and if the visit turns up something we fix that day, that fee is applied toward the repair.

A useful annual visit covers the whole system, not just the pump. That means the wellhead and cap, the pressure tank, the switch and gauge, the electrical side, and the way the system behaves under a real draw.

- **Wellhead and cap.** The cap should be tight, undamaged, and standing above grade with the ground sloping away from it. A cracked cap or a rodent nest inside the casing is a drinking water problem, not a plumbing one.
- **Pressure tank charge.** We check the air pressure in the tank against the pump's cut-in setting. A tank that has lost its charge is the most common thing we find, and it is the thing most likely to kill a healthy pump.
- **Pressure switch and gauge.** Contacts get pitted and burned. A switch that is arcing on every start is a $150 to $350 part that is quietly shortening the motor's life.
- **Amp draw on the motor.** A pump pulling above its rated amps is working against something: worn impellers, a restriction, a voltage problem, or a well that is not producing what it used to.
- **Cycle behavior.** We run water, watch a full on and off cycle, and count the run time. Short cycling shows up here before it shows up in your water bill or your pump failure.
- **Water clarity and basic observations.** Sand, cloudiness, iron staining or a sulfur smell all point somewhere specific and change what we recommend next.

## What Water Testing Adds to Your Annual Well Maintenance Cost

Equipment is half the job. The other half is the water, and it is the half people skip because nothing looks wrong.

The EPA recommends that private well owners [test their water at least once a year](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells) for coliform bacteria, nitrates, total dissolved solids and pH. Nitrates matter here in particular. This is orchard country, with irrigation and fertilizer on the landscape, and nitrate contamination has no taste, no color and no smell.

Lab fees are not our charge and they vary by lab and panel, so treat this as a general estimate: a basic bacteria and nitrate screen commonly runs somewhere in the $25 to $150 range, and a broad panel adding metals or arsenic costs more. Call a certified lab directly for their current price list. If you want context on which panel to ask for and how to read the results when they come back, our guide to [well water testing in Wenatchee](/blog/well-water-testing-wenatchee/) walks through it.

Budget the test separately from the service visit. They are two different things and one does not substitute for the other.

## Three Field Visits and What They Cost

Numbers in a table are abstract. Here is what the range looks like on actual properties, with identifying details removed.

**A 1990s manufactured home on a 220-foot well outside East Wenatchee.** The owner booked an inspection because the pump seemed noisier than it used to be. The pump was fine. The pressure switch contacts were pitted and the gauge was reading about 8 psi high, which meant the system was running at a different pressure than anyone thought. Switch replaced on the spot, and because the repair happened that visit, the diagnosis fee rolled into it. The whole call landed inside the $150 to $350 switch range. The noise was the switch chattering on start, not the motor.

**An orchard house above Cashmere running a submersible and a 44-gallon bladder tank.** No complaints at all. The tank had lost its air charge and the pump was cycling on and off roughly every 40 seconds under a garden hose draw. The tank was 14 years old with a failed bladder, so it was replaced rather than recharged, landing near the middle of the $600 to $1,800 range. The pump was 9 years old and healthy. Left alone, that cycling pattern would very likely have cost them a submersible replacement at $1,800 to $4,500 inside a couple of seasons, and it would have failed in July with the orchard drawing hard.

**A cabin near Leavenworth left unheated over the Christmas break.** The owners arrived to no water and called on a Sunday. A supply line in the pump house had split at a fitting and the well wiring had been chewed at a splice by rodents that moved in for the winter. The repair fell in the $300 to $1,200 wire and drop pipe band, plus the $150 to $300 after-hours premium because it was a weekend in January with no water at the property. A fall shutdown visit at $150 to $250, with the pump house drained and the exposed lines insulated, would have covered it.

None of these were emergencies when they were found. Two of them became emergencies later for people who did not look.

## When Maintenance Money Is Not Worth Spending

We will tell you to skip it in a few situations, because paying for a visit that changes nothing is not maintenance, it is a subscription.

If your well and pump were installed in the last two or three years by a competent installer, and nothing has changed in how the system behaves, an annual equipment inspection is low value. Keep testing the water annually. Look at the wellhead yourself each spring. Call when something changes.

If you are handy and willing to learn the basics, a lot of the monthly walk-through is yours to do for free: listen to a full pump cycle, note the cut-in and cut-out pressures on the gauge, look at the wellhead, check the pump house for damp spots and chewed insulation. Write the numbers down. Trends are what matter, and a homeowner who records gauge readings monthly will spot a drifting system faster than an annual visit will. Our [well maintenance schedule](/blog/well-maintenance-schedule/) lays out the whole routine.

And if the pump is 15 years old, the tank is original, and the quote to keep it going is climbing past a third of replacement cost, stop spending on maintenance. Plan the replacement on your schedule instead of the pump's. That conversation belongs in [well pump replacement](/well-pump-replacement-wenatchee/), not in a service visit.

Where site conditions decide the answer, they decide it. Well depth, water chemistry, how hard the system runs in irrigation season and whether the wellhead sits in a flood-prone spot all change how often a system needs eyes on it. A pump running a house plus orchard irrigation in Quincy is under a completely different load than a two-person household in town.

## How to Keep Your Annual Well Maintenance Cost Down

Four things do most of the work.

**Fix the pressure tank early.** It is the cheapest single decision on this list. A tank at $600 to $1,800 protects a pump at $1,800 to $4,500, and a failing tank gives you months of warning if anyone is listening. Read [pressure tank waterlogged](/blog/pressure-tank-waterlogged/) for the symptoms.

**Book in spring or fall, not in a crisis.** The after-hours premium is $150 to $300 on top of the repair, and it is entirely avoidable on anything that is not a true emergency. Spring, before irrigation season loads the pump, is the best window here.

**Do not let small electrical faults ride.** A breaker that trips "only sometimes" or a pump that hums before it starts is a $200 to $450 control box or capacitor problem today. Ignored, it is a burned motor and a pump pull.

**Keep records.** Well depth, pump horsepower, install date, gauge readings, and the last water test. It shortens every future diagnosis, which means you pay for less diagnostic time. Washington keeps well construction records and rules through the [Department of Ecology](https://ecology.wa.gov/regulations-permits/permits-certifications/wells), and your well log is worth tracking down if you do not have it.

Some work genuinely needs a rig and a crew: pulling a submersible from a deep well, repairing casing, or anything that involves opening the well itself. That is not a homeowner job and it is not a handyman job, both for safety and because the well is your drinking water supply.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does an annual well maintenance visit cost?

Our service call and diagnosis runs $150 to $250, and that covers a full inspection of the wellhead, pressure tank, switch, gauge, electrical connections and cycle behavior. If we find something and you have us fix it on the same visit, that fee is applied toward the repair. Water testing is a separate lab fee and is not included.

### How often should a private well be serviced?

Once a year is the standard for the equipment side, and spring is the best timing in North Central Washington because it puts you ahead of irrigation season. Test the water annually as well, which the EPA recommends for all private wells. Systems under heavy load, such as a house plus orchard or livestock demand, or wells with known sand or iron problems, are worth looking at more often.

### Does well maintenance actually make the pump last longer?

It removes the things that kill pumps early, which is not the same as extending the life of a healthy motor. Short cycling from a failed pressure tank, running dry, and electrical faults account for most of the premature failures we see, and all three announce themselves months ahead. Catching those is the entire value, and it depends on somebody actually checking.

### Can I do well maintenance myself instead of paying for it?

You can do the monthly walk-through yourself: listen to a pump cycle, record the gauge readings, inspect the wellhead and the pump house. That is genuinely useful and it costs nothing. What you cannot safely do at home is measure motor amp draw, set a tank charge correctly against the switch settings, or open the well casing, so plan on a professional visit for those.
