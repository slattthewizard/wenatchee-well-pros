## Before the Crew Ever Shows Up: How Installation Day Gets Scheduled

Most well pump installations aren't a surprise. They follow a diagnosis, either a pump that's clearly dying (weak pressure, short cycling, a humming motor that won't start) or one that's already dead. Either way, a tech usually visits first to confirm the well depth, pump setting, wire size, and pipe material before a crew shows up with equipment.

That first visit matters more than people expect. Ordering the wrong horsepower pump or showing up without enough drop pipe turns a half-day job into a two-day job. We pull your well log when we can, since Washington requires one to be filed with the state, and it tells us your casing size and original depth before we ever touch the wellhead.

Once the pump and materials are confirmed, installation day gets scheduled around your well's depth. A 150-foot well in town is a different job than a 400-foot well up toward Quincy, and a good crew blocks the calendar accordingly instead of squeezing a deep well into a rushed morning slot.

## Hour One of Well Pump Installation Day: Arrival and Shutdown

The truck usually rolls up between 8 and 9 in the morning. First thing the crew does is walk the site, confirm the wellhead location, and set up the hoist or tripod that pulls the pipe straight up out of the casing without kinking wire or scraping the pipe against the sides.

Power gets shut off at the breaker before anyone touches the well cap. That's not optional. Well circuits run on 240 volts, and a tech isn't opening a live wellhead for any reason. With power confirmed off, the pressure tank gets drained and the cap comes off, exposing the pitless adapter and the top of the drop pipe.

Dana, who owns a small place outside Cashmere, watched her crew do all of this in about twenty minutes flat before they'd even started pulling pipe. "I thought they'd just yank it," she said. "They spent more time making sure the power was dead than I expected." That caution is normal, and it's a good sign, not a delay.

## Pulling the Old Pump: What Comes Up From Down Below

This is the part homeowners find most interesting, and often the most telling. The crew pulls the drop pipe up in sections, usually 20-foot lengths, disconnecting and stacking each one as the pump rises toward the surface.

What comes up tells the story of what killed the pump. Scarred impellers usually mean sand or grit ground the pump down early, common in Columbia Valley wells. A pump caked in orange slime points to iron bacteria rather than simple wear. A pump that looks fine but just stopped running often means the failure was electrical, in the wiring or control box, not the pump itself.

Marisol, whose well sits at 380 feet outside Quincy, had a pump pulled last spring that looked almost new on the outside. The motor had burned out from a failing check valve that let water fall back down the pipe every time the pump shut off, forcing it to restart against a full column of water hundreds of times a day. That extra strain is exactly the kind of thing a good crew catches on the way up, not after the new pump fails the same way.

For most 100 to 250 foot wells, pulling the old pump takes 30 to 60 minutes. Deeper wells, or a pump that's stuck or corroded to the pipe, can stretch that closer to 90 minutes.

## Checking the Wire and Drop Pipe Before Anything Goes Back Down

Once the old pump is up, the crew doesn't just uncoil new wire and drop a new pump. They inspect what's already there, because reusing sound wire and pipe saves money, and replacing bad wire now costs far less than pulling the whole system again in a year.

The wire gets checked splice by splice for corrosion, cracked insulation, or water intrusion, especially at any point where it was spliced rather than run as one continuous length. Drop pipe gets checked for pitting, thread wear, and whether it's galvanized steel (older, heavier, more prone to corrosion) or poly pipe (lighter, more common in newer installs).

Frank, out on a benchland property above Chelan, figured his crew would just swap the pump and be done. Instead they found a wire splice from the original 1990s installation, the insulation gone brittle and starting to crack. Replacing that splice added about $180 to his bill that day. Skipping it would have meant pulling a brand-new pump again within a year or two when that splice finally failed underwater.

This is also when a check valve gets inspected or replaced if it's original to the well, since a bad check valve is one of the more common reasons a "new" pump seems to struggle from day one.

## Setting the New Pump: Depth, the Pitless Adapter, and Torque

With the wire and pipe checked, the new pump gets wired to the drop pipe and lowered into the casing in the same sections it came up in, torqued at each joint so nothing works loose under the weight of hundreds of feet of water-filled pipe.

Depth matters more than most homeowners realize. Set a pump too high and it can suck air during low water periods, especially on wells that draw down in late summer irrigation season. Set it too low and it works through more sediment than it needs to. Your original well log and the water level noted during the first visit tell the crew exactly where to land it, usually a set distance above the bottom of the casing or the well's screen.

The pitless adapter is the fitting that connects the underground pipe to the horizontal line running to your house, and it's what lets the whole assembly seat properly without a pit or vault at the wellhead. A misaligned pitless connection is one of the more common causes of a leak that shows up weeks later, so this step gets checked carefully before the cap goes back on.

On a sandy well, like several we service around Quincy and the lower valley, this is also when a sand shroud gets added if the old pump's impellers showed heavy wear. It's a small part, usually $100 to $250, that can add years to a new pump's life on a well that runs gritty.

## Chlorination and Startup: How Your Water Gets Restored

Before the system gets buttoned up for good, the well gets shocked with a chlorine solution. Opening a well cap, even carefully, introduces some risk of bacteria or debris getting in, and a fresh pump installation is the standard moment to disinfect the whole system rather than wait and find out later with a bad coliform test. We cover the mechanics of that process in detail in our guide to [well chlorination and shock treatment](/blog/well-chlorination-shock/), including how long to let it sit before running water again.

Once the cap is sealed and the chlorine is in, power gets restored at the breaker and the pressure tank refills. The crew runs water at an outside spigot first to flush chlorinated water away from the house plumbing, then checks pressure and flow at a few fixtures inside, kitchen sink, a bathroom, sometimes a hose bib, to confirm steady pressure and no air in the lines.

This is also when the crew tests amp draw and run time on the new pump, confirming it's pulling power the way the pump's spec sheet says it should. A pump running outside its expected amp range on day one is a red flag worth catching before the crew leaves, not after a callback three weeks later.

If your home's water has been off for more than a few hours, expect some cloudy or air-sputtered water for the first several minutes as lines clear. That's normal and settles quickly.

## When Installation Day Runs Long

Not every job wraps in an afternoon, and it's worth knowing what pushes installation day into a second visit or a longer first one.

A stuck pump, one that's corroded to the drop pipe or wedged against the casing, can add an hour or more just to free it safely without damaging the well itself. A collapsed or damaged casing sometimes isn't visible until the old pump comes up, and that's a different, more involved repair than a straightforward swap. Wells with no accurate log or water level record on file also take longer, since the crew has to determine depth and setting on site rather than confirming it in advance.

None of that should be handled without a licensed pump contractor. Working a stuck pump loose, assessing a damaged casing, or touching anything electrical at the wellhead are jobs where the risk of injury or a ruined well goes up fast for anyone without the right equipment and experience. If your installation day hits a snag like this, that's exactly the moment to lean on your contractor's judgment rather than push to finish it yourself.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does well pump installation day actually take?

Most wells between 100 and 250 feet deep take 3 to 5 hours from arrival to water restored, which covers the vast majority of homes in the Wenatchee Valley. Deeper wells past 350 feet, a stuck pump, or a pressure tank swap at the same time can stretch that to a full day. Your contractor should give you a realistic time estimate once they know your well's depth and condition.

### Will I have water during well pump installation day?

No, water is off for the duration of the job since the pressure tank has to be drained and the system depressurized before the old pump comes out. Plan around a few hours without water, and fill a couple of jugs beforehand if you have pets, plants, or a coffee habit to manage. Most crews restore working water the same day in nearly every case.

### Do I need to be home for the entire installation?

You don't need to watch the whole process, but it helps to be reachable in case the crew finds something unexpected, like a damaged casing or a well depth that doesn't match old records. Being home at the start to confirm access and at the end to test water at your own fixtures is the most useful time to be present. Many homeowners step away during the multi-hour middle stretch and come back for startup.

### Why does the crew shock the well with chlorine after installing the new pump?

Opening the well cap and pulling pipe creates a chance for bacteria or debris to get introduced, even with careful work, so a chlorine shock treatment resets the well to a clean baseline before it goes back into daily use. It's standard practice after any job that opens the casing, not a sign that something went wrong. The chlorinated water gets flushed out through an outside spigot before anyone uses water inside the house.
