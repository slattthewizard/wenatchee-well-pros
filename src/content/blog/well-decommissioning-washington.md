## Why Washington Takes Abandoned Wells So Seriously

An unused well isn't just a hole. It's a direct, unfiltered pipeline from the surface straight into the aquifer your neighbors drink from.

Here in North Central Washington, most rural homes pull from the same groundwater. A single open or poorly sealed well can let surface runoff, fertilizer, orchard spray residue, septic leakage, or a drowned rodent flow right down into that shared water supply. The casing that once protected the water becomes the contamination shortcut. The [EPA's private well program](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells) flags abandoned wells as one of the most common preventable threats to rural groundwater.

There's also the physical hazard. Old dug wells in particular, the wide ones from the 1940s and 50s, are big enough for a kid, a dog, or a deer to fall into. We've seen them hiding under a sheet of plywood with thirty years of pine needles on top.

And then there's liability. In Washington, the well stays the property owner's responsibility forever, even if it was drilled three owners ago. If your abandoned well contaminates a neighbor's water or someone gets hurt, that lands on you. Sellers around the Wenatchee Valley are also finding that lenders and buyers increasingly demand proof of proper decommissioning before closing. ## Washington Well Decommissioning Rules: What the Law Requires

Washington regulates wells under chapter 18.104 RCW and chapter 173-160 WAC, administered by the [Washington Department of Ecology](https://ecology.wa.gov/water-shorelines/water-supply/wells). The rules are specific, and they exist because "filled with whatever was handy" wells kept poisoning aquifers.

Here's what the state requires when a well is permanently taken out of service:

**A licensed driller has to do the work.** Decommissioning must be performed by a Washington licensed water well contractor, the same license required to drill a well. There's a narrow exemption for owners working on their own property, but you'd still have to follow every technical standard, file the same paperwork, and somehow place grout properly at depth without a grout pump and tremie pipe. In practice, this is licensed-driller work.

**A Notice of Intent gets filed first.** Ecology must be notified before work begins, at least 72 hours ahead. Your contractor files this. It's routine paperwork, but skipping it can void the whole job in the state's eyes.

**The well gets sealed bottom to top with approved materials.** Not dirt. Not gravel. Not old concrete chunks. The rules require sealing material like bentonite or neat cement grout, placed from the bottom of the well upward so there are no gaps or bridges. Pumps, piping, wiring, and debris come out first.

**The casing gets handled correctly.** Depending on the well's construction, the casing may be perforated so grout can squeeze into the space around it, cut off below ground surface, or pulled entirely. The goal is restoring the natural barrier between water-bearing zones that the well punched through.

**A decommissioning report gets filed afterward.** Within 30 days of finishing, the contractor files a report with Ecology documenting how the well was sealed. That report is your proof, and it matters at sale time. Keep a copy with your property records.

One more wrinkle that surprises people: most residential wells in our area are permit-exempt wells, meaning they never needed a water right to drill. That exemption covers use, not abandonment. The decommissioning rules apply to every well, exempt or not. |---|---|---|

Why the spread? Sealing material is the main variable. A 300 foot well holds a lot more grout than a 60 foot one, and bentonite isn't cheap by the ton. Wide old dug wells can swallow surprising amounts of fill and engineered seal. And if a previous owner dropped a pump down the hole in 1987, somebody has to fish it out before grouting can start.

## The Decommissioning Process, Step by Step

Most residential decommissioning jobs take one to two days on site. Here's what actually happens.

### 1. Site assessment and records check

We pull the original well log from Ecology's database if one exists, which tells us depth, casing size, and construction. No log? We measure and camera the well ourselves. This is where we find the surprises, like the Hendersons' place near Chelan, where the "60 foot well" on a faded 1971 log turned out to be 240 feet with a liner inside the original casing. Better to know before the bid than after.

### 2. Notice of Intent filed with Ecology

We file the NOI and wait out the notice period. You don't touch this paperwork, it's on us.

### 3. Equipment removal

The pump, drop pipe, wiring, and pitless adapter all come out. Anything dropped or stuck gets fished. The hole has to be clear top to bottom, because grout poured on top of a stuck pump leaves an unsealed void below it. That void is exactly the contamination pathway the law is trying to close.

### 4. Sealing from the bottom up

Bentonite chips or grout, or neat cement, goes in through a tremie pipe lowered to the bottom of the well. We fill upward in measured lifts, verifying the level as we go. On cased wells we may perforate the casing at set intervals so the seal pushes into the annular space outside the pipe.

### 5. Surface finish and reporting

The casing gets cut off below grade, the last few feet are sealed, and the surface is restored so you can mow, plant, or pave over it. Within 30 days, the decommissioning report goes to Ecology and a copy goes in your file.

One honest warning. We sometimes get called to "finish" a well someone already half filled with rock and concrete chunks. Clearing a botched DIY fill costs more than decommissioning an untouched well, sometimes double, because drilling out debris is slow work. If you've got an open well, leave it capped and call a licensed contractor first.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I decommission my own well in Washington?

Technically there's a limited owner exemption, but you'd still have to meet every standard in chapter 173-160 WAC, file the Notice of Intent and decommissioning report, and place grout from the bottom up through a tremie pipe. Almost no homeowner has the equipment to do that correctly. A bad seal can contaminate the aquifer and leaves you liable, so this is one job that belongs with a licensed well contractor.

### How much does it cost to decommission a well in Washington?

Most residential wells in the Wenatchee area run $1,200 to $4,000 depending on depth, diameter, and access. Shallow wells under 60 feet often come in under $1,800, while deep wells past 250 feet can reach $6,000. Stuck pumps, dropped debris, or a previous DIY fill attempt add cost because the hole has to be cleared before sealing.

### Do I have to decommission an old well before selling my property?

State law requires unused wells to be properly decommissioned regardless of a sale, but a sale is usually when it gets enforced. Lenders, inspectors, and buyers' agents flag abandoned wells, and many lenders won't close until you produce Ecology's decommissioning report. Handling it before listing is cheaper and faster than doing it under deadline during escrow.

### How long does well decommissioning take?

Most residential jobs take one to two days on site, plus the 72 hour notice period before work starts. Wells with stuck equipment, collapsed sections, or difficult access can stretch to several days. Start to finish, including paperwork, plan on one to two weeks, so don't wait until the week before closing to schedule it.
