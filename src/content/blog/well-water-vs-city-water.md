A family on Wenatchee city water pays roughly $700 to $1,000 a year for water and sewer, every year, forever. A family on a private well pays close to zero most years, then eats a $1,500 to $4,500 repair bill the day the pump finally quits. So which one's actually the better deal?

Fair question, and if you're house hunting anywhere between Cashmere and Moses Lake, it matters a lot more than the kitchen countertops. The well water vs city water debate isn't really about which water is "better." It's about cost, control, and how much responsibility you want to carry yourself. We've worked on wells all over Chelan, Douglas, and Grant counties, so here's the honest version. We'll break down what each option really costs, how the water quality compares, and the trade-offs that catch new well owners off guard.

## Well Water vs City Water: The Real Cost Breakdown

Let's start with the numbers, because this is where most online comparisons get fuzzy.

City water in our area runs most households $50 to $90 a month once you fold in base rates and usage, and that's before sewer charges. Rates only move one direction. Wenatchee and East Wenatchee have both bumped utility rates in recent years, and there's no opting out.

Well water flips the math. Your "water bill" is the electricity to run the pump, usually $5 to $20 a month for a typical household. The catch is that you own the entire system: pump, pressure tank, wiring, pipe, casing, and cap. When something breaks, there's no utility truck coming for free.

Here's how a typical 20-year stretch shakes out for a household in our area:

| Expense | City Water (20 yrs) | Private Well (20 yrs) |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly water bill | $12,000 to $21,600 | $0 |
| Pump electricity | Included in bill | $1,200 to $4,800 |
| Pump replacement (1x) | N/A | $1,800 to $4,500 |
| Pressure tank (1x) | N/A | $800 to $2,000 |
| Annual water testing | N/A | $600 to $1,500 |
| Misc repairs (switches, wiring) | N/A | $500 to $1,500 |
| **Rough 20-year total** | **$12,000 to $21,600** | **$4,900 to $14,300** |

Over the long haul, the well usually wins on raw dollars. The difference is how the costs arrive. City water is a steady drip you barely notice. Well costs show up in lumps, often at the worst possible moment.

Marcy in East Wenatchee learned that the hard way last August. She'd owned her place for six years and never spent a dime on the well. Then on a 102 degree Saturday, the water just stopped. The submersible pump was original to the 1998 well, and it was done. Total bill: $3,200 for a new pump, wire, and drop pipe. She still came out ahead of two decades of city bills, but it didn't feel that way writing the check.

## Water Quality: What's Actually in Your Glass

This is where opinions get loud and facts get scarce. Here's the straight version.

### City water: treated, tested, and chlorinated

Municipal water in Wenatchee, East Wenatchee, and Moses Lake is tested constantly and has to meet [EPA drinking water standards](https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water). If something goes wrong, the utility has to notify you. That's a real benefit, and we won't pretend otherwise.

The trade is treatment. City water carries chlorine or chloramine, and some folks can taste it. The mineral content is managed but not eliminated. You get safety and consistency, not necessarily the best tasting glass of water in the valley.

### Well water: nobody tests it but you

Private wells aren't regulated for ongoing water quality in Washington. Once the well is drilled and approved, testing is entirely on the owner. The [Washington State Department of Health](https://doh.wa.gov/community-and-environment/drinking-water) recommends testing for bacteria and nitrates every year, but most well owners we meet haven't tested in five years or more. Some never have.

That matters here specifically. North Central Washington is orchard country, and decades of agriculture mean nitrates show up in shallower wells around Quincy, Moses Lake, and parts of the Wenatchee Valley. Rob and Tina bought five acres near Quincy in 2023 and tested the well as part of their purchase, mostly because their lender asked. The nitrate result came back at 12 mg/L, above the 10 mg/L federal limit. A reverse osmosis system at the kitchen sink fixed it for about $1,400, but they'd never have known without the test. We covered the local picture in detail in our post on nitrates in well water around Quincy and Moses Lake.

The flip side: well water in this valley is often genuinely excellent. Deep wells pulling from clean aquifers produce cold, mineral-rich water that beats anything from a tap in town. Plenty of our customers wouldn't trade it for anything. But "often excellent" isn't "guaranteed," and the only way to know which you've got is a lab test. Our guide to [well water testing in the Wenatchee area](/blog/well-water-testing-wenatchee/) walks through what to test for and what it costs.

## The Trade-offs Nobody Mentions Until You Own a Well

The cost and quality numbers tell most of the story. These are the parts they leave out.

### No power means no water

A city water system keeps pressure during an outage. Your well pump doesn't run without electricity, period. When a windstorm knocks out power in the Cashmere or Leavenworth foothills for two days, city folks still flush their toilets. Well owners are hauling buckets.

Dale up Brender Canyon outside Cashmere got tired of that after the 2024 windstorms left him dry for 31 hours, twice in one winter. He put in a transfer switch and a generator sized for his pump, about $1,900 all in, and hasn't worried since. If outages are common where you live, a generator setup like Dale's is worth pricing out before the next storm season.

### You're the water department now

On a well, every problem is your problem. Low pressure on a Sunday morning. A pressure switch clicking like a metronome. Sediment in the toilet tank. None of it is hard to deal with if you catch it early, but you have to be paying attention, because nobody else is.

The good news is that well systems give plenty of warning before they fail. Sputtering faucets, cycling pumps, pressure swings. Most pumps last 10 to 15 years, and knowing where yours sits on that curve helps you budget instead of getting blindsided. Our article on [how long well pumps last](/blog/how-long-do-well-pumps-last/) covers the warning signs worth knowing.

### Water rights and well rules in Washington

Most rural homes here sit on permit-exempt wells, which allow up to 5,000 gallons a day for domestic use without a water right. That covers a household easily. But rules vary by county and watershed, and in some basins new exempt wells face restrictions. If you're buying bare land planning to drill, check with the county and the Department of Ecology before you assume anything.

### Resale and lending quirks

Some lenders require a water quality test and a flow test before closing on a well property. A well that produces 3 gallons a minute will pass for most households, but a weak or failing well can stall a sale fast. Sellers who can hand over recent test results and service records close smoother and often at better prices.

## When City Water Wins, and When a Well Is the Better Deal

After enough service calls, you start to see the pattern. Here's our honest take.

**City water makes sense if** you want zero involvement, you're rarely home, you're on a tight monthly budget that can't absorb a surprise $3,000 repair, or you're in town anyway and connection is cheap. Predictability has real value. Not everyone wants a mechanical system under their lawn.

**A well makes sense if** you're on acreage, you irrigate a garden or a few fruit trees, you want long-term costs lower and under your control, or you simply like the independence. Watering a half acre on city rates gets painful fast. On a well, irrigation water costs you pennies of electricity.

**The choice is already made for most of us.** Outside city limits in Chelan, Douglas, and Grant counties, there's usually no municipal line to connect to. The real question isn't well vs city. It's whether you'll run your well proactively or reactively.

Proactive looks like this: test the water annually, check pressure tank air charge once a year, listen for changes in pump behavior, and budget about $300 a year toward eventual replacement. Reactive looks like Marcy's August Saturday with no water at 102 degrees.

## Buying a Home on a Well? Do These Three Things First

We get calls every month from new owners who skipped this part. Don't be one of them.

**First, get the well inspected, not just the house.** A standard home inspection barely touches the well system. You want pump age, flow rate, pressure tank condition, casing and cap condition, and the wellhead checked for surface contamination paths. We laid out the full checklist in our guide to [well inspections when buying a home](/blog/well-inspection-buying-home/).

**Second, test the water at a certified lab.** Bacteria and nitrates at minimum. In this region, we'd add arsenic, which occurs naturally in some local aquifers. Figure $150 to $350 depending on the panel. It's the cheapest insurance you'll ever buy on a property.

**Third, get the paperwork.** Ask for the well log (drilling report), any service records, and past test results. The well log tells you depth, casing, and yield at drilling. No log and no records means you're buying blind, which should show up in your offer price.

A well isn't a reason to walk away from a property. Some of the best water in Washington comes out of private wells between Leavenworth and Lake Chelan. It's just a system, and like a roof or a furnace, you want to know its age and condition before you own it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is well water safe to drink?

Usually yes, but the only way to know is testing, because private wells aren't monitored by anyone but the owner. Test annually for coliform bacteria and nitrates, and at least once for arsenic, which occurs naturally in parts of North Central Washington. A certified lab panel runs $150 to $350. If a test comes back bad, treatment systems exist for nearly every problem.

### Is well water cheaper than city water?

Over 10 to 20 years, almost always. A well costs $5 to $20 a month in pump electricity versus $50 to $90 for typical city water in our area. The trade-off is occasional large repairs, like a $1,800 to $4,500 pump replacement every 10 to 15 years. Budget around $300 a year for upkeep and the well still comes out well ahead.

### Does a private well hurt home resale value?

Not in rural North Central Washington, where wells are the norm and buyers expect them. What hurts resale is a well with no records, low flow, or a failed water test discovered during the sale. Keep service records and recent test results on hand and a good well can actually be a selling point, since buyers know they'll never pay a water bill.

### What happens to a well pump during a power outage?

It stops, and so does your water, though you'll have whatever's stored in the pressure tank, usually just a few gallons. Many rural owners here install a generator with a transfer switch sized for the pump, typically $1,500 to $3,000 installed. Never wire a generator to a pump circuit yourself without a proper transfer switch. That's a job for a licensed electrician.
