## How Each Pump Actually Works, in Plain English

The core difference is simple. A submersible pushes water up from inside the well. A jet pump sits above ground and pulls water up by suction. Pushing is easy. Pulling is hard. Almost everything else flows from that one fact.

### Submersible pumps: the pusher

A submersible is a long, skinny cylinder, usually about 4 inches around and 2 to 4 feet long, that hangs on a pipe deep inside your well casing, fully underwater. The motor is sealed inside it. When your pressure tank calls for water, the pump spins up and pushes water straight up the drop pipe to your house.

Because it pushes from below, depth barely matters. A good submersible will lift water 400 feet or more without complaint. It's also silent from the surface, it never needs priming, and the water around it keeps the motor cool. The tradeoff: when it fails, someone has to pull it out of the well, and that's not a Saturday afternoon job on a 300-foot well.

### Jet pumps: the puller

A jet pump looks like a motor bolted to a snail shell, and it sits above ground, usually in a pump house, well pit, or basement. It uses suction plus a clever venturi nozzle to draw water up out of the well.

Here's the catch, and it's physics, not marketing. Suction at our elevation can only lift water about 25 feet, period. No pump on Earth beats that number because it's set by atmospheric pressure. A shallow well jet pump is honest about this and tops out around 25 feet. A deep well jet pump cheats by sending part of its own output back down the well through a second pipe to help push, which stretches the range to roughly 90 feet, but efficiency falls off a cliff the deeper you go.

If you want a deeper dive into the mechanics, the [USGS has a solid plain-language explainer on how wells and groundwater work](https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/groundwater-wells).

## Submersible vs Jet Pump: Depth Decides Most of It

Before you compare price tags, find out your static water level, meaning how far down the water sits in your casing. Not the well depth, the water depth. Those are different numbers, and the water level is the one that matters.

Here's the cheat sheet:

- **Water within 25 feet of the surface:** either pump works. A shallow well jet pump is the budget option.
- **Water 25 to 90 feet down:** a deep well jet pump can work, but a submersible will outperform it on pressure and power use almost every time.
- **Water deeper than 90 feet:** submersible. There's no real debate.

Now the local reality. Most drilled wells around Wenatchee, East Wenatchee, and Quincy hit water somewhere between 100 and 400 feet down. So where do jet pumps still earn their keep here? If your pump is bolted to the wall of a pump house and you can hear it run, you've got a jet pump.

## Reliability, Noise, and What Winter Does to Each

On paper the lifespans look close. In practice, around here, submersibles win on reliability, and winter is a big reason why.

### The freeze factor

Our valley winters drop below zero most years, and a jet pump full of water sits above ground waiting for it. Teresa, outside Leavenworth off the Chumstick, learned that one in January. Her pump house heat lamp bulb burned out during a cold snap that bottomed at minus 4. Three days later the jet pump housing cracked, and she was hauling water for her horses until we got there. Total damage: $2,100 for a new pump, fittings, and pipe repair, plus a properly insulated pump house this time.

A submersible spends its whole life 100-plus feet down where the ground stays around 52 degrees year round. The pump itself essentially cannot freeze. Your pipes and pressure tank still can, so the pump house still matters, but you've removed the most expensive component from the danger zone. Insulate the pump house either way, because frozen pipes don't care what kind of pump you own.

### Priming, noise, and wear

Jet pumps must stay primed, meaning full of water. Lose prime from a leaky foot valve or a dropping water table and the pump runs dry, overheats, and chews itself up. Submersibles never need priming. They can still be damaged by a well running dry, but they don't lose prime from a 50-cent check valve failing.

Noise is the quality-of-life difference people don't think about until they live with it. A jet pump cycling on and off is loud enough to hear in the house if it's in the basement or near a bedroom wall. A submersible is dead silent. The only thing you'll hear is the pressure switch clicking.

One more local wrinkle: sand and grit. Some wells around Quincy and Moses Lake produce fine sand, which wears jet pump impellers and venturis fast. The fix is proper pump selection and sometimes screening, not just swapping like for like every five years.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a submersible pump better than a jet pump?

For wells with water deeper than 25 feet, yes, almost always. A submersible delivers stronger pressure, uses less electricity, runs silently, and typically lasts 12 to 25 years versus 8 to 15 for a jet pump. Jet pumps only win on up-front cost, and only on shallow wells where suction can actually reach the water.

### Can I replace my jet pump with a submersible?

Usually, if your well casing is at least 4 inches in diameter and in decent shape. The conversion involves removing the old suction piping, setting a submersible on a drop pipe, running new wiring down the well, and often updating the pressure tank and switch. Around the Wenatchee area the conversion typically runs $2,500 to $5,000 depending on depth.

### How deep can a jet pump pull water?

A shallow well jet pump maxes out around 25 feet, which is a hard physical limit set by atmospheric pressure. A deep well jet pump uses a two-pipe ejector down the well to reach roughly 90 feet, but it loses efficiency badly past 50 feet. Beyond 90 feet, a submersible is the only practical choice.

### How do I know which type of pump my well has?

Look and listen. If there's a motor you can see and hear above ground in a pump house, pit, or basement, it's a jet pump. If your well cap just has wires and a pipe disappearing down the casing and you never hear the pump run, it's a submersible. Your pressure tank looks the same either way, so the tank won't tell you anything.
