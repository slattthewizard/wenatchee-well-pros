## What a Well Pump Tripping the Breaker Is Really Telling You

Your breaker isn't broken. It's doing its job.

A circuit breaker trips when the pump circuit pulls more current than the wire is rated to carry. For most submersible well pumps around here, that's a 20 or 30 amp double-pole breaker. When the pump motor draws more amps than it should, the breaker opens the circuit before the wiring overheats inside your walls or down your well casing.

So a tripping breaker is almost never a breaker problem. It's a symptom. Something downstream, the motor, the wiring, the pressure switch, the control box, is pulling too much current or shorting out. Resetting the breaker over and over doesn't fix that. It just forces a struggling motor to keep cooking itself.

## 7 Reasons a Well Pump Keeps Tripping the Breaker

Here's what we find on real service calls across Chelan, Douglas, and Grant counties, roughly in order of how often we see it.

### 1. A failing pump motor

This is the big one. As motor bearings wear and windings degrade, the motor draws more amps to do the same work. Eventually it crosses the breaker's threshold. A pump that trips the breaker after running for 10 or 20 minutes, rather than instantly, often points to a motor that's overheating under load. Most submersibles around here last 10 to 15 years, so if yours is in that window, the motor is suspect number one. Our guide on [how long well pumps last](/blog/how-long-do-well-pumps-last/) covers the full lifespan picture.

### 2. A bad capacitor or control box

Three-wire submersible pumps use a control box mounted in the house or pump house, and inside that box is a start capacitor. When the capacitor fails, the motor tries to start without the electrical kick it needs, stalls, and pulls locked-rotor current, which can be five to six times normal draw. The breaker trips almost immediately. This is one of the cheapest fixes on the list, usually $250 to $450 including the service call.

### 3. Damaged or shorted wiring

The wire running down your well takes abuse. It rubs against the casing every time the pump starts and torques. Splices corrode. In older installations, insulation simply wears through. When a bare spot touches the casing or water, you get a dead short and an instant trip the moment the pump tries to start. Above ground, we've also pulled chewed wiring out of pump houses in Cashmere and Leavenworth, mice love a warm pump house in January.

### 4. A seized or sand-bound pump

North Central Washington wells produce sediment, especially older wells and wells in the sandier ground out toward Quincy and Moses Lake. Sand packs into the impellers, the pump end binds, and the motor stalls trying to spin it. A stalled motor pulls massive current and trips the breaker fast. Sometimes the pump frees up after sitting, which is why this one can come and go for months before it locks up for good.

### 5. A failing pressure switch

The pressure switch tells your pump when to start and stop. When the contacts pit and burn, they can arc, chatter, or weld shut. Arcing contacts create current spikes, and a switch that won't shut off makes the pump run until something gives. If you hear rapid clicking from the switch near your pressure tank, that's a strong clue. We cover this in detail in our post on [pressure switch problems](/blog/pressure-switch-problems/).

### 6. Short cycling from a waterlogged pressure tank

When a pressure tank loses its air charge, the pump kicks on every time someone opens a tap, sometimes six or eight times an hour. Every motor start is a current spike and a heat event. Enough of them back to back and the breaker trips, or worse, the motor burns up. If your pump clicks on and off constantly, read up on [well pump short cycling](/blog/well-pump-short-cycling/) because fixing the tank may fix the breaker.

Rob, who runs five acres of orchard near Quincy, hit this one last July. His pump breaker tripped every afternoon during that 100-degree stretch, always when irrigation demand peaked. The culprit was a waterlogged pressure tank making the pump start 70-plus times a day. An $850 tank replacement ended the tripping and probably saved a $4,000 pump.

### 7. The breaker itself

Breakers do wear out, especially ones that have tripped many times. A fatigued breaker can trip below its rated load. This is the one cause that isn't the pump's fault, but it's also the least common, maybe one call in fifteen. Don't assume it's the breaker just because that's the cheapest answer. And never let anyone "fix" the problem by installing a bigger breaker. That removes the protection without removing the fault, and it's how well wires start fires.

## What You Can Safely Check Yourself

There's a hard line here. Breaker panel resets and observation are fine. Anything involving pump wiring, the control box internals, or the pressure switch contacts is 240-volt work, and it can kill you. Here's what's on the safe side of the line.

**Reset once and time it.** Flip the breaker fully off, then on. If it trips instantly, you likely have a dead short in the wiring or a seized pump. If it trips after several minutes of running, think overheating motor or overload. That one detail tells us half the diagnosis before we load the truck.

**Look at your pressure gauge.** Watch the gauge by the pressure tank while water runs. If pressure swings fast and the pump clicks on and off every minute or two, you've probably got a tank or switch issue, not a dying pump.

**Listen.** Humming with no water means the motor is trying and failing to start. Rapid clicking points at the pressure switch. Note what you hear and when.

**Check for the obvious.** Walk the pump house. Look for scorch marks, melted insulation, chewed wires, water dripping on electrical parts, or a control box that smells burnt. Look, don't touch.

**Then stop.** If the breaker trips a second time, leave it off. Every additional restart on a faulted circuit risks turning a $300 repair into a $3,500 replacement, exactly what happened to Marcy. The EPA's [private well owner resources](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells) are great for water quality and maintenance basics, but electrical pump faults are firmly licensed-pro territory.

## When a Tripping Breaker Means the Pump Is Done

Sometimes the breaker is the last chapter, not the first warning. Here's when we tell homeowners to put money toward replacement instead of repair.

**The pump is past 12 to 15 years old.** A motor drawing high amps at that age is wearing out, not having a bad day. You can replace a capacitor and buy six months, but you're patching a tire that's out of tread.

**The trips come with other symptoms.** Falling pressure, sputtering faucets, sediment in the water, or a pump that runs longer and longer to fill the tank. Stack two or three of those on top of breaker trips and the motor is telling you everything. ## How to Keep the Breaker From Tripping Again

Once the fault is fixed, a little prevention keeps you off this merry-go-round.

**Check your pressure tank's air charge yearly.** A properly charged tank is the single cheapest way to extend motor life. Tank pressure should sit 2 psi below the pressure switch cut-in.

**Keep the pump house sealed and dry.** Mice, moisture, and dust cause a surprising share of the electrical calls we run, especially in older pump houses around Cashmere and Chelan.

**Don't oversize irrigation off a domestic pump.** A pump sized for a household will overload trying to feed a big sprinkler system in a 100-degree July. If you've added irrigation, get the pump sizing checked.

**Get the system looked at every couple of years.** A 30-minute checkup that catches a pitted pressure switch or a soft capacitor costs a fraction of a burned motor.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why does my well pump trip the breaker immediately?

An instant trip almost always means a dead short or a locked rotor. The usual suspects are damaged down-well wiring, a failed start capacitor, or a pump bound up with sand. Leave the breaker off after the second trip and get it diagnosed, because repeated restarts into a short can destroy the motor and damage wiring.

### Can I just install a bigger breaker so it stops tripping?

No, and don't let anyone talk you into it. The breaker is sized to protect the wire, not the pump, and oversizing it lets a faulted circuit overheat wiring inside your walls or well casing. That's a fire risk and a code violation. Fix the fault that's causing the overcurrent instead.

### Is it the breaker or the pump?

Usually the pump or its controls. Worn breakers cause maybe one in fifteen of these calls. A quick clue: if the breaker trips after the pump runs a while, suspect the motor; if it trips instantly, suspect wiring, the capacitor, or a seized pump. A technician with an amp meter can settle it in minutes.

### How much does it cost to fix a well pump that keeps tripping the breaker?

In the Wenatchee area, most fixes land between $175 and $1,500 depending on the cause. Pressure switches and capacitors sit at the cheap end, down-well wiring repairs in the middle, and full pump replacement runs $1,800 to $4,500 or more. Catching the problem after the first few trips usually keeps you at the cheap end of that range.
