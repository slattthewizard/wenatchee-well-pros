## Deepen the Well or Drill a New One: What Each Option Actually Means

Deepening means a drill rig re-enters your existing bore and continues down, extending the same hole further into the aquifer. Your current casing usually stays in place for the upper section, with new casing or open bore extending below it. It's faster than starting fresh because the top of the hole is already drilled, developed, and connected to your property.

Drilling a new well means exactly what it sounds like. A fresh bore somewhere else on your property, all new casing top to bottom, a new well seal, a new pump system, and new trenching to the house. The old well usually gets decommissioned once the new one is proven, which is its own cost and its own paperwork.

Neither option is a DIY project or even a judgment call to make alone. Both require a licensed well driller to evaluate the existing bore, and the pump and wiring work on either path is a job for a licensed well contractor, not a homeowner with a rented auger. Get a professional evaluation before you spend a dollar on either direction.

## Can Your Well Be Deepened? The Casing Test That Decides It

Whether deepening is even possible comes down to one question: is the existing casing sound enough for a rig to work through it safely?

Steel casing that's decades old can be corroded, especially in mineral-heavy groundwater common from Cashmere to Quincy. A driller will typically run a camera or caliper survey down the casing first, checking for pitting, cracks, or collapse before committing to deepen. If the casing shows significant corrosion, deepening becomes risky or impossible, and a new bore is the safer call.

Depth and formation matter too. A well that's already 300 feet deep in soft alluvial ground may not have much usable aquifer left below it before hitting bedrock, while a basalt well in Douglas County might have hundreds of feet of fractured rock still untapped. Your driller can pull nearby well logs to get a sense of what's actually down there before committing to either path.

Age plays a role as well. Wells drilled before Washington tightened casing standards decades ago sometimes used thinner steel or lacked a proper surface seal, both of which make deepening a poor bet even if the casing looks fine on camera. If your well is older than 25 to 30 years, budget for a full camera inspection before you decide anything.

## Cost Comparison: Deepening vs. Drilling a New Well

Here's how the two paths compare on price across our service area in 2026.

|---|---|---|
| Typical cost | $8,000 - $20,000 | $18,000 - $50,000+ |
| Time to complete | 1-3 days | 3-7 days plus permitting lead time |
| Casing requirement | Existing casing must pass inspection | All new casing, no prior condition risk |
| Permitting | Notice of intent, often simpler | Full notice of intent, may need siting review |
| Pump system | Often reusable if depth change is modest | Always new pump, wire, and drop pipe |

Deepening generally runs 40 to 60 percent of what a comparable new well costs, mostly because you're not paying for casing and development on the upper section a second time. That discount only holds if the casing inspection comes back clean. A failed inspection midway through a deepening attempt can leave you paying for both the failed attempt and a new well afterward.

## When You Need a New Well Instead

Sometimes the casing test settles it fast, and sometimes it's the site itself that rules out deepening.

Denise Whitfield, near Moses Lake, had a well that had been sputtering for two summers. A camera survey found her older casing had corroded through in two spots below 60 feet, with visible daylight where groundwater was seeping in around the outside of the pipe. Deepening through compromised casing like that risks a collapse mid-job, so her driller recommended a new well instead. It ran $27,800 for a 320-foot bore in decent ground, plus $3,800 to properly decommission the old well per state requirements.

A new well also becomes the only real option when a well has gone completely dry rather than just low-yield, when the aquifer at the current depth is fully tapped out with nothing deeper worth reaching, or when the existing well sits in a location that's since been compromised by a new septic system, a building addition, or a nearby contamination concern.

## Permits and Paperwork Either Way

Both deepening and drilling a brand new well require a Notice of Intent filed with the Washington Department of Ecology before work starts, and both fall under the same groundwater permit-exemption rules most single homes rely on. The [Department of Ecology's well construction program](https://ecology.wa.gov/water-shorelines/water-supply/wells) covers the filing requirements and keeps public well logs you or your driller can pull before deciding anything.

Deepening paperwork is usually lighter since the well already exists and its location is already approved. A brand new well may trigger a fresh look at septic setbacks, property line distances, and in some closed basins, additional review before permit-exempt status applies.

Either way, once work is done, get the well tested. The [Washington Department of Health](https://doh.wa.gov/community-and-environment/drinking-water) recommends a full bacteria and nitrate panel any time a well is deepened or newly drilled, since disturbing the bore can temporarily introduce contaminants that clear with a proper flush and test.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it cheaper to deepen a well or drill a new one?

Deepening typically runs 40 to 60 percent of a comparable new well, generally $8,000 to $20,000 versus $18,000 to $50,000 or more for a full new bore. The catch is that deepening only works if the existing casing passes inspection. A well with corroded or compromised casing usually isn't a candidate no matter how much cheaper it looks on paper.

### How do I know if my well can be deepened?

A licensed driller runs a camera or caliper survey down the existing casing to check for corrosion, pitting, or structural damage before recommending deepening. Wells with sound casing and known aquifer depth below the current bore are usually good candidates. Wells older than 25 to 30 years should get this inspection regardless of symptoms, since casing condition matters more than age alone.

### Do I need a new pump if I deepen my well?

Sometimes, but not always. If the deepening only adds a modest amount of depth, your existing submersible pump may still handle it once the drop pipe is extended. If the new depth changes the pump's performance needs significantly, or the pump was already near the end of its life, plan on a new pump as part of the project.

### Can a well be deepened more than once?

Yes, as long as the casing keeps passing inspection and there's usable aquifer left to reach. Some older wells in our area have been deepened twice over several decades. Each round still requires its own Notice of Intent and casing evaluation, so treat every deepening decision as a fresh one rather than assuming a well that qualified once will always qualify again.
