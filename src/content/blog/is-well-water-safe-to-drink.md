Your well water can look clear, taste fine, and still be dangerous. That's not a scare line. It's just how bacteria and nitrate behave: neither one changes how water looks, smells, or tastes at the levels that matter.

So is well water safe to drink? Usually, yes. But the honest answer is that only a lab can tell you, and on a private well, nobody else is checking for you.

There's no utility running monthly samples on your line out here. The Safe Drinking Water Act doesn't cover private wells at all, and the [EPA is direct about what that means](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells): if you own the well, you're the water department, responsible for your own testing and your own results.

This page is the overview, not a deep dive into any single contaminant. It covers what to test for, how often, what the numbers mean, and where to read more on each risk that matters in Chelan, Douglas and Grant counties. If you're here because of a cracked cap or a wellhead that sat underwater this spring, keep reading. That part is a well repair, not a lab question, and it's the part we do.

## Why Clear, Good-Tasting Water Doesn't Mean Safe Water

Taste and clarity tell you about sediment, iron, or a swampy smell. They tell you almost nothing about coliform bacteria and nitrate, the two the state health department singles out because they can affect someone's health quickly. Both are invisible and tasteless, and a glass that looks perfect can carry either one at a level that matters.

That's the entire reason testing exists as a yearly habit and not a one-time chore. You can't eyeball your way to an answer, and neither can we, standing in your pump house with a flashlight. A lab is the only source that counts, and it needs an actual sample, not a guess.

## Is Your Well Water Safe? The Annual Test List for This Area

Two tests carry most of the weight, and they're the two you can't taste. Total coliform bacteria tells you whether something got into the well that shouldn't be there, often surface water or something fecal. Nitrate tells you about fertilizer, manure, or a septic system reaching your groundwater.

The [Washington State Department of Health recommends testing every year for coliform bacteria and nitrate](https://doh.wa.gov/community-and-environment/drinking-water/contaminants/testing-your-water). If your nitrate result comes back at 5 mg/L or higher, retest within six months instead of waiting for next year's sample, since nitrate can climb between seasons. EPA guidance for private wells adds total dissolved solids and pH to that yearly list, and recommends testing more often if there's an infant, an elderly person, or someone pregnant or nursing in the house.

Here's the yardstick those results get measured against. Public systems have to meet EPA limits, and they're the closest thing a private well owner has to a pass or fail line: nitrate at 10 mg/L, arsenic at 0.010 mg/L, which is 10 parts per billion, and for bacteria, any E. coli at all counts as a fail. There's no safe trace amount of E. coli in drinking water.

## Local Risks That Affect Well Water Safety in Chelan, Douglas and Grant Counties

The annual list above is the baseline everywhere. What you're actually watching for beyond it depends on where your well sits.

Nitrate tends to run higher near orchards, farmed fields, and septic systems, because fertilizer and effluent both move through soil into groundwater. If your well sits downhill from a drain field or close to an orchard block, know that before you see a number. Check your setback against [Washington's septic and well separation rules](/blog/septic-well-separation-distance-washington/) if you're not sure yours is far enough away.

Arsenic occurs naturally in the rock under parts of Washington, not from anything anyone did wrong. It has no taste or smell, so it's worth a baseline test even though it isn't on the yearly list. You'd never notice it on your own.

Lead is almost never in the groundwater itself. It usually comes from old plumbing, solder, or fixtures inside the house, which means a sample from the well and a sample from the kitchen tap can tell different stories.

PFAS, sometimes called forever chemicals, tend to show up near airports, fire training areas, and some industrial sites. It's not part of the routine annual test, but if your well sits anywhere near one of those, ask the lab about it by name. Read [PFAS in well water](/blog/pfas-well-water-washington/) for the local picture.

Radon comes from bedrock, granite especially, and it matters more in the air of a closed-up house than in the water. It's not on the standard annual list either, but it's worth a look if your well is drilled into granite. [Radon in well water](/blog/radon-in-well-water/) covers when testing for it actually makes sense.

Coliform bacteria and E. coli are tied most directly to getting sick, and they move the fastest of the bunch. A well that tested clean last spring can carry bacteria this spring after a wet season, a flood, or a wellhead problem that wasn't there before.

## Nuisance vs Health: What Iron, Manganese and Hardness Actually Mean

Not everything that bothers you about your water is a health issue, so know the difference before you panic over a rust stain.

Iron, odor and total dissolved solids sit on what the EPA calls [secondary standards](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/secondary-drinking-water-standards-guidance-nuisance-chemicals): guidance about taste, color and staining rather than health limits. Hardness isn't on that list at all. It's a scale problem. Rust-colored water, crust on your fixtures, or a sulfur smell from the hot tap cost you in stained laundry and worn-out appliances, and they're worth fixing. On their own, they're not a reason to stop drinking the water.

Manganese is the one exception worth knowing. It's on the secondary list for its black stains, but at higher levels it's a health concern as well, which our [manganese article](/blog/manganese-in-well-water/) covers.

The distinction matters because it's easy to fixate on the water that's visibly wrong and skip the test for what shows no sign at all. Fix the nuisance because it's annoying and expensive over time. Test for coliform and nitrate because those decide whether the water is safe.

## What a Positive Bacteria Result Means and What to Do Next

If a test comes back positive for coliform bacteria or E. coli, stop drinking the water or switch to boiling it first. Bring it to a full rolling boil for at least a minute and let it cool before you drink it. Boiling kills bacteria. It does the opposite for nitrate, since boiling concentrates nitrate as water evaporates, so if nitrate is the problem, boiling makes it worse rather than better.

Once you're past the immediate risk, the next step is fixing the well itself, not just the water sitting in it. [Shock chlorinating the well](/blog/well-chlorination-shock/) is the standard way to knock down bacterial contamination, and plenty of handy owners do it themselves with the right process and some patience. Then find and fix whatever let the contamination in. Chlorinating without fixing the entry point just buys a few clean weeks before the same problem comes back.

Retest once the chlorine has flushed out, and don't trust a single clean result right after a known contamination. It can take more than one round to clear a well, so a second clean test a few weeks later is what closes it out. ## Where Contamination Usually Gets Into a Well

Bacteria and surface contamination usually get in at the top of the well, not from somewhere deep underground. That's good news, because the top of the well is the part within reach, and it's the part in our trade.

A cracked, loose, or poorly fitted well cap is a classic entry point. It can look fine from a few feet away and still let insects, rodents and surface runoff straight down the casing. A [well cap replacement](/blog/well-cap-replacement/) is a small job.

Casing damage is less obvious. A crack or a bad seam below grade lets groundwater and surface water mix in a way that shows up in a lab report long before it shows up as anything you can see at the wellhead. Flooding is the seasonal version of the same problem. ## When to Test Outside the Annual Schedule

The annual coliform and nitrate test is the baseline, but a handful of events should push you to test sooner than the calendar says.

Test right away if the taste, smell, or color of your water changes for no obvious reason. Test after any flooding near the wellhead, even once the water looks fine again. Test after any well or pump work, since disturbing the system can stir up sediment or introduce something that wasn't there before. Test if there's a new baby, a new pregnancy, or anyone with a weakened immune system moving into the house.

Test before you buy or sell a home on a private well, too. A [well inspection before buying a home](/blog/well-inspection-buying-home/) covers what that process actually involves, and why a stale test the seller hands you isn't the same as one you ordered yourself. None of these events need to wait for a scheduled visit. A same-day sample beats a guess every time.

## How to Get Your Well Water Tested the Right Way

Use a state-accredited lab, not a kit from a hardware store, for anything you plan to act on. A hardware store kit is fine for a rough read on hardness or chlorine. It's not built for a coliform or nitrate result you'd trust with a real decision. Your county health district is a good first call for a list of accredited labs. In Chelan and Douglas counties that's the [Chelan-Douglas Health District](https://cdhd.wa.gov/), and Grant County has its own. The lab will give you a sterile bottle and instructions.

Follow the lab's sampling instructions to the letter. A contaminated sample bottle, a sample pulled from an outside hose bib instead of an indoor tap, or a sample that sat in a hot car for two days can throw off a result in either direction. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is well water safe to drink if it looks and tastes fine?

Usually, but looks and taste aren't a test. Coliform bacteria and nitrate, the two contaminants most likely to cause a real health problem, have no taste, smell, or color at the levels that matter. A lab test is the only real way to know, not a glass held up to the light.

### How do I know if my well water is safe without paying for a full lab panel?

You can't fully know without testing, but you can prioritize. Test for coliform bacteria and nitrate every year at minimum, since those are the two tied most directly to health and the ones state and federal guidance both recommend annually. Add arsenic, lead, or PFAS based on your specific location and plumbing age rather than testing for everything every time.

### What does E. coli in well water actually mean for my household?

It means stop drinking the water until it's fixed. E. coli points to fecal contamination, most often from surface water, a septic issue, or an animal getting into the wellhead. Boil water for drinking and cooking in the meantime, then shock chlorinate the well and find the entry point before you trust it again.

### What should well water be tested for every year in this area?

At minimum, total coliform bacteria and nitrate, which is what Washington's Department of Health recommends annually for private wells. Federal guidance for private wells adds total dissolved solids and pH to that yearly list. Beyond that, arsenic, lead, and PFAS are worth testing for based on your well's location and your home's plumbing rather than on a fixed yearly schedule.
