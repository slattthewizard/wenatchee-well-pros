Every forum thread on how to replace a well pump ends the same way: rent a pump puller, thread some pipe, done by lunch. On a shallow well with poly pipe, that's roughly true. On a galvanized string, a 300-foot set, or wiring older than the person asking, "done by lunch" turns into a long afternoon and sometimes a pump at the bottom of the well.

This is the honest version. What pulling and resetting a submersible pump actually takes, where a handy owner can run the whole job, and where the smart move is picking up the phone before you rent anything. It isn't "never do this yourself," and it isn't "anyone can." Your depth and your pipe decide which one you are.

## Is This a Well Pump Replacement or a Ten-Minute Fix?

Before you touch the well cap, confirm the pump is actually the problem. A dead pump and a stuck pressure switch look identical from the kitchen tap, but a switch runs $150 - $350 to replace and a submersible pump runs $1,800 - $4,500, so it's worth ten minutes with a meter before you decide which one you've got.

Two checks settle it. First, watch the pressure gauge while someone flips the breaker. If the needle never moves and you hear nothing, the circuit is open somewhere between the panel and the motor. That could be the switch, a splice, or the motor windings, and you can't tell which from the tap. Our guide on [testing a well pump without pulling it](/blog/test-well-pump-without-pulling-it/) takes you through the checks in order.

Second, an amp draw test. Zero amps with voltage present means an open circuit: a broken wire or a burned-open motor. Very high amps that trip the breaker point at a locked or shorted motor. Normal amps with no water usually means a split pipe or a broken coupling, and the pump is spinning for nothing. This test puts a clamp meter on a live 240-volt circuit, so if you haven't done that before, it's the point to hand it off. The readings are explained in [well pump amp draw testing](/blog/well-pump-amp-draw-testing/).

If both checks say the motor itself is gone, you're looking at a real replacement. If yours is a deep galvanized set and none of this sounds like a weekend you want to spend, call [(509) 300-5151](tel:+15093005151) for a free estimate before you rent a single tool.

## Know Your Well Before You Touch the Cap

Three facts change everything about this job, and you need all three before you start: depth, pipe material, and wire configuration.

Depth tells you the weight you're about to lift and whether this is a two-person job or a rig job. Well records, an old invoice, or a call to the county can usually get you a number; our piece on [how deep wells run around Wenatchee](/blog/how-deep-are-wells-wenatchee/) explains where to look.

Pipe material matters just as much. Poly drop pipe flexes, coils on the ground as it comes up, and weighs a fraction of steel. Galvanized steel pipe doesn't flex at all, threads together in rigid sections, and gets heavy fast. [Drop pipe, galvanized versus poly](/blog/drop-pipe-galvanized-vs-poly/) breaks down which one you're likely to find and why it matters here.

Wire configuration is the third piece. A two-wire pump starts itself off the pressure switch. A three-wire pump needs a control box with a start and run capacitor, which is one more thing to buy and match correctly. Know which one you have before you order a replacement, not after.

## Pull It Yourself or Call a Rig?

Depth and pipe type together answer the question most people ask first: can I actually do this. Here's how we'd size it up.

| Pipe Type | Depth | Yourself or Call a Rig | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Poly, under 150 ft | Shallow | Yourself, two people | Light, flexible, coils easily as it comes up |
| Poly, 150 to 300 ft | Moderate | Doable with two or three people and a pipe puller, budget a full day | Manageable weight, but a kink or a dropped clamp costs you the whole string |
| Galvanized steel, under 150 ft | Shallow | Borderline, two strong people minimum | Steel doesn't flex, every foot is dead weight, and a cross-threaded joint ruins the pipe |
| Galvanized steel over 150 ft, or any set past 300 ft | Deep | Call a rig | Weight exceeds what two people can control safely over an open hole |

The physics behind that last row is worth seeing in numbers. Schedule 40 galvanized 1-1/4 inch pipe runs roughly 2.3 lb per foot empty, and once it's full of water that climbs to close to 3 lb per foot. Three hundred feet of it is on the order of 800 to 900 lb before you've added the pump or the wire. That's not a job for a rope over a rafter.

## Shutting Down Power and Opening the Wellhead

If you've decided to pull it yourself, the order matters. Skip a step here and you either get shocked or drop something down the hole.

Kill the pump breaker at the main panel, then find any second disconnect near the pump house or wellhead. A lot of rural setups have one, and both need to be off. Put tape over the breaker so nobody flips it back on while you're over the hole. Then open an outside tap and let it run until the flow stops. That relieves the pressure, and if the pump doesn't kick on as the gauge drops to zero, you've confirmed the power really is off.

Remove the well cap. Underneath it you'll find the pitless adapter, the fitting that lets the drop pipe pass through the casing wall without the well needing to be dug up every time. Thread a pipe puller's T-handle into the top of the adapter and lift straight up until it clears its seat in the casing wall.

Expect that first lift to be the hardest of the day. The O-rings on the adapter have been sitting in the casing for years and they don't want to let go. If you want to know what you're looking at down there, see [how a pitless adapter works](/blog/pitless-adapter-explained/). Once it's off the seat, the whole string (pipe, wire and pump) is hanging on your T-handle.

## Pulling the String Without Losing It Down the Hole

This is where the job either goes smoothly or goes very wrong. The worst thing that can happen on a DIY pull is dropping the pump or the pipe back down the well. It happens when a clamp slips, a pipe joint lets go under load, or two people lose their grip at the same moment.

Getting it back out means a fishing job with specialized tools. It's expensive, nobody can tell you in advance how long it'll take, and in the worst case the well is lost. That risk is the real reason owners hire this part out even when they could muscle through it.

If you're pulling poly, seat a pipe clamp across a board or a pair of sawhorses so it always has something solid under it before anyone lets go. Pull a length you can control, clamp, coil it on the ground, then pull again. Never let the full weight hang on hands alone between clamps. Steel pipe comes up in threaded lengths, one joint at a time, and every joint needs a clamp holding the string below it while you unscrew the section above.

Here's why it's heavier than people expect. The check valve at the pump holds the whole water column in the pipe as you lift, so you're pulling pipe full of water. A 1-1/4 inch pipe holds about 0.064 gallons per foot, and at 8.34 lb a gallon that's roughly half a pound of water per foot. On 200 feet of poly, that's around 100 lb of water before you count the pipe, the wire and the pump. On galvanized, it's the 800 to 900 lb figure above. Know your number before you decide how many hands you need.

## What to Check as the Old Pump Comes Up

As the string comes up, slow down and actually look at what you're pulling. This is the only time you'll see any of it until the next failure.

Check every splice for corrosion or a cracked jacket. A pinhole in the wire insulation down the well is a slow, miserable failure to diagnose, and this is your one chance to catch it. Look along the wire for a shiny worn stripe where it's been rubbing the casing. Check the pipe threads or the poly fittings for wear and cracks.

Then look at the check valve, usually threaded into the pump discharge or set a short way up the pipe. If it's letting water fall back, that alone can explain a pump that short cycles or a gauge that bleeds down overnight.

## Matching the Replacement Pump and Wiring It Right

Buying the wrong pump is its own way to waste a day. Match horsepower and voltage to the old pump's nameplate, and match two-wire or three-wire to what's already there. A three-wire pump won't run without its control box, and a two-wire pump won't run with one. The differences are laid out in [two-wire versus three-wire well pumps](/blog/two-wire-vs-three-wire-well-pump/).

Wire size matters as much as pump size. Undersized wire on a long run drops voltage at the motor and shortens its life even if everything else is right, and the gauge-by-depth tables are in [well pump wire size and voltage drop](/blog/well-pump-wire-size-voltage-drop/). If the old pump never kept up with the house, don't just buy the same one again. Read [how to size a well pump](/blog/well-pump-sizing/) first.

Before it goes back down, put in a new check valve. It's cheap, and you won't be down here again for years ([which check valve fits which setup](/blog/well-pump-check-valve-types/)). Splice the new leads with a heat-shrink kit rated for submersion, never electrical tape. Fit a torque arrestor a short way above the pump. It's a rubber collar that wedges against the casing, because a submersible jerks sideways every time it starts, and without one the pump knocks the casing and the wire chafes. Then tie on a safety rope rated for the full weight of the string, so if a joint ever lets go, the pump hangs on the rope instead of dropping.

If that's more assembly than you expected, fair enough. The same job from our side, start to finish, is in [well pump installation day, what to expect](/blog/well-pump-installation-day-what-to-expect/). And if it turns out a switch or a capacitor failed rather than the pump, you don't need a pull at all. Start with [DIY well pump repair](/blog/diy-well-pump-repair/).

## Lowering It Back In and the First Start

Lower the new string with the same clamp-and-release rhythm you used to pull it, taping the wire to the pipe every 10 feet or so so it can't loop or get pinched. Seat the pitless adapter back into the casing wall with a fresh O-ring. It costs a few dollars, and a worn one is a slow leak you won't notice until the pressure won't hold. Put the well cap back on snug, with its vent screen intact, because a loose cap is an open door for insects and surface water.

On first start, watch the amp draw if you have a meter and compare it to the nameplate. A reading well outside spec means stop now, before the motor has run long enough to hurt itself. Then watch a full pressure cycle from cut-in to cut-out. The gauge should climb steadily, shut off at the right number and hold there.

Once it's running right, disinfect the well. The casing has been open and everything that went down the hole came off your lawn, so bacteria is a real possibility. A shock chlorination treats the well and the plumbing in one go, and the steps and the wait time are in [how to shock chlorinate a well](/blog/well-chlorination-shock/). If wiring at the panel was part of the job, check with [Washington's L&I](https://lni.wa.gov/licensing-permits/electrical/) before you call it done. Homeowners doing their own electrical work normally need a permit.

For budgeting, submersible replacement runs $1,800 - $4,500, jet pump replacement $800 - $2,000, and wire or drop pipe repairs alone $300 - $1,200. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I know for sure the pump itself is dead and not something else?

Don't pull anything until you've ruled out the cheap parts. A pressure switch, a capacitor, or a tripped breaker look exactly like a dead pump from the kitchen tap, and each costs a fraction of a full replacement to fix. Test the amp draw and watch a pressure cycle first. If the motor pulls no current at all with power confirmed at the panel, you're looking at a real pump replacement.

### Can I replace a well pump myself if I've never done it before?

On a shallow poly well with a couple of extra hands, plenty of owners do it once and never again. On a deep galvanized set, this isn't a first-timer project, since the pipe alone can weigh more than you can safely hold onto over an open hole. Be honest about your depth, your pipe type, and your confidence lifting several hundred pounds before you decide.

### What happens if I drop the pump or pipe down the well?

It happens even to people who've done this before, usually from a slipping clamp or a joint that lets go under load. Getting it back out means a fishing job with specialized tools, and nobody can promise how long that takes or whether it works. That risk alone is why a lot of owners hire out anything past a shallow, light poly set.

### Do I need a permit to replace a well pump myself?

The pump and pipe work itself generally isn't the issue, the wiring is. In Washington, electrical work falls under L&I, and homeowners doing their own wiring normally need an electrical permit. Check with L&I before you connect anything at the panel or splice new wire down the well.
