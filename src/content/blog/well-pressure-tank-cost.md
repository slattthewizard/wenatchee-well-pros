Pay attention to that drawdown column. A "44 gallon" tank doesn't hold 44 gallons of water you can use. Roughly a third of the tank is water, and only part of that drains out before the pump kicks back on. Lots of folks buy a tank based on the big number on the label and end up undersized.

## What Drives Pressure Tank Replacement Cost Up or Down

Two neighbors in East Wenatchee can get quotes $400 apart for the same size tank and both quotes can be fair. Here's why.

### Tank size and type

Size is the biggest lever, as the table shows. Type matters too. Almost everything installed today is a bladder or diaphragm tank, where a butyl membrane separates air from water. Old galvanized tanks with no bladder still exist in plenty of orchard country pump houses, but nobody should be installing new ones. They waterlog constantly and they're not worth the savings.

Brand moves the price 10 to 25 percent. Well-X-Trol and Flexcon cost more than the box store house brands, and in our experience they earn it. A quality tank carries a 5 to 7 year warranty and often runs 15 years. A bargain tank might give you 5.

### What else needs replacing

A pressure tank rarely fails alone. When we swap a tank, we almost always replace the pressure switch, gauge, and relief valve at the same time, usually $50 to $150 in parts. They're cheap, they're already disconnected, and a 12 year old switch on a brand new tank is asking for a callback. If the switch is your actual problem and the tank is fine, that's a much cheaper visit. Our guide on [pressure switch problems](/blog/pressure-switch-problems/) walks through how to tell the difference.

Corroded tank tees, seized shutoff valves, and galvanized fittings that crumble when you put a wrench on them add parts and time. So does bringing an old setup up to code with a proper relief valve.

### Access and location

A tank in a dry, lit pump house next to the driveway is a fast job. A tank wedged in a crawlspace under a Cashmere farmhouse, or in a well pit you have to climb down into, takes twice the labor. Winter work costs more too. Thawing out a pump house at 10 degrees in January so you can even start the swap adds real hours.

### Emergency timing

A scheduled weekday replacement costs less than a Saturday night no-water call. Emergency rates typically add $150 to $400. That's the strongest argument for replacing a tank that's showing symptoms instead of waiting for the funeral.

## How to Tell Your Tank Is Failing (Before It Takes the Pump With It)

Here's the part most cost guides skip. A bad pressure tank doesn't just inconvenience you. It kills pumps.

When the bladder fails, the tank waterlogs and stops doing its job, which is giving the pump a rest between cycles. The pump starts kicking on every few seconds, a problem called short cycling. Pumps are built for thousands of starts, not millions. Short cycling burns through that budget fast. We wrote a full breakdown of [what short cycling does to a well pump](/blog/well-pump-short-cycling/) if you want the mechanics.

Rick in Quincy learned this the expensive way last summer. His pressure gauge had been bouncing for about a month, and the pump was clicking on and off every time the irrigation ran. He figured he'd deal with it after harvest. By late August the 1 horsepower submersible had cooked itself, and instead of a $750 tank swap he was looking at $2,900 for a new pump and tank together. The tank was a $400 part. The delay turned it into a $2,900 problem.

Watch for these signs:

- Pump clicks on and off rapidly when water is running
- Pressure surges and drops at the faucet
- The top half of the tank feels heavy and sounds dull when you knock on it (a healthy tank sounds hollow up top)
- Water spits from the air valve on top of the tank when you press the pin
- Rust streaks, weeping seams, or a wet floor under the tank

That knock test takes ten seconds and tells you a lot. If the whole tank rings dull, it's waterlogged. Our post on [waterlogged pressure tanks](/blog/pressure-tank-waterlogged/) covers what to check next, including how to test the air charge with a tire gauge. If the bladder's intact and the tank just lost air, recharging it is sometimes a $0 fix you can do yourself.

## What Size Pressure Tank Do You Need?

Bigger tanks cost more up front but they're usually the better buy. Here's the simple math.

The standard rule: your tank's drawdown should give the pump at least one minute of runtime per cycle, and ideally closer to two. Take your pump's flow rate in gallons per minute and match it to drawdown. A 10 GPM pump wants at least 10 gallons of drawdown, which means a 44 gallon tank at minimum.

Rough guide for our area:

- **Cabin or small 2 bedroom home:** 32 to 44 gallon tank
- **Typical 3 to 4 bedroom family home:** 44 to 62 gallon
- **Larger home, big family, or some irrigation:** 62 to 86 gallon
- **Home plus serious irrigation or livestock:** 86 to 119 gallon, sometimes two tanks

Around here, irrigation is the wildcard. A lot of properties from Chelan down to Quincy run sprinklers or drip lines off the domestic well all summer. Hot, dry July weather means that pump might run for hours a day, and an undersized tank multiplies the cycle count brutally. When in doubt, go one size up. The price jump from a 44 to a 62 gallon tank is usually only $150 to $300 installed, and it can add years to your pump's life. That's cheap insurance when [pump replacement runs $1,800 to $4,500](/blog/well-pump-replacement-cost/).

## Repair vs. Replace, and What's Safe to DIY

Honest answer: pressure tanks are rarely worth repairing. Once the bladder tears, there's no fixing it. The tank is a sealed unit, and replacement is the only real option.

What is worth checking before you spend a dime:

1. **The air charge.** With the pump breaker off and a faucet open to drain pressure, check the valve on top of the tank with a tire gauge. It should read 2 PSI below your pump's cut-in pressure (so 28 PSI on a 30/50 system). If it's low and no water comes out of the valve, add air with a compressor and watch it for a week. That's a free fix.
2. **The pressure switch.** A $25 switch causes symptoms that mimic a dying tank. Burned contacts, clogged sensing tube, erratic cut-in and cut-out.
3. **The gauge.** They stick. A $10 gauge has fooled more than one homeowner into pricing a new tank.

Swapping the tank itself is a moderate DIY job for someone comfortable with plumbing: shut off the pump breaker, drain down, cut in the new tank with a union, set the air charge, done. Where we tell people to stop is anything electrical past flipping the breaker. Pressure switches sit on 240 volt circuits, and wiring mistakes there hurt people and burn up pumps. If your situation involves switch wiring, a control box, or anything at the wellhead, bring in a pro. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does it cost to replace a well pressure tank?

Most homeowners pay $600 to $1,200 installed in 2026. Small 20 to 32 gallon tanks can run as little as $350 to $650, while large 86 to 119 gallon tanks run $900 to $2,200. The price includes the tank, a new tank tee package with pressure switch and gauge, and 2 to 3 hours of labor.

### How long does a well pressure tank last?

A quality bladder tank typically lasts 10 to 15 years, and we've seen well-maintained ones go 20. Cheap tanks and tanks on systems with iron-heavy or sediment-heavy water often fail in 5 to 8 years. Checking the air charge once a year is the single best thing you can do to stretch its life.

### Can I replace a pressure tank myself?

The plumbing side is a reasonable DIY job if you're handy: shut off the breaker, drain the system, swap the tank, set the air charge. Stop at anything electrical beyond flipping the breaker, since pressure switches carry 240 volts. Factor in that a mis-set air charge or undersized tank can shorten your pump's life by years.

### Is a bigger pressure tank worth the extra money?

Usually, yes. Going up one size typically adds only $150 to $300 to the installed cost, and the larger drawdown means your pump starts far less often. Fewer starts means a longer pump life, steadier pressure, and fewer no-water surprises. It's some of the cheapest protection you can buy for a $2,000+ pump.
