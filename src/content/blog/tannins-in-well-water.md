## What Are Tannins, and Why Does Your Water Look Like Tea?

Tannins are organic acids that leach out of decaying plant material. Think fallen leaves, pine needles, bark, peat, and rotting wood sitting in soil above or near your aquifer. As rainwater and snowmelt pass through that organic layer on their way down to your well, they pick up tannic and humic acids the same way water picks up color and flavor from tea leaves in a pot.

The result is water that ranges from a faint straw color to a dark amber-brown, sometimes with a slightly earthy or musty taste. It's most common in shallow wells, wells near wetlands or seasonal ponds, and properties with heavy leaf litter, mulch, or compost close to the wellhead. Wells drilled into deep, mineral basalt aquifers rarely see tannins at all, since there's little organic matter down that far.

## Tannins vs. Iron: Telling the Difference

This mix-up costs homeowners real money, because tannin treatment and iron treatment are two different systems. Guess wrong and you'll pay twice.

Denise in Chelan spent $600 on an iron filter cartridge setup after her water turned brownish-yellow near her property, close to a low wetland area. The filter did almost nothing. A lab test later showed her iron level was normal, well under 0.1 ppm, but her tannin count was high. She'd bought the wrong system entirely. We swapped in an anion-exchange resin unit built for tannins, and the discoloration cleared within a day of the first backwash cycle.

Here's the quick way to tell them apart before you pay for a lab test:

- **Color.** Iron tends toward orange, rust, or reddish-brown. Tannins run more yellow, gold, or tea-brown.
- **Staining pattern.** Iron leaves hard orange rings on fixtures that resist scrubbing. Tannins leave a lighter yellowish tint on porcelain that's easier to wipe away.
- **Behavior over time.** Clear water that turns orange after sitting is classic ferrous iron. Water that's tea-colored right out of the tap, consistently, points to tannins.
- **Taste.** Iron tastes metallic. Tannins taste faintly earthy, musty, or like weak tea.

Both problems can occur in the same well, which is exactly why a full lab panel beats guesswork every time. We break down iron specifically, including its own set of treatment costs, in our guide to [iron in well water](/blog/iron-in-well-water/).

## Are Tannins in Well Water Safe to Drink?

For most households, yes. Tannins are naturally occurring organic compounds, not the kind of contaminant that triggers a health advisory. The EPA doesn't list tannins among its primary health-based drinking water standards, and color itself falls under the EPA's secondary standards, which cover aesthetics like taste, odor, and appearance rather than safety. You can read more on the [EPA's secondary drinking water standards page](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/secondary-drinking-water-standards-guidance-nuisance-chemicals).

That said, tannin-colored water deserves a closer look for two reasons. First, tannins often travel with low pH, since the same organic acids that stain your water can make it more acidic. Acidic water corrodes copper pipes, brass fittings, and pump components over years, which is a real cost even if the water itself won't make you sick. Second, and more importantly, tannins are a marker of shallow groundwater or surface water influence. Where organic matter gets in, bacteria and other contaminants can follow.

That's why we never recommend treating the color and calling it done. A full panel that includes coliform bacteria, pH, iron, and manganese alongside the tannin count gives you the whole picture. The Washington Department of Health has solid guidance for private well owners on what a complete test should cover, available through their [drinking water program](https://doh.wa.gov/community-and-environment/drinking-water). We also covered local testing details, including which labs serve this area, in our guide to [well water testing in Wenatchee](/blog/well-water-testing-wenatchee/).

## When Discolored Well Water Means a Bigger Problem

Most tannin problems are simply geology and shallow groundwater doing what shallow groundwater does. But sometimes a change in water color points to a hardware issue at the well itself, and treating the water without fixing the source just means you're filtering the same problem forever.

Watch for these warning signs:

- **Sudden color change after years of clear water.** This can mean a cracked or corroded well cap or casing is letting surface water and debris seep in directly. A failing seal at the top of the well is a common culprit, especially after heavy rain or snowmelt.
- **Color that gets worse during or right after irrigation season.** That points to surface water intrusion pulling organic matter into a shallow aquifer faster than usual.
- **Discoloration plus grit or sediment.** A damaged casing or screen may be letting more than just tannins into your water supply.
- **Reduced pressure alongside the color change.** Organic sediment can build up in pump intakes and pressure tanks over time, the same way iron and mineral scale do.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What causes tannins in well water?

Tannins come from decaying organic matter, like leaves, pine needles, bark, and peat, sitting in soil above or near your aquifer. As rain and snowmelt filter through that organic layer, they pick up tannic and humic acids the same way water picks up color from tea leaves. Shallow wells near wooded areas, wetlands, or heavily mulched ground are the most common spots to see it.

### How can I tell if my water has tannins or iron?

Look at the color and how it behaves. Iron usually runs orange or rust-colored and often starts clear before turning color after sitting in air. Tannins tend to run yellow, gold, or tea-brown right out of the tap, with an earthy or musty taste instead of a metallic one. A lab test is the only way to know for certain, especially since both can occur in the same well.

### Do I need to remove tannins from my well water?

Not for health reasons in most cases, since tannins aren't classified as a health hazard by the EPA. Many people still choose to treat them because of the taste, staining, and the acidity that often comes along with tannin-heavy water, which can corrode pipes and fixtures over time. It's also worth testing for bacteria, since tannins can signal shallow groundwater that's more exposed to surface contamination.

### How much does it cost to fix tannins in well water?

Moderate to heavy tannins typically need a dedicated anion-exchange system, which runs $1,800 to $3,000 installed, sometimes more if hardness or acidity needs correcting at the same time.
