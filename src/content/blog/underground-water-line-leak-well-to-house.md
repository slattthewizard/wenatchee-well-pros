The house is quiet. Nobody is showering, the irrigation is off, the washing machine finished an hour ago. And every twenty minutes or so, you hear the pump kick on for a few seconds and shut back down.

That pattern is one of the most reliable signs in the well business. Water is leaving your system somewhere, and if every fixture inside the house is dry and tight, the most likely place it is going is into the ground, out of the buried line running between your wellhead and your house.

Homeowners around Wenatchee tend to assume this is a pump problem, because the pump is the part making noise. It usually is not. The pump is doing exactly what it was built to do, which is replace pressure that keeps disappearing. Chase the pump and you will spend money without fixing anything. This guide walks through how to tell a service line leak apart from the half dozen other things that cause the same symptom, how the line gets located without digging up your whole yard, and what the repair actually runs.

## Signs the Leak Is in the Line, Not the Pump or the House

A leaking service line has a signature. Once you know it, it is hard to miss.

The pump cycles with no water running. This is the headline symptom. Close every tap, shut off the irrigation zones, and listen. If the pump starts on its own within an hour, water is going somewhere.

Pressure drops overnight. Note the gauge reading before bed with everything off, then check it first thing in the morning. On a tight system it should read almost exactly the same. A drop of ten or twenty psi overnight means a leak, and the bigger the drop, the bigger the hole.

Your power bill climbs without an explanation. A pump running a few extra hours a day adds up. If you want to put a number on it, our breakdown of [what a well pump actually costs to run](/blog/well-pump-electricity-cost/) will get you close.

There is a green stripe, a soft spot, or a patch of yard that never dries. Buried leaks often surface as vegetation that stays greener and grows faster than everything around it, sometimes in a line you can follow with your eyes. In August in the Wenatchee valley, a strip of yard that stays lush without irrigation is a strong tell.

Pressure is fine at the wellhead but weak at the house. If you have a hose bib at or near the well and another at the house, and one has noticeably better pressure than the other, the loss is happening in between.

What does not point to a service line leak is dirty or sandy water, air spitting from the taps, or a pump that runs constantly without ever shutting off. Those have their own causes, covered in [sand in well water](/blog/sand-in-well-water/), [air in the water lines](/blog/air-in-water-lines/), and [a pump that runs constantly](/blog/well-pump-runs-constantly/).

## The Fifteen Minute Test That Tells You Where the Water Is Going

Before anybody digs anything, the leak needs to be isolated to a section of the system. The test is simple and you can do most of it yourself.

Find the main shutoff where the water line enters the house. On most properties around here it is in a crawlspace, a basement wall, or a pump house, usually just past the pressure tank or just before it, depending on how the system was plumbed.

Start by shutting off everything that uses water, including ice makers, humidifiers, and irrigation timers. Let the pump build to its cutoff pressure and shut down. Write down the gauge reading.

Now close the main shutoff valve at the house. That divides your system into two halves, the buried line and wellhead on one side, the house plumbing on the other. Wait an hour without touching anything.

If the pump kicks on while the house is valved off, the leak is on the well side, which means the buried line, the pitless adapter, a check valve, or the wellhead itself. If the pump stays quiet and pressure holds, your buried line is fine and the leak is inside the house, which usually means a toilet flapper, a water heater relief valve dripping into a drain, or a fixture you forgot about.

That single test saves more money than anything else in this article, because it stops you from excavating a line that was never the problem. If the well side is the culprit, the next question is whether the loss is in the buried run or right at the well. A well contractor pressure tests the line at the pitless adapter to split those apart. The [pitless adapter](/blog/pitless-adapter-explained/) is a frequent enough failure point on older installations that it is always worth ruling out before anyone digs a trench.

## Why Buried Lines Fail Around Here

Service lines in North Central Washington fail for a handful of reasons, and most of them trace back to what is in the ground.

Rocky backfill is the big one. Much of the valley sits on glacial outwash, which means cobble and sharp rock mixed into the native soil. When a line was installed decades ago and backfilled with whatever came out of the trench, every rock resting against that pipe is a pressure point. Poly pipe is tough, but decades of thermal cycling and ground movement will eventually wear a hole where a rock is pressing into it.

Bad or missing bedding is the same story from the other direction. A properly installed line sits on a few inches of sand or fines, with more sand over the top before the native material goes back in. Skip that step and the line takes the full load of the backfill.

Freeze damage shows up on lines that were buried too shallow. Frost commonly reaches about two feet in the valley and deeper on the benches and up toward Leavenworth, and an older line set at eighteen inches can freeze in a hard winter. The split often does not announce itself until spring, when the ground thaws and the pipe starts leaking under pressure.

Bad fittings and old repairs are a category all their own. Barbed insert fittings with worn stainless clamps, a compression coupling that was overtightened, or a previous repair done with the wrong pressure rating of pipe are all extremely common leak points. When a line has been repaired twice already, the third leak is rarely a coincidence.

Galvanized and steel service lines corrode from the inside out. If your line dates to the 1950s or 60s and nobody has touched it since, you may be looking at a pipe that is thinning along its entire length rather than failing at one spot.

## Locating the Leak Without Trenching the Whole Yard

The good news is that finding a leak no longer means guessing and digging. The bad news is that a buried line under pressure does not always announce itself where the hole is, because water follows the path of least resistance and can travel along the trench for a surprising distance before surfacing.

Locating starts with tracing the line. A pipe locator puts a signal on a metallic line, or on a tracer wire if the original installer ran one alongside the poly. If there is no tracer wire, which is common on older properties, the line is traced by walking the logical route between the wellhead and the point of entry and confirming with test holes.

Acoustic listening equipment picks up the specific frequency of pressurized water escaping through a small opening. On a quiet property with the pump running, a good technician can often narrow a leak to within a few feet this way.

Pressure testing sections narrows the search further. By isolating the line at the pitless and putting a known pressure on it with a gauge, we can watch how fast it bleeds down. A fast drop means a large opening near the surface. A slow bleed means a pinhole, which is harder to find but also easier to live with while you plan the repair.

Then there is the honest low-tech method, which is walking the route after a dry stretch and looking for the green stripe. On irrigated properties in August that method is useless, but on dryland ground it works better than people expect.

## What This Repair Costs in the Wenatchee Valley

Costs vary more on this job than on almost anything else we do, because the number is driven by access and distance, not by parts.

Locating and diagnosis typically runs $150 to $400 depending on how much equipment the job needs and how long the line is. On a short, straightforward run, it may just be a service call.

A spot repair, meaning excavating one location and installing a coupling or a section of new pipe, commonly lands between $600 and $1,800. The variables are depth, what the machine has to dig through, and whether the leak is under a driveway, a patio, or a mature tree.

Replacing the full run is where the range widens. A short line from a wellhead sixty feet from the house may be $2,000 to $3,500. A line running three hundred feet uphill through orchard ground, across a driveway, or under landscaping can reach $6,000 to $10,000 or more. Hard rock, existing utilities in the way, and restoration work all push the number up.

Two things reliably make the bill bigger. One is finding out mid-job that the line runs somewhere nobody expected, which is why locating first is money well spent. The other is discovering that the pump and pressure tank have been running hard for months compensating for the leak and now need attention too. ## Repair the Spot, or Replace the Whole Run?

This is the decision that matters most, and it comes down to the condition of the pipe rather than the size of the hole.

Repairing one spot makes sense when the line is modern poly in good shape, the leak is clearly a single event like a rock wear point, the run is short, and the pipe elsewhere looks healthy when you have it exposed.

Replacing the whole run makes more sense when the line is galvanized or steel, when it is the second or third leak in a few years, when the existing line is undersized for what the property now demands, or when the trench has to be opened anyway. Once a machine and an operator are on site, the marginal cost of pulling new pipe the rest of the way is far lower than coming back in two years to do it all again.

There is also a capacity argument worth considering. Plenty of older properties around Quincy and Moses Lake have a 3/4 inch service line feeding a house that has since added bathrooms, a shop, or [irrigation](/blog/irrigation-well-pumps/) and [livestock demand](/blog/livestock-well-water-demand-washington/). If you are already paying to open the trench, upsizing to a 1 inch or 1 1/4 inch line costs very little extra in materials and solves a pressure complaint you were probably going to call about eventually.

## When to Stop Troubleshooting and Call

Do the shutoff valve test yourself. It is safe, it costs nothing, and it tells a contractor more than any description of symptoms could.

Past that point, call somebody. Excavating near a pressurized water line, a wellhead, or a buried electrical run feeding a submersible pump is not a DIY afternoon. The power to a well pump is a real hazard, buried utilities are rarely where people remember them being, and a wellhead that gets damaged or contaminated during a dig turns a plumbing repair into a water quality problem. If the trench opens up the wellhead itself, the EPA's guidance for [private well owners](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells) is worth reading on why keeping that connection sealed matters so much.

Call sooner rather than later if the pump is cycling every few minutes, if you have lost pressure entirely, if water is surfacing anywhere near the wellhead, or if the leak sits under a driveway or a structure where it can undermine what is on top of it. A slow leak in the middle of a pasture can wait for a scheduled appointment. Water eroding soil under a driveway cannot.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I know if the leak is in the buried line or inside my house?

Shut off every fixture, then close the main shutoff valve where the line enters the house and wait an hour. If the pump kicks on with the house valved off, the leak is on the well side, meaning the buried line, the pitless adapter, or the wellhead. If the pump stays quiet, the leak is inside the house, most often a toilet flapper or a dripping water heater relief valve.

### Can a buried water line leak without ever showing a wet spot?

Yes, and it happens often. In the coarse, rocky ground common through much of the Wenatchee valley, escaping water can drain straight down or travel along the trench line rather than surfacing. A leak can waste hundreds of gallons a day and leave nothing visible on the surface, which is why pressure testing tells you more than walking the yard does.

### How much does it cost to replace the water line from a well to a house?

Most full replacements fall between $2,000 and $10,000, driven almost entirely by distance, depth, and access rather than by materials. A sixty foot run through open ground sits at the low end. A three hundred foot run crossing a driveway, mature landscaping, or rocky orchard ground sits at the high end, and restoration work adds to it.

### Will a leaking service line damage my well pump?

Over time, yes. A leak makes the pump start and stop far more often than it was designed to, and the repeated starts are what wear out a submersible motor, not the running hours. Months of short cycling can also waterlog a pressure tank and burn out a pressure switch, so a leak left alone often turns into a larger repair than the pipe itself would have been.
