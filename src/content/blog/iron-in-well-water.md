## Why Wells Around Wenatchee Pull Up So Much Iron

Iron is the fourth most common element in the earth's crust. Our local geology has plenty of it. The Columbia River basalts that underlie most of Chelan, Douglas, and Grant counties are loaded with iron-bearing minerals, and as groundwater moves through fractures in that rock, it dissolves a little iron along the way.

Deeper wells often have more dissolved iron, not less. Down at 200 or 300 feet there's almost no oxygen in the water, and iron stays dissolved in low-oxygen conditions. That's why your water can look crystal clear at the tap and still leave orange streaks in the toilet tank a week later. The iron is invisible until air hits it.

A few other local factors make it worse:

- **Old steel well casings.** Plenty of wells around Wenatchee were drilled in the 60s and 70s with steel casing that's now rusting from the inside.
- **Orchard country water tables.** Heavy summer irrigation draws water levels down, which can pull water from different (sometimes irony) zones of the aquifer.
- **Corrosive water.** Slightly acidic water eats at steel pipes and pump components, adding rust to whatever natural iron is already there.

If your water suddenly turned rusty after years of running clean, that points to a failing casing or a corroding pump component rather than the aquifer itself. That's worth a professional look. ## The Three Types of Iron in Well Water (and Why It Matters)

Here's where most homeowners go wrong: they buy a filter before they know which kind of iron they have. Iron shows up in well water in three forms, and each one needs different treatment. Match the treatment to the type and you're done. Mismatch it and you've wasted a thousand dollars.

### Ferrous iron (clear-water iron)

This is dissolved iron. Water comes out of the tap perfectly clear, then turns orange or leaves rust stains after sitting in contact with air. Fill a white bucket and let it sit overnight. If clear water develops reddish particles by morning, you've got ferrous iron. It's the most common form in deeper wells around here.

### Ferric iron (red-water iron)

This iron has already oxidized before it reaches your tap. Water comes out tinted orange, red, or yellow, sometimes with visible rust particles. It's common in shallower wells, wells with rusting casings, and systems where water sits in a pressure tank long enough to oxidize. Sediment filters catch some of it, but they clog fast if the load is heavy.

### Iron bacteria

These are living organisms that feed on iron and leave behind a reddish-brown slime. Lift the lid on your toilet tank. If you see fuzzy rust-colored gunk clinging to the walls or floating like an oil sheen, that's iron bacteria. They aren't a health threat by themselves, but they're the hardest form to deal with, and they'll foul pumps, screens, and pressure tanks if you let them go.

## What Iron Does to Your Fixtures, Laundry, and Your Wallet

Let's be clear about one thing first: iron at the levels found in residential wells is not a health hazard. The EPA classifies iron as a secondary contaminant, which means the 0.3 ppm limit is about staining and taste, not safety. You can read the standard yourself on the [EPA's secondary drinking water standards page](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/secondary-drinking-water-standards-guidance-nuisance-chemicals).

But "not dangerous" doesn't mean "not expensive." Here's the running tab iron quietly builds:

- **Stained fixtures.** Orange rings in tubs, toilets, and sinks that resist normal cleaners. Replacing stained fixtures runs $150 to $600 each.
- **Ruined laundry.** Iron binds to fabric, and chlorine bleach makes it worse, setting the stain permanently. One bad load can cost you $100 in whites.
- **Clogged pipes and valves.** Iron deposits narrow pipes over time and gum up fill valves, dishwasher solenoids, and water heater elements.
- **Shortened appliance life.** Water heaters, dishwashers, and washing machines in heavy-iron homes often die 3 to 5 years early.

There's also the taste. Above about 0.5 ppm, water picks up a metallic flavor that ruins coffee, tea, and anything you cook in it. ## When Iron Is a Symptom, Not the Problem

Most of the time, iron in well water is just geology. But sometimes the rust is a warning light for failing hardware, and treating the water without fixing the hardware is throwing money down the well.

Watch for these patterns:

- **Sudden rusty water in a well that ran clear for years.** Suspect a corroding steel casing or a failing galvanized drop pipe. A camera inspection will confirm it.
- **Rust plus sand or grit.** A corroded casing or damaged screen may be letting sediment in along with the rust.
- **Rust only on the hot side.** That's your water heater's anode rod or tank rusting out, not your well. Cheaper fix, different trade.
- **Iron staining plus pressure loss plus a pump that runs longer than it used to.** Iron buildup or bacterial slime may be choking the pump intake. Catch it early and you're cleaning a screen, not buying a motor.

That last one is the expensive trap, because pumps fail quietly until they fail completely. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is iron in well water safe to drink?

Yes, at the levels found in virtually all private wells. The EPA's 0.3 ppm limit is a secondary standard based on staining and taste, not health risk. Your body actually needs iron, and water is a minor source compared to food. The practical problems are stained fixtures, ruined laundry, metallic taste, and long-term plumbing damage.

### How do I know if I have iron bacteria instead of regular iron?

Check your toilet tank, since it's a quiet, dark reservoir where the bacteria thrive. Reddish-brown slime, fuzzy growth on the walls, or a rainbow oil-like sheen on the water points to iron bacteria. Regular dissolved iron leaves hard orange staining without the slime. A lab can confirm it with a biological activity test if you're not sure.

### Will a water softener remove iron from my well water?

Only a little, and at a cost. Softeners can exchange out small amounts of dissolved ferrous iron, roughly up to 1 or 2 ppm, but iron gradually fouls the resin and shortens the softener's life. If your test shows meaningful iron, install a dedicated iron filter ahead of the softener so each unit does the job it's built for.

Light particle iron might need only a $300 to $800 sediment setup, while iron bacteria or very high iron can push a chlorination-plus-filtration system to $5,500.
