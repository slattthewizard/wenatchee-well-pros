You lock the cabin door in October, and you don't think about the well again until Memorial Day. That gap, five or six months where nobody's watching the system, is exactly when a seasonal cabin well gets into trouble, and most of the damage happens quietly enough that you won't know until you turn the water back on.

Seasonal cabin well startup and shutdown isn't the same job as winterizing a house you live in every day. A cabin sits empty and unheated for months, and whatever went wrong in December is waiting for you the first weekend you show up in May. Get the order right on both ends and a cabin well runs for decades with almost no drama. Skip steps and you're looking at burst pipes, seized pumps, or water you can't trust to drink.

## Why Seasonal Wells Around Leavenworth, Plain, and Chelan Take More Damage Than Year-Round Ones

A well that runs every day, even lightly, has a few things going for it. Water is moving through the lines regularly, which resists freezing. Someone notices right away if pressure drops or a fixture starts acting strange. None of that applies to a cabin that sits closed from fall through spring.

North Central Washington makes the empty months harder on a system than most places. Up around Plain and Lake Wenatchee, and in the higher draws near Leavenworth, winters run colder and longer than in Wenatchee proper. A cabin well with no heat source and no one checking on it can sit through weeks of single-digit nights with nothing standing between a hard freeze and an exposed pipe.

Lake Chelan cabins face a different version of the same problem. Many sit on older lots where the well was drilled decades ago, and the property can sit vacant for a full season with nobody visiting at all. A well unused that long doesn't just risk freeze damage. Stagnant water in a pressure tank and casing all winter is exactly the environment bacteria and biofilm need to get established.

The fix for both problems is the same: a real shutdown routine in the fall and a real startup routine in the spring, done in the right order, every single year, regardless of how mild the past winter seemed.

## Shutting Down a Seasonal Cabin Well for Winter: The Checklist

Do this before your last visit of the season, not on your way out the door with the car loaded. Some of these take a few minutes but ruin your whole weekend if skipped.

1. **Drain every exposed line and hydrant.** Outdoor faucets, yard hydrants, hose bibs, and any above-ground irrigation piping need to be drained and left open. Disconnect every hose. A hose left attached traps water behind even a supposedly frost-free hydrant.
2. **Check the pressure tank and switch.** If the tank sits in an unheated crawl space or pump house, confirm it's insulated and the air charge is correct. A waterlogged tank freezes and cracks faster than one holding proper pressure.
3. **Insulate or heat the pump house.** A cheap heat lamp on a thermostatic plug can keep a small pump house above freezing through most of the winter for about $35 in equipment. We cover the full setup in our guide to [winterizing a well pump](/blog/winterize-well-pump/), which applies just as much to a cabin as a full-time house.
4. **Decide whether to shut off the well breaker.** If nobody will be checking on the property, most cabin owners shut the pump breaker off entirely rather than leave it live and unsupervised all winter. Leave a note at the panel so whoever opens the place next spring knows what to expect.
5. **Consider shock chlorinating before you close up**, especially if you did any work on the well that season, pulled the pump, or replaced the cap. Disinfecting before a long closure gives you a clean baseline instead of finding out what grew in there over the winter.
6. **Document what you did and when.** A simple note taped inside the pump house, breaker off, tank drained, date, saves the next person real guesswork.

Skip step one and you're rolling the dice on the priciest single mistake we see on cabin properties. A cracked yard hydrant or a burst line behind an exterior wall doesn't show itself until spring, and by then it's flooded a crawl space or soaked into insulation for months.

## Starting Up a Seasonal Cabin Well in Spring: The Checklist

Opening weekend at the cabin should not be the first time you find out something's wrong. Work through these before you run water through the whole house.

1. **Inspect the wellhead before you do anything else.** Walk out to the cap and casing. Look for cracks, a chewed vent screen, or signs an animal got in over the winter. A damaged cap left unnoticed is how contamination gets a foothold during the closure.
2. **Turn the breaker back on and listen.** With the tank pressure released, the pump should cycle on, build pressure, and shut off cleanly. Hunting, humming with no water movement, or tripping the breaker means stop and troubleshoot before you flip on every faucet in the house.
3. **Open taps slowly, one at a time**, starting with an outside spigot if you have one. This lets trapped air escape gradually and gives you a chance to spot a leak before it's pressurized through the whole system.
4. **Check every fixture and joint you drained in the fall.** The connections you disconnected for winter are the same ones most likely to leak the first time they're pressurized again.
5. **Test the water before you drink it**, particularly if the place sat closed for more than a couple months. A basic coliform and nitrate panel runs $150 to $400 and tells you in a few days whether the water is safe, rather than guessing.
6. **Shock chlorinate if you skipped it in the fall**, or if the test comes back positive. Our [step-by-step shock chlorination guide](/blog/well-chlorination-shock/) walks through bleach amounts by well depth and how long to wait before retesting, and it's the same process whether the well sat closed two months or eight.

That order matters. Testing water before the pump and pressure system are confirmed working just wastes a sample. Inspecting the wellhead first catches problems before they get expensive.

## Water Quality After a Season Closed: Testing and Chlorination

Stagnant water sitting untouched in a casing and pressure tank for months is one of the more overlooked risks with seasonal properties. It's not usually dangerous water, but it's water you shouldn't assume is fine just because it looks and smells normal.

A well that sat closed all winter should get tested before anyone drinks from it, full stop. That's a basic coliform and nitrate panel through a certified lab, the same test recommended annually for any private well. If the property changed hands during the closure, or if you're not certain the previous owner tested regularly, add a fuller panel that also checks for arsenic and other regional concerns common in North Central Washington groundwater.

A positive test after a long closure doesn't mean the well is ruined. It usually means the water needs the same shock chlorination process used for any coliform hit, and cabins that sit closed for a full season are exactly the situation where a shock treatment before closing up in the fall pays for itself. Doing it going into the closure means you're not starting the season with a boil-water guess and a two-week wait on a retest before your first weekend up there.

The [Washington Department of Health's private well guidance](https://doh.wa.gov/community-and-environment/drinking-water/private-wells) covers testing frequency and what results mean for private well owners, and it's worth bookmarking if you manage a well you only visit part of the year. The [EPA's private well page](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells) has similar guidance if you're comparing recommendations across sources.

## When to Call a Professional Instead of Doing It Yourself

Most of the checklist above is manageable on your own. Draining hydrants, insulating a pump house, and flipping a breaker don't require special tools or licensing. A few situations are worth a call before you touch anything further:

- **The wellhead looks damaged**, whether that's a cracked cap, a corroded seal, or evidence something got into the casing over the winter.
- **The pump won't cycle normally on startup**, humming without building pressure, tripping the breaker, or short cycling. That's a sign of a real problem, not something to keep flipping the breaker on and hoping clears itself.
- **Anything involves the wellhead's electrical connections.** Flipping the breaker is fine. Touching wiring or the pressure switch internals is not, since well circuits run on 240 volts.
- **The property changed hands and you have no history on the well.** A first visit from a licensed tech before you rely on the water is worth the cost of a service call.
- **A water test comes back positive** and you're not confident running the shock chlorination process correctly on your own.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I shut off the well pump breaker completely when closing a cabin for the season?

Most cabin owners are better off shutting the breaker off entirely if nobody will be checking on the property for months, since a pump left live with no one to notice a problem can run into trouble unsupervised. The tradeoff is that the system needs a proper startup check in spring rather than just resuming on its own. Leave a note at the panel noting the date and what was done so whoever opens the cabin next knows where things stand.

### How long can a cabin well sit unused before the water quality is a concern?

There's no hard cutoff, but anything beyond a couple of months of complete closure is worth testing before you drink the water again. Stagnant water in a casing and pressure tank gives bacteria more time to establish than a well in regular daily use. A basic coliform and nitrate test after reopening is the only way to know for sure rather than assuming it's fine because it looks clear.

### Do I need to shock chlorinate my cabin well every time I close it for winter?

Not every single closure requires it, but it's smart practice any time you did work on the well that year, aren't certain of its recent testing history, or are closing for a particularly long stretch, like a full season with no visits planned. Shocking before closing gives you a clean baseline instead of an unknown one, and it's far cheaper to do proactively than to deal with a positive test and a delayed reopening in spring.

### What's the biggest mistake cabin owners make with seasonal well shutdown and startup?

Draining exposed lines and hydrants is the step most often skipped, and it's also the one that causes the most expensive damage, since a burst pipe or cracked hydrant can flood a crawl space over an entire winter before anyone notices. The second most common mistake is skipping a water test after a long closure and assuming the water is fine because nothing looks different. Both take only minutes to address and prevent the repairs that run into the thousands.
