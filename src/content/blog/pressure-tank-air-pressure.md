Nobody checks it. Not the last owner of the house, often not the person who installed the tank, and probably not you, standing in the pump house wondering why the pump kicks on every time someone runs the dishwasher. Pressure tank air pressure is the one number on a well system that almost never gets looked at, and when it's wrong, the pump pays for it. Checking it takes about ten minutes with a tire gauge you probably already own.

## What the Air Charge Actually Does

Inside a modern bladder-style pressure tank, a rubber bladder separates two things: water on one side, compressed air on the other. Before any water ever enters the tank, that air side is charged to a set pressure, called the precharge. As the pump pushes water in, the bladder squeezes the air pocket smaller and the air pressure climbs. When you open a tap, the compressed air pushes water back out, and pressure drops until the switch tells the pump to start again.

Get the precharge right and that air cushion does real work: it delivers several gallons before the pump has to start at all, so the motor isn't cycling on and off every time someone washes their hands. Get it wrong, low or gone entirely, and the tank stops cushioning anything. The pump ends up starting almost every time water moves, which is hard on a motor built for long, infrequent runs rather than short, constant ones.

Tank size changes the math but not the idea. A small 20-gallon tank on a cabin system and an 80-gallon tank feeding a full house both rely on the same air cushion principle, just at different volumes. Whatever size sits in your pump house, the precharge number is set by your pressure switch, not by the tank's size.

## The 2 PSI Rule for Pressure Tank Air Pressure Settings

The rule is simple, and it's the one the Washington State Department of Health spells out in its [tech tip on bladder pressure tanks](https://doh.wa.gov/sites/default/files/legacy/Documents/Pubs/331-342.pdf): set the air precharge 2 psi below your pressure switch's cut-in setting, the pressure at which the pump turns on. Not the cut-out. Not the middle. Two pounds under cut-in.

| Pressure switch setting (cut-in / cut-out) | Air precharge should read |
|---|---|
| 30 / 50 | 28 psi |
| 40 / 60 | 38 psi |
| 50 / 70 | 48 psi |

Those three cover most houses on a private well, but they aren't the only settings out there. If your switch is set to something else, subtract 2 from whatever the cut-in actually is.

Why 2 under and not exactly at cut-in? Because it leaves a little water in the bladder at the moment the pump starts. The tank never runs completely dry, the bladder isn't squashed flat against the outlet every cycle, and you still get nearly all the stored water the tank was built to give you between starts.

## Finding Your Cut-In Pressure First

You can't apply the 2 psi rule without knowing your cut-in, and plenty of switches have been adjusted or swapped over the years without anyone updating a label. Two ways to find it. Open a tap, watch the system gauge fall, and note exactly where the needle sits the instant the pump kicks on. That's your cut-in. Or read the switch: most are marked 30-50 or 40-60 on the housing or the box it came in.

If the two don't match, trust the gauge. A switch that's been serviced twice since it was installed may not be set anywhere near its label. If you want to confirm both numbers before touching the air side, see [how to read and adjust a pressure switch](/blog/well-pump-pressure-switch-adjustment/).

## How to Check Pressure Tank Air Pressure, Step by Step

This follows the same order as the DOH tech tip, and it works the same on a five-year-old tank or a twenty-year-old one.

### 1. Shut off power at the breaker

Kill the well pump breaker before you touch anything. You don't want the pump starting while the tank is draining down.

None of this requires opening the pressure switch cover. That cover hides 240 volt contacts and the cut-in and cut-out adjustment screws, and it has nothing to do with the tank's air side. Checking or adding air pressure is a job you can do without ever going near it.

### 2. Drain the tank completely

Open a low faucet or an outside hose bib and let it run until the flow stops, not just slows down. The system gauge should read zero. A tank with water still in it gives you a false reading.

### 3. Find the valve and read it

Look for a Schrader valve on top of the tank. It looks just like the valve on a car or bike tire, because mechanically it's the same part. Press a decent tire gauge onto it and read the number.

### 4. Add air if it's low

If the reading is under the target for your switch setting, add air in short bursts using a compressor or a bike pump, checking the gauge after each burst so you don't overshoot. Small pumps and compressors both work fine, since the volume of air a tank needs is modest.

### 5. Recheck, close up, and restart

Once the number matches your target, close the faucet, restore power at the breaker, and watch a full cycle. The pump should start at cut-in and stop at cut-out, same as before.

If air comes out mixed with water or a fine mist when you press the valve pin, stop. That means the bladder has already failed, and no amount of added air will fix it. That's a [waterlogged tank](/blog/pressure-tank-waterlogged/), and the fix is a new tank, not a top-up. ## Why a Full Tank Reading Lies to You

This is the mistake people make most. Press a gauge onto that valve while the tank is full and pressurized, and you'll read something close to your system pressure, 45 or 55 psi, and think everything's great. It isn't telling you anything. With water in the tank, the air and the water sit at the same pressure, so the valve just reads whatever the system is at.

That's why step two isn't optional. The precharge only shows up on the gauge with the tank drained and the water side at zero. Skip the drain and you can walk away happy with an air charge that's actually gone.

## Signs Your Air Charge Is Too Low or Too High

Low air pressure is the far more common problem, and it shows up as [short cycling](/blog/well-pump-short-cycling/): the pump starting and stopping every few seconds, sometimes dozens of times an hour instead of a handful. With little or no air cushion left to push water out on its own, almost any water use forces the pump to start. That wears out switch contacts and motor windings well ahead of schedule.

Too much air is less common, and it's usually someone overcorrecting. If the precharge sits above cut-in, the tank empties before the pressure falls far enough to start the pump. For a moment the house has no stored water and no pump, so you get a sharp pressure dip at the tap, and you get less water between starts than the tank should give you. That's what "weak drawdown" means. Either direction is a ten-minute fix once you know the target number.

A common version of this: a rancher on a 40/60 switch where the pump has been starting every couple of minutes for weeks, and nobody's checked the tank because pressure at the taps looks fine. Pressure at the faucet can look completely normal while the pump underneath is cycling itself ragged. A weak air cushion still lets the pump build pressure. It just leaves the pump doing all the work.

## When the Charge Won't Hold, or the System Is Different

A precharge that's right one month and low again a few weeks later means something is leaking. Start with the cheap suspect: dab soapy water on the valve, and if it bubbles, the valve core is leaking. That's the same little part that fails on a bike tire, and it's a cheap swap. If the valve is dry and the charge keeps dropping, the air is going somewhere else, usually a bladder on its way out, and the drift gets faster until the tank is waterlogged. A charge that won't hold for a few weeks is your cue to stop topping it off and have the tank looked at.

One more thing worth remembering: if you or a tech ever changes the pressure switch setting, change the precharge to match. The two numbers are linked. A tank set for a 30/50 switch that later gets bumped to 40/60 needs its air charge moved up too, or the math above stops working.

Never pump a tank past the maximum working pressure stamped on its own label. That number is specific to the tank, not the switch, and it's there for a reason.

Constant pressure and variable-speed well systems are a different animal entirely. Those use a drive that holds one steady pressure rather than cycling between a cut-in and cut-out point, and the tank precharge on that kind of setup is set by the drive manufacturer's own spec, not the 2 psi rule above. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What should my pressure tank be set at?

Set the air precharge 2 psi below your pressure switch's cut-in number, measured with the tank fully drained and the power off. A 30/50 switch calls for 28 psi, a 40/60 switch calls for 38 psi, and a 50/70 switch calls for 48 psi. If your switch has been changed at some point, trust what the gauge shows during a real cycle over any sticker on the housing.

### How much air should be in a well pressure tank?

It depends on your pressure switch setting, not a single fixed number for every system. The target is always 2 psi under cut-in, so a system that starts the pump at 40 psi needs 38 psi in the air side, while one that starts at 30 psi needs 28. Constant pressure and variable-speed systems are the exception and follow the drive manufacturer's own spec instead.

### Can I add air to my pressure tank myself?

Yes, and it's one of the few jobs on a well system that doesn't involve opening anything electrical. Shut off the pump breaker, drain the tank completely at a low faucet, then add air through the Schrader valve on top in short bursts with a compressor or bike pump, checking the gauge often so you don't overshoot the target.

### How often should I check pressure tank air pressure?

Once a year is enough for most households, ideally on the same visit where you're already checking other seasonal items around the house. Check it sooner any time you notice the pump starting and stopping more often than usual, since that's the clearest sign the air charge has dropped low.
