A July storm rolls up the Columbia, a transformer somewhere above your road takes a hit, and the next morning there is no water. You open the pump house and the control box is scorched. The quote to put water back in the house is $3,100. Your first thought, reasonably, is that this is exactly what you pay insurance for.

So does homeowners insurance cover a well pump? Sometimes. The pump itself is almost never the deciding factor. What decides the claim is why it died, and most well pumps die of something no policy in America pays for.

## The Question Your Insurer Is Actually Asking

An adjuster is not asking whether a well pump is covered equipment. On most policies it is, either as part of the dwelling or as detached structure or personal property, depending on how it is mounted and how the policy is written.

The adjuster is asking a different question: was this sudden and accidental, caused by something on the list of covered perils? A standard homeowners policy pays for damage from fire, lightning, windstorm, explosion, falling objects, vehicle impact, vandalism and a short list of similar events. The Insurance Information Institute keeps a plain-language summary of [what a standard homeowners policy covers](https://www.iii.org/article/what-covered-standard-homeowners-policy) that is worth ten minutes before you call your agent.

What no standard policy covers is equipment that simply wore out. Insurance is not a maintenance plan, and a well pump is a motor with bearings and seals running underwater for a decade or more. Wearing out is what it is designed to eventually do.

That distinction explains nearly every approval and nearly every denial we see. Lightning is a peril. Fifteen years of sand is a maintenance issue.

## What Homeowners Insurance Covers on a Private Well

Here is how the common failure causes typically land. Coverage varies by carrier and by the exact form of your policy, so treat this as the shape of the answer, not a guarantee.

| What caused the failure | Usually covered? | What the adjuster looks at |
|---|---|---|
| Lightning strike or utility power surge | Often yes | Scorched wiring, utility outage records, storm date |
| Fire in the pump house | Usually yes | Named peril on nearly every policy |
| Falling tree or vehicle strike on the wellhead | Usually yes | Sudden, accidental, external cause |
| Vandalism or stolen equipment | Often yes | Police report, evidence of forced entry or removal |
| Pump worn out after 12 to 15 years | No | Age, service history, gradual loss of pressure |
| Sand or sediment abrasion | No | Water quality, wear over time |
| Motor burned out from short cycling | Almost never | Traced back to a failing pressure tank or switch |
| Freezing in an unheated pump house | Depends | Whether heat was maintained or the system was drained |
| Spring runoff or flooding into the wellhead | Not under a standard policy | Needs separate flood coverage |
| Ground settling or earth movement | No | Standard exclusion on most forms |

Two more things shape the payout even when the cause is covered. The first is whether your policy pays replacement cost or actual cash value. Actual cash value subtracts depreciation, so a twelve-year-old pump destroyed by lightning may be valued at a fraction of what a new one costs.

The second is your deductible. Deductibles on rural Washington homes commonly run somewhere between $1,000 and $2,500, and that is a general figure rather than a quote from us. Against a repair bill in the low four figures, the deductible often eats most of the claim.

## Why Wear and Tear Sinks Most Well Pump Insurance Claims

We pull a lot of pumps. The honest breakdown of why they come out is roughly this: age and hours, sand and grit, and electrical damage caused by something else in the system failing first.

Only that last category ever gets interesting to an insurer, and even then the trail usually leads somewhere excluded. A waterlogged pressure tank makes the pump start and stop dozens of times an hour. Every start is a heat spike in the motor windings. Six months of that and the motor burns out.

From your side that looks like a sudden failure. From the adjuster's side it is a maintenance defect that caused predictable damage, and the file gets closed. The same logic applies to a pump that has been pulling sand for years, or a pressure switch that has been chattering since spring.

This is also why a service record helps you more than an argument does. If you can show the pressure tank was checked, the switch was in good order and the system was running normally the week before a storm, you have a story an adjuster can approve. If the file shows nothing since the house was bought, you have an age problem.

Worth knowing separately: the manufacturer's warranty on a newer pump is a different door entirely, and it sometimes pays when insurance will not. We covered that in detail in our post on [well pump warranty coverage](/blog/well-pump-warranty-coverage/).

## Three Failures From Around the Valley and How the Coverage Landed

Names and addresses stay out of these on purpose. The numbers are real.

### A 1990s manufactured home on a 220-foot well outside East Wenatchee

Summer storm, a confirmed outage on the utility's own log, and a control box with burn marks inside it. The submersible motor was shorted to ground when we tested it. This was as clean a lightning claim as they come.

Our invoice was $3,100: $2,750 to replace the submersible pump and $350 for a new control box, both inside our normal ranges. The carrier paid on the claim and the homeowner covered their $1,000 deductible, so roughly $2,100 came back. The outage record from the utility did more work in that file than anything we wrote.

### An orchard house above Cashmere on a submersible and a 44-gallon bladder tank

The pump was fourteen years old. The bladder tank had failed months earlier and nobody noticed, so the pump had been short cycling through a whole irrigation season. The motor finally quit in August with fruit on the trees.

Claim denied, and honestly it was the correct call under the policy. The repair came to $4,300: $3,200 for the pump and $1,100 for a new pressure tank, paid out of pocket. Had the tank been replaced when it first went waterlogged, the bill would have been the tank alone.

### A cabin near Leavenworth left unheated over Christmas

The power went out during a cold snap, the pump house dropped below freezing and the pressure tank split. Water ran until someone arrived in January.

The carrier paid for the water damage to the structure but excluded the well equipment, because the policy required either maintained heat or a drained system in an unoccupied dwelling. Our part came to $1,570: $1,350 for the pressure tank and $220 for the pressure switch. ## Freezing and Flooding: The Two Exclusions That Catch People Here

These two deserve their own warning because North Central Washington produces both, and both surprise homeowners who assumed they were covered.

Freezing damage to plumbing is generally covered while you live in the house. It is generally excluded once the dwelling is vacant or unoccupied, unless you used reasonable care to keep the heat on or shut the water off and drained the system. Cabins, rentals between tenants and houses on the market are the ones that get caught.

Flood is simply not part of a homeowners policy anywhere in the country. Spring runoff pouring into a wellhead, a creek jumping its bank, saturated ground pushing water down the casing: none of that is covered without a separate flood policy. If your wellhead sits low, read our post on a [flooded wellhead during spring runoff](/blog/flooded-wellhead-spring-runoff/) before the melt starts.

If you are unsure what your own form says, the Washington State Office of the Insurance Commissioner answers consumer questions and reviews complaints against carriers at [insurance.wa.gov](https://www.insurance.wa.gov/). They are free and they are not selling you anything.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does homeowners insurance cover a well pump that burned out?

Usually not. A burned out motor is normally the end of a gradual process, whether that is age, sand or short cycling from a failing pressure tank, and gradual wear is excluded on every standard policy. The exception is when an outside event burned it out, such as a lightning strike or a utility power surge, which is a covered peril on most forms.

### Will my insurance pay to replace the whole well?

Almost never. The well itself, meaning the borehole and casing, is treated as land improvement or as part of the property in ways that most policies exclude, and drilling is not covered as a repair. Equipment damage from a covered peril is a different question and can be paid, but a dry or collapsed well is a homeowner expense.

### Should I file a claim for a $900 well pump repair?

Probably not, if your deductible is $1,000 or more. Even below the deductible line, a claim on your record can affect renewal pricing, so many homeowners pay small repairs directly. Get the diagnosis first, because the difference between a $250 switch and a $3,000 pump changes the answer completely.

### Does insurance cover water damage caused by a failed pressure tank?

Often yes, even when the tank itself is not covered. Most policies separate the failed part, which is excluded as wear, from the sudden water damage it caused, which is frequently covered. That is exactly how the Leavenworth cabin above was settled, so it is worth asking your carrier about the damage even when the equipment is denied.
