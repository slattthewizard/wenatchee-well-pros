# How to Test Well Water at Home: What a Kit Can Tell You, and What Needs a Lab

Most well owners find out they need to test their water at an inconvenient moment: buying a house, noticing a new taste, or just realizing it has been years since anyone checked. The next question is almost always the same. Do you grab a test kit off the shelf, or send a sample to a lab? The honest answer is that you will probably do both, just not for the same things. Here is what each one is actually good for, so you are not paying for a lab test a strip could have answered, or trusting a strip for something only a lab can tell you. For a broader rundown of what to test and when in this area, see our guide to [well water testing in Wenatchee](/blog/well-water-testing-wenatchee/); this article focuses specifically on the kit-versus-lab question.

## What a Home Test Strip or Kit Is Good For

Dip strips and small home kits are fast and cheap, and for a handful of measurements that is exactly what you need. Strips can give you a reasonable read on pH and on general hardness, the kind of number that tells you whether your water leans acidic, alkaline, or somewhere in between, and whether it is mineral heavy enough to explain scale on fixtures. Iron test kits can flag whether iron is present at a level worth paying attention to, though they read in ranges rather than an exact lab number. If you are on a shared system or your water gets chlorinated somewhere upstream, a chlorine test strip is a quick way to check a residual.

Think of these as screening tools. They are good for a first look, for tracking a known issue day to day between lab checks, or for confirming that a change you made (adjusting a softener, replacing a filter) actually moved the number. What they are not good for is anything with a health consequence attached to a specific concentration, or anything a lender, health department, or insurer would need to see documented.

## What a Home Kit Cannot Tell You

Strips and home kits cannot reliably detect bacteria, and they cannot give you a defensible number for nitrate, arsenic, lead, or PFAS. Bacteria testing requires a culture process in a lab, not a color change on a card. Nitrate and arsenic need instrument-based chemistry to get an accurate reading at the levels that actually matter for health, and a drugstore kit simply is not built for that. Both the [CDC's guidelines for testing well water](https://www.cdc.gov/drinking-water/safety/guidelines-for-testing-well-water.html) and the [EPA's guidance for protecting your home's water](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/protect-your-homes-water) point private well owners to a state-certified laboratory for well water testing.

There is also a paperwork reason to use a lab. If you are selling a home, refinancing, or filing an insurance claim, a report from an accredited lab holds up. A test strip reading does not. ## Home Kit or Lab: A Quick Side-by-Side

| What's being measured | Home strip or kit | Certified lab |
|---|---|---|
| pH | Yes, a reasonable quick read | Yes, more precise |
| General hardness | Yes, good for a ballpark number | Yes, exact number |
| Iron (screening only) | Rough presence or absence | Yes, exact concentration and form |
| Chlorine or chloramine residual | Yes, useful on treated or shared systems | Yes |
| Total coliform bacteria | Not reliable | Yes, this is a lab-only test |
| Nitrate and nitrite | Not reliable at the levels that matter | Yes, this is a lab-only test |
| Arsenic | Not reliable | Yes, this is a lab-only test |
| Lead | At best "detected or not detected" | Yes, exact concentration |
| PFAS | No home option exists | Yes, though not every lab offers it |

## Coliform Bacteria and Nitrate: Test These Every Year, at a Lab

If you only test for two things every year, this is the pair. The [EPA recommends annual testing](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/protect-your-homes-water) for total coliform bacteria, nitrate, total dissolved solids, and pH, and Washington's Department of Health recommends private well owners test every year for coliform bacteria and nitrate specifically, because both can affect health quickly, possibly with just one drink of water. That is a different risk profile than most of the other things on a lab menu, and it is why these two do not wait for a multi-year rotation.

Nitrate is worth taking seriously in orchard country. Fertilizer and manure applied to nearby land can move through soil into shallow groundwater, and a well that tested clean five years ago is not guaranteed to test clean today. Our [nitrates in well water](/blog/nitrates-in-well-water/) article covers what a positive result means and what to do next, and our [coliform bacteria](/blog/coliform-bacteria-well-water/) article covers the same for a bacteria hit. Both require a lab. Neither shows up reliably on a strip.

## Arsenic Testing Runs on a Slower Clock

Arsenic gets treated differently, and it is worth understanding why so you do not either skip it entirely or over-test for it. Washington's Department of Health does not put arsenic on the same annual schedule as coliform and nitrate. Instead, the [state's guidance on testing your water](https://doh.wa.gov/community-and-environment/drinking-water/contaminants/testing-your-water) suggests testing for arsenic at least twice over the time you own the well, once in summer and once in winter, to catch any seasonal difference in the level. The reasoning is that arsenic does not cause a rapid health effect the way bacteria or nitrate can. The concern is continued exposure over time, so the testing schedule reflects that slower kind of risk rather than an emergency one.

Say your two arsenic results come back close to each other across seasons and under the health-based limit. That meets the state's suggestion; retest if your water changes or a lab or your county advises it. If a result comes back elevated, our [arsenic in well water](/blog/arsenic-in-well-water-washington/) article covers testing frequency and treatment options in more depth. Either way, this is a lab test, not a kit.

## When a Full Lab Panel Is Worth It

Beyond the annual basics, there are specific moments when paying for a broader panel makes sense rather than sticking to coliform and nitrate alone. New wells and home purchases are the clearest case. Washington State University's extension guidance on well maintenance recommends [a comprehensive baseline panel](https://extension.wsu.edu/clark/naturalresources/smallacreageprogram/simple-tips-for-well-maintenance/) when a well is new or changing hands, covering the range of contaminants that could plausibly be present, then narrowing to targeted testing after that because testing everything every year is not something most households can justify on cost. The [USGS's own guidance on where to get well water tested](https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/where-can-i-get-my-well-water-tested) points in the same direction: start with your county, since some counties offer free well water testing, then move to a state-certified lab if your county does not offer what you need.

Other good reasons to expand beyond the annual basics: a change in taste, smell, or appearance that was not there before, flooding at or near the wellhead, recent well or pump repairs, or a household with a pregnant or nursing family member, an infant, or an elderly resident, since some contaminants matter more for those groups. ## Taking a Sample the Lab Can Actually Use

A lab result is only as good as the sample that reached it. A few habits make the difference between a result you can trust and one you have to explain away later.

1. Call the lab before you collect anything. Washington's Department of Ecology puts it simply: [sampling your well water starts with contacting an accredited lab](https://ecology.wa.gov/water-shorelines/water-supply/wells/testing-drinking-water) first, since different tests need different bottles, preservatives, and handling.
2. Use the container the lab gives you, not a jar from the kitchen. Bacteria sample bottles are typically sterile and sometimes contain a preservative tablet. Do not rinse it out.
3. Sample before any treatment equipment, not after. If your water passes through a softener, filter, or chlorinator before it reaches most taps, sample from a tap that bypasses the equipment, such as a sample port near the pressure tank, so the result reflects the well, not the equipment. Ask the lab which tap it wants, since some advise against outdoor hose bibs for bacteria samples.
4. Follow the lab's specific instructions on running the water first or not. This differs by test, so do not assume the same routine applies to every bottle.
5. Get it to the lab quickly. Bacteria samples in particular can change while they sit, so plan your sample day around the lab's drop-off hours rather than collecting on a Friday afternoon and hoping for the best.
6. Test again after any event that could change your water: flooding at the wellhead, nearby well work, or a repair on your own system. Washington's Ecology guidance specifically calls out flooding, and a home sale, as situations that should trigger a fresh sample rather than relying on an old one.

## When the Sample Points to the Well, Not the Water

Sometimes a water test tells you less about water chemistry and more about the well itself. Say your lab results come back clean for bacteria and nitrate, but the sample jar had visible grit sitting in the bottom that was not there the last time you checked. That is not a chemistry problem a filter or a softener will fix. It can point to a failing well screen, a pump set too close to the bottom of the well, or a casing issue letting sediment in, and that is a well and pump question, not a water treatment one.

The same logic applies to sudden cloudiness right after you pull a sample, a metallic smell that was not there before, or a noticeable drop in flow while you are filling the sample bottle. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a home test kit replace my annual well water test?

Not for coliform bacteria or nitrate. Home kits can give you a fast read on pH, hardness, and a rough iron screening, but they are not built to detect bacteria reliably or to measure nitrate accurately at the levels that matter for health. Washington's guidance for private well owners calls for annual coliform and nitrate testing at a certified lab, and a home kit is not a substitute for that specific pair. Use a kit for the things it is good at, and keep sending the annual samples to a lab.

### How do I know if I need arsenic testing on top of the annual basics?

Washington's Department of Health suggests testing for arsenic at least twice over the years you own the well, once in summer and once in winter, rather than every year. It is not part of the recommended annual coliform and nitrate check. If you have never tested for it, or it has been years, that is a reasonable time to add it to your next lab order. A certified lab can run it alongside your regular sample.

### What container do I use to collect a well water sample?

Whatever container the lab hands you for that specific test, and nothing else. Bacteria bottles are usually sterile and sometimes have a preservative tablet inside, and other tests may need different bottles or preservatives entirely. Call the lab before you collect anything so you get the right container and the right instructions for that test, including whether to run the tap first. Using a jar from home, or the wrong bottle, can invalidate an otherwise good sample.

### My test strip showed a problem, but the lab result came back clean. What now?

Strips read in ranges and can be affected by lighting, timing, or how the color is read, so a strip flag and a lab result will not always agree, especially for iron or hardness. If the lab used a certified process and tested for the same thing, trust the lab number. If the strip flagged something the lab does not test for by default, like a specific metal or bacteria type, ask the lab whether that test can be added, or whether what you are seeing points to a well or plumbing issue rather than water chemistry at all.
