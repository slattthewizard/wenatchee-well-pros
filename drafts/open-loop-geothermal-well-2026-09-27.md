# Geothermal Well Systems: What Open-Loop Actually Means for Your Water Supply

If you're researching a "geothermal well," you've probably run into two very different things wearing the same name. One kind of geothermal system never touches your well at all. The other one runs your well water through a heat pump and back into the ground. Knowing which one a contractor is quoting changes what you need to ask about your water supply, and in Washington, it changes what state rules apply.

We don't install geothermal systems. Our work is wells, pumps, tanks, wiring, and the wellhead. But a lot of open-loop geothermal questions come back to exactly that equipment, so this is where we can actually help.

## What "Geothermal Well" Actually Means

Ground source heat pumps move heat between your house and the earth, and they do it two different ways. A closed-loop system circulates water or an antifreeze solution through sealed plastic tubing buried in the yard, laid in a pond, or run down a vertical borehole. No water is pulled from the ground and none of it ever mixes with groundwater. The [Department of Energy's overview of geothermal heat pumps](https://www.energy.gov/hgeo/geothermal/geothermal-heat-pumps) describes this kind of buried loop, laid either horizontally or vertically. A less common variation, direct exchange, runs refrigerant through buried copper tubing instead of a water loop.

An open-loop system is the one people usually mean when they search "geothermal well." It draws real groundwater from a well, runs it once through the heat pump's heat exchanger, and sends it back out, either to a second well, an approved discharge, or in some cases surface water. Because the water passes through only once, this design is often called "pump and dump." A variation called a standing column well uses one deep borehole for both jobs: water is drawn from the bottom of the column and returned near the top.

| | Closed-loop (horizontal, vertical, pond) | Open-loop (pump and dump) | Standing column well |
|---|---|---|---|
| Needs a well | No | Yes, usually a supply well and a return well | Yes, one deep well |
| What moves through it | Sealed water or antifreeze loop | Real groundwater, used once | Real groundwater, used once |
| Where the water ends up | Recirculates, never leaves the loop | Second well, drainfield, or approved surface discharge | Returned to the same borehole at a different depth |
| Our role | None, this is a geothermal contractor's work | Well yield, pump, wiring, wellhead | Well yield, pump, wiring, wellhead |

If the pitch involves plastic loops in a trench or a pond, that's a closed-loop system and your well isn't part of the conversation. If it involves pulling water from the ground, you're looking at an open-loop system, and everything below applies.

## How an Open-Loop System Uses Your Well Water

In a typical open-loop setup, a supply well feeds water to the heat pump's heat exchanger. The unit itself doesn't add anything to the water, it just moves heat across a coil. From there the water goes to a return well, sometimes called an injection well, drilled a set distance away. In a standing column design, one well handles both jobs at different depths in the same borehole.

The part that trips people up is scale. A household well pump turns on when pressure drops and shuts off once the tank refills. It runs in short bursts. An open-loop heat pump doesn't work that way. It needs a steady, continuous flow of water for as long as the compressor is running, at a rate set by the unit's size, not by how big your pressure tank is.

## The Water Demand Is Different From a House Tap

This is the piece that gets glossed over in a sales pitch: a geothermal load and a household load are two different kinds of draw on the same aquifer.

Your domestic well and pump are sized around peak household demand, a shower running while the dishwasher fills, that kind of thing, cycling on and off through the day. An open-loop geothermal system asks for a fixed, continuous flow rate for the entire time the heat pump is heating or cooling, on top of whatever the house is using. The correct flow rate comes from the equipment manufacturer's own sizing table for that unit's tonnage, not a rule of thumb, and it's worth having that number in hand before anyone talks about which well will supply it.

If a contractor is proposing to use your existing domestic well for this, ask for a documented [well yield test](/blog/well-yield-test/) result, not an estimate. A well that comfortably supplies a house day to day may or may not have the sustained capacity for a continuous industrial-style draw layered on top. This is also where [pump sizing](/blog/well-pump-sizing/) matters: a pump chosen for household service isn't necessarily right for a continuous geothermal load, and getting that wrong shows up as a pump that runs hot or wears out early. If the answer is a bigger pump, our [well pump cost guide](/well-pump-cost/) has the typical ranges for that kind of replacement.

## Washington's Rules: Registration, Injection Control, and Water Rights

Pulling well water for a heat pump and putting it back in the ground is regulated in Washington on two separate tracks, and both matter. If you want the broader background first, our [primer on water rights and your well](/blog/water-rights-well-washington/) covers the general framework this fits into.

The return side is treated as an injection well under the state's Underground Injection Control program, [chapter 173-218 WAC](https://app.leg.wa.gov/wac/default.aspx?cite=173-218). According to [Ecology's own guidance on heat pump and air conditioning return-flow wells](https://ecology.wa.gov/regulations-permits/guidance-technical-assistance/underground-injection-control-program/heat-pump-air-conditioning-return-flow-wells), an open-loop system's return well has to be registered, and two of the conditions for the rule's automatic authorization are that no chemicals are added to the system and that the system meets water right permitting requirements. A return flow system in hydraulic connection with surface water, or a well that requires a water right permit, doesn't get that automatic pass.

The withdrawal side is a groundwater question. Washington's small-use exemption, [RCW 90.44.050](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=90.44.050), exempts single or group domestic uses up to 5,000 gallons a day, and an industrial purpose up to 5,000 gallons a day, from needing a state water right permit, plus limited allowances for stock water and small lawn irrigation (see our [rundown of Washington's permit-exempt well rules](/blog/permit-exempt-wells-washington/) for how those categories work generally). But Ecology's injection guidance makes meeting water right permitting requirements a condition for an open-loop heat pump system, so the withdrawal side has to be settled either way. Don't assume your household's existing domestic exemption automatically stretches to cover a geothermal load layered on top of it. That's a question for Ecology's regional office, before anything gets drilled.

Closed-loop systems sidestep both of these tracks entirely, since a sealed loop never withdraws groundwater and never discharges into it.

## The Return Well and Why It Matters

The water that goes through the heat pump has to go somewhere, and where it goes is regulated for a reason. A return well accepts the discharge back into the same aquifer it came from. Done right, that keeps the water balance neutral: what comes out goes back in, roughly where it started.

Placement and separation between the supply and return wells are part of the system's design, not an afterthought. A return well set too close to the supply well, or too shallow relative to it, can let warmed or cooled water find its way back to the intake over time. When that happens, the system quietly loses efficiency, and it's not always obvious why until someone checks the wells themselves. If you already have an open-loop system that's been running less efficiently than it used to, that's worth having looked at.

## Water Quality: What an Open-Loop System Is Picky About

An open-loop system runs raw groundwater through a mechanical heat exchanger, over and over, for the life of the equipment. Whatever is in that water matters more here than it does for a household tap.

Iron and manganese are common problems. They can plate out inside the heat exchanger and the return well over years of continuous use, cutting flow and efficiency. Hardness adds scale on top of that. None of this is exotic. It's the same mineral profile that shows up across the basalt aquifers common in this part of the Columbia Plateau, where groundwater tends to run hard and mineral-heavy to begin with. That's exactly the kind of water an open-loop heat exchanger struggles with over time.

We don't sell or install water treatment equipment, that's a separate trade, and any treatment sized for this kind of load should come from a company that specializes in it. But testing the well itself, and being straight about what its water actually contains before it's asked to run through a heat pump for the next fifteen years, is squarely a well question. ## If You Already Have a Well and Someone's Pitching You Open-Loop

Say a geothermal contractor tells you your existing domestic well can double as the supply side for the system. Before you agree to anything, get independent answers on the well, separate from the geothermal sales pitch:

1. What is the well's documented sustained yield, from an actual test, not a guess based on how the house has performed so far.
2. Do the casing, the pump, and the wiring have headroom for a continuous draw on top of normal household use, or does the well need a larger pump, upgraded wire, or a dedicated well of its own.
3. Has anyone actually confirmed with Ecology whether the combined household and geothermal use still fits inside the exempt-use allowance, or whether it now needs a water right permit.
4. Where is the return water going, and is that return well registered the way the state's injection control rule requires.
5. When was the water last tested for iron, manganese, hardness, and pH, and how old is that result.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does an open-loop geothermal well need to be separate from my house well?

Usually, yes. Most residential open-loop systems use a dedicated supply well and a dedicated return well, or one standing column well, kept apart from the household domestic well. That's because a geothermal load needs continuous flow for as long as the heat pump runs, which is a different demand than a house's on-and-off draw, and because Washington regulates the return water as an injection well with its own registration requirement. We don't install the geothermal equipment, but we can evaluate whether an existing well has any realistic capacity to share.

### Can I use my existing domestic well for a geothermal heat pump?

Sometimes, but it depends on documented yield, casing and pump capacity, and how Washington's water right rules apply once you stack a continuous geothermal draw on top of normal household use. Ecology's guidance for open-loop heat pump systems makes meeting water right permitting requirements a condition of the injection rule's authorization, so don't assume your household's small-use exemption automatically covers the added load. Get a real yield test and check with Ecology's regional office before a geothermal contractor drills or connects anything to that well.

### Does Washington require a water right for a geothermal well?

It depends on how much water the system withdraws and returns. State law exempts domestic uses up to 5,000 gallons a day, and an industrial purpose up to 5,000 gallons a day, from needing a water right permit, but Ecology's guidance for open-loop heat pump and air conditioning wells lists meeting water right permitting requirements as a condition of authorization, and the return well needs separate registration under the state's underground injection control rule. Confirm the specifics with Ecology's regional office rather than assuming either exemption covers your setup.

### What water quality problems show up in open-loop geothermal systems?

Iron and manganese are common troublemakers, and both can plate out inside the heat exchanger and the return well over years of continuous use. Hard water adds scale on top of that. This is the same mineral profile common in the hard, alkaline groundwater found across much of this region's basalt aquifers, but an open-loop system runs raw well water through a heat exchanger constantly, so problems tend to show up faster than they would in ordinary household plumbing. Testing for iron, manganese, hardness, and pH before committing catches most of this early.
