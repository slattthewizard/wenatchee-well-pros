# Iron Filter for Well Water: How to Pick the Right System

Your water test came back with iron above where you'd like it, or you already have rust colored rings in the toilet and a metallic taste in your coffee. The obvious next step feels like walking into a plumbing supply store and asking for "an iron filter." The trouble is that phrase covers four different technologies that work in different ways and fail in different situations. Picking the wrong one means a filter that clogs every few days, drains water on a schedule that doesn't match your household, or does nothing at all if what you actually have is iron bacteria instead of dissolved iron.

Where our lane does overlap is the well and pump side: a filter that clogs constantly can be a media problem, or it can be a sign that sand or bacteria is coming from the well itself. ## Ferrous Iron vs. Oxidized Iron: Why the Form Matters

Not all iron in well water behaves the same way, and the form it's in when it reaches your treatment system is the biggest factor in which filter will actually work.

Dissolved, or ferrous, iron starts out invisible. Water comes out of the tap looking clear, then turns orange or brown in the glass or the toilet tank as it sits and reacts with air. [Penn State Extension](https://extension.psu.edu/iron-and-manganese-in-private-water-systems) notes that this dissolved form is most common in groundwater with a pH below 7.0. Oxidized, or ferric, iron is the opposite: it's already rust colored the moment it leaves the faucet, which Penn State attributes to higher pH water or water where oxygen has already reacted with the iron underground.

That distinction matters because some equipment, a plain water softener among them, only works on iron that's still fully dissolved. Feed it iron that's already oxidized into solid particles and it fouls the resin instead of removing anything.

Manganese often shows up alongside iron and follows the same dissolved-versus-oxidized pattern. The EPA sets a secondary standard for both, based on taste and staining rather than health, at [0.3 mg/L for iron and 0.05 mg/L for manganese](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/secondary-drinking-water-standards-guidance-nuisance-chemicals). Manganese gets treated more seriously than that number suggests these days. The [Washington Department of Health](https://doh.wa.gov/sites/default/files/2023-12/331-741.pdf) now recommends that water systems treat manganese above that same 0.05 mg/L level, and at or above its 0.3 mg/L health advisory level it says to use another water source for preparing baby formula, citing research on manganese exposure and neurodevelopment in infants, not just staining. If you're still working out whether what you're looking at is iron, manganese or both, our [iron in well water](/blog/iron-in-well-water/) and [manganese in well water](/blog/manganese-in-well-water/) articles cover the symptoms in more detail. This one is about what to do once you've confirmed it.

## What Your Water Test Should Show Before You Buy Anything

The standard annual well test most homeowners run covers coliform bacteria and nitrate. That's the right baseline for safety, but it tells you nothing about iron, manganese or hardness. Before anyone can size a filter or tell you which technology fits, you need a separate test that includes:

1. Total iron, in mg/L
2. Dissolved (ferrous) iron specifically, if the lab reports it separately from total
3. Manganese, in mg/L
4. pH
5. Hardness (labs often report it in mg/L; divide by 17.1 for grains per gallon)
6. A note on whether the sample was clear or already colored when it was drawn

That last item tells a treatment company whether they're dealing with dissolved or already-oxidized iron. Our [well water testing](/blog/well-water-testing-wenatchee/) article covers where to get a test done locally, and [how to read your results](/blog/well-water-test-results-how-to-read/) walks through the report line by line once it comes back. If that same report flags coliform or nitrate, treat that as a separate, more urgent problem from staining or taste.

## Air Injection (Aeration) Filters

An air injection system, often labeled an AIO tank, pulls in a bubble of air at the top of a sealed tank each time it regenerates. Water passes through that air pocket on its way through, which oxidizes dissolved iron and manganese into solid particles, and a media bed underneath filters those particles out before the water reaches your plumbing. [Nebraska Extension](https://extensionpubs.unl.edu/publication/g1714/na/html/view) lists aeration followed by filtration for combined dissolved iron and manganese up to roughly 5 to 10 mg/L, well above the level it gives for a plain oxidizing filter, and notes it isn't suited to water carrying iron bacteria, which clog the system.

The appeal is that it uses air instead of a chemical, so there's no regeneration solution to buy. The tradeoff is that the air charge has to be maintained, and the media still needs periodic backwashing. A water treatment company sizes the tank and air system to your flow rate and iron level; skipping that sizing step is an easy way to end up with a system that underperforms.

## Manganese Greensand Filters

Manganese greensand is a granular media coated in manganese dioxide. As water passes through, the coating oxidizes dissolved iron, manganese and, in some cases, hydrogen sulfide into particles that get trapped in the bed. The coating is consumed in that reaction, so the system periodically regenerates with a potassium permanganate solution to recharge it, on top of the regular backwash cycle that flushes out trapped sediment.

Greensand has a long track record and handles moderate combined iron and manganese levels well; Penn State's guidance generally recommends greensand when combined iron and manganese falls in the 3 to 10 mg/L range; above 10 mg/L, it points to oxidation with a chemical such as chlorine ahead of filtration. The maintenance load is the real consideration: someone has to keep the permanganate topped up and know when the media has lost capacity, which is usually a service contract with whoever installed the system.

## Catalytic Oxidizing Media and Water Softeners

A newer generation of oxidizing media works as a catalyst rather than a consumable coating. It speeds up the reaction between dissolved oxygen and the iron or manganese in the water so particles form and get trapped in a single pass, without a permanganate regeneration step. These systems still need enough dissolved oxygen in the water to work, which is why some are paired with an aeration stage ahead of the media bed, and they still need backwashing on a schedule set by iron load and household water use. They can be picky, too: Penn State's guidance for one common catalytic media calls for dissolved oxygen in the raw water, a pH of at least 6.8 for iron removal, and dissolved iron under 1.0 mg/L. With no permanganate step, there's less to buy on a recurring basis. Whether that tradeoff makes sense depends on iron level, water use and how much hands-on maintenance a household wants to take on, which is a conversation for whoever sizes and installs the system.

A standard ion exchange softener can pull out a limited amount of dissolved iron along with the calcium and magnesium it's designed for, which is why some homes with mild iron never install anything else. Penn State's guidance puts the rough ceiling at 5 mg/L of dissolved iron, water pH above 6.7, and hardness in the 3 to 20 grains per gallon range.

Push past those numbers, or hand a softener iron that's already oxidized into solid particles, and it stops working the way you want. The resin fouls, capacity drops, and the softener starts using more salt for less result. If your test shows iron near or above that ceiling, or the water is already rust colored coming out of the tap, a softener alone isn't the answer regardless of what a display model at the hardware store suggests.

## Iron Bacteria: The Complication That Changes the Plan

None of the filters above fix iron bacteria, and none of them run well with it in the system. There are no drinking water standards for iron bacteria, and [Penn State Extension](https://extension.psu.edu/iron-and-sulfur-bacteria-a-slimy-problem) treats them as more of a nuisance than a health hazard, but it lists clogged well systems among the problems they cause. The bacteria feed on dissolved iron and manganese and leave behind a reddish brown or orange slime as a byproduct. That slime coats the inside of the casing, the pump, the pressure tank and eventually the treatment media itself, which is why a brand new filter can clog every few days when bacteria is the real problem instead of iron concentration alone.

Penn State notes that iron bacteria are often introduced into wells during construction or maintenance, and that once established they can be impossible to remove entirely, because the slime shields them from chlorine. Shock chlorination, run through the plumbing with the pump pushing chlorinated water out every fixture in the house, knocks the population back, and if a well needs shocking more often than every six months, Penn State treats that as a sign other treatment is needed. ## Comparing the Options at a Glance

| Filter type | How it treats iron | Needs a chemical? | Typical maintenance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air injection (AIO) | Oxidizes with an air charge, then filters the particles out | No | Backwash cycle, air charge upkeep |
| Manganese greensand | Oxidizes on a treated media bed, then filters | Yes, potassium permanganate | Backwash plus periodic permanganate regeneration |
| Catalytic oxidizing media | Speeds oxidation at the media surface, then filters | No | Backwash on a schedule; media eventually replaced |
| Water softener | Exchanges dissolved iron ions along with hardness minerals | Salt, not permanganate | Regular salt refill; limited to fully dissolved, low iron |

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I install an iron filter myself?

Iron filters connect into your main water line and usually need a bypass valve, a drain line for backwash water and, for greensand or similar systems, a way to refill the regeneration chemical. It's plumbing work, not something to improvise from a general how-to video, and getting the sizing wrong, tank diameter, media volume or backwash flow, is a common reason a new system underperforms. A local water treatment company handles sizing, installation and the ongoing service contract. Our part stays limited to the well, pump, tank and wellhead upstream of wherever the treatment system gets installed.

### Will an iron filter also get rid of a rotten egg smell?

Not reliably. A rotten egg smell is almost always hydrogen sulfide gas, a different problem from iron even though both can show up in the same well. Some oxidizing systems, greensand among them, can handle low levels of it, but only if the system was sized for it. If the smell only appears in hot water and not cold, it's usually the water heater's anode rod reacting with sulfate rather than the well itself. Our [well water smells like rotten eggs](/blog/well-water-smells-rotten-eggs/) article covers the sulfur side. An iron filter targets iron and manganese; heavier sulfur usually needs its own treatment step.

### How can I tell if I have iron bacteria instead of just high iron?

Look for a reddish brown, orange or sometimes gray slime in the toilet tank, on the underside of the tank lid, or coating the inside of an aerator screen, often with a swampy or musty smell. A high iron reading alone doesn't tell you this; a lab can run a specific iron bacteria test if you ask for it. If a new filter is clogging every few days rather than every few weeks, that pattern can point toward bacteria rather than concentration, and dealing with it starts at the well, not the filter media.

### Is iron in well water actually a health risk?

Iron itself is classified by EPA as a secondary contaminant, meaning the concern is taste, staining and appearance rather than a health limit, and that holds at the levels most private wells produce. Manganese is different. Washington's Department of Health has moved away from treating it as only a cosmetic issue, since research now links elevated manganese exposure to developmental effects in infants and young children. Above 0.05 mg/L, treatment makes sense for the same staining reasons as iron. At or above 0.3 mg/L, DOH's health advisory level, it says to use another water source for baby formula.
