## How a Frost-Free Yard Hydrant Works: The Plunger, Rod, and Bury Depth

A frost-free (or "frost-proof") yard hydrant looks like a simple standpipe with a handle, but the part that actually stops the water is nowhere near the handle. It's a valve seat and plunger sitting at the bottom of the unit, buried below the frost line, sometimes three or four feet down depending on the model and how deep the installer set it.

When you lift the handle, a long steel rod running down the inside of the pipe lifts a rubber-tipped plunger off that buried seat, and water rises up through the riser to the spout. Push the handle down, and the same rod reseats the plunger against the valve, cutting off flow at the bottom, not the top. That's the whole trick. The shut-off point sits below ground where the soil temperature never drops near freezing, so the valve itself doesn't have a freezing problem.

The riser pipe above the buried valve is a different story. It's exposed to outside air all winter, and once the valve closes, every drop of water sitting inside that riser needs to drain back out or it's sitting there waiting for the next cold snap. That's what the weep hole is for, and it's the part almost nobody thinks about until something goes wrong.

## The Weep Hole: Why a Hydrant That Holds Water Freezes

Down near the buried valve, most frost-free hydrants have a small weep hole drilled into the side of the housing. When the plunger seats and shuts off flow from the well side, that same motion opens the weep hole, and gravity pulls the standing water out of the riser into a gravel pocket or drainage sump around the base.

Here's the catch. For that water to drain, air has to be able to get in at the top. A yard hydrant sheds its water the same way a straw stops dripping when you cap the top with your finger, except backward, block the air path at the spout and the water in the riser can't fall, weep hole or not.

Leave a hose screwed onto the spout, even with the hydrant switched off, and you've sealed that air path. Install a backflow preventer or vacuum breaker at the head that isn't a drainable type, and you've done the same thing. Either way, the riser stays full of water all winter, right where the outside temperature can reach it, and a hydrant that's supposed to be frost-free freezes exactly like an ordinary pipe would.

A clogged or silted-in weep hole causes the identical problem from the other direction. Dirt settles into the drainage pocket over years, the hole itself packs with sediment, and water that should drain in seconds just sits there instead. Either failure produces the same result, a hydrant that holds water and splits open underground on the coldest night of the year, usually where you won't notice until the ground thaws in spring.

Backflow prevention isn't just about freeze protection either. The EPA's [private well owner resources](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells) cover why keeping a proper air gap or drainable device at any outdoor spigot also protects your well from contamination, not just from ice.

## Why It Won't Shut Off: Worn Plungers, Seals, and Rod Misalignment

A hydrant that keeps dripping after you've shut it off almost always comes down to the buried valve itself. The rubber washer or seal on the plunger wears out over years of use, mineral buildup pits the metal seat it presses against, or the connecting rod slips out of adjustment so the plunger never fully seats even though the handle looks like it's all the way down.

Roy Tillman grows cherries and apples on a family orchard outside Quincy, and one of his yard hydrants had been dribbling for weeks by the time he called us. He'd assumed it was just old and past saving. It turned out the plunger washer had perished, a common failure on a hydrant that's seen fifteen-plus seasons of hard water. We pulled the stem, replaced the rebuild kit, and had it sealing tight again for $310, parts and labor included.

A hydrant that won't fully shut off wastes real water over a season, an issue any orchard operation running drip lines or micro-sprinklers off the same supply feels directly. It also means the riser above the valve never fully drains, since water is still trickling up through a valve that hasn't sealed, which sets up the exact freeze risk described above even on a hydrant that was working fine the year before.

## Why It Won't Drain: Hoses, Backflow Devices, and Weep Hole Clogs

The second failure mode is the opposite complaint. The handle shuts off cleanly, water stops at the spout, but the riser stays full because nothing is draining out the bottom.

The Voss family runs cattle and a few horses on their place outside Moses Lake, with a yard hydrant feeding a float-valve stock tank through a hose left connected most of the year for convenience. That hose blocked the air path at the spout all winter without anyone realizing it. The riser froze solid in a January cold snap, and the housing split about two feet underground, invisible until spring thaw brought a wet, muddy patch to the surface. By the time we dug it up, the whole unit needed replacing rather than a simple rebuild, which ran $980 including the excavation.

Denise Okafor in Cashmere ran into the same drainage failure from a different cause. She'd had a backflow preventer added to her yard hydrant to keep well water from siphoning back into the supply line, good instinct, wrong device. The one installed wasn't a drainable type built for freeze climates, so it sealed the top just like a forgotten hose would. Her hydrant cracked two winters in a row before we diagnosed the real cause and swapped in a code-compliant, drainable backflow assembly along with a fresh plunger kit, for $340.

Both cases point to the same lesson. A frost-free hydrant only works if the whole system, valve, rod, weep hole, and whatever's attached to the spout, stays open to drain every single time it's shut off.

## When to Call a Professional Instead of Digging It Up Yourself

Adjusting a handle linkage or swapping a hose-end backflow device is reasonable DIY territory. A few situations call for a professional instead:

- **The hydrant won't seal no matter what you try at the handle.** That usually means the buried valve seat is corroded or damaged, and diagnosing it means pulling the stem, which takes the right tools to do without breaking the housing.
- **You suspect a freeze-split riser underground.** Digging blind around a buried water line risks damaging the line further or hitting something else buried nearby, like a septic line or electrical conduit, especially on older rural properties without clear as-built records.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why does my yard hydrant keep dripping even when it's shut off?

A dripping hydrant almost always means the plunger seal or valve seat at the bottom of the unit, buried below the frost line, isn't sealing tight anymore. Years of use wear down the rubber washer, mineral deposits pit the metal seat, or the connecting rod slips out of adjustment so the plunger never fully closes. A rebuild kit typically fixes this for $200 to $450.

### Why does my yard hydrant freeze every winter even though it's a "frost-free" model?

The freeze-proof design only works if the riser pipe fully drains through the weep hole after every use. A hose left attached, a non-drainable backflow device at the spout, or a silted-in weep hole all block that drainage, leaving water sitting in the exposed riser where it freezes just like ordinary pipe. The "frost-free" part refers to the buried valve, not the whole hydrant automatically.

### Can I leave a hose attached to a frost-free yard hydrant?

Not through freezing weather, unless the hydrant has a specific drainable backflow assembly rated for a permanent connection. A hose left on the spout blocks the air path the riser needs to drain, which is exactly how stock tank hydrants on ranch properties end up freezing and splitting underground every winter. Disconnect hoses before the first hard freeze or have a proper drainable fitting installed.

### How deep does a frost-free yard hydrant need to be buried in the Wenatchee area?

Frost in North Central Washington typically penetrates about 24 inches, and hydrants sold for this climate are usually rated for a 3 to 4 foot bury depth on the buried valve, well below that line. If your hydrant is an older or shallower installation, or you're not sure how deep it was set, that's worth confirming before you count on it through a hard winter.
