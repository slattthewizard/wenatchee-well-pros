# Well Pressure Gauge: How to Read It and When to Replace It

If you've ever crouched next to the pressure tank with a flashlight trying to make sense of a small dial, you already know the well pressure gauge is the one part of the whole system that's actually trying to talk to you. Everything else, the switch, the tank, the pump down the well, works silently until something fails. The gauge is the readout. Learning to read it well takes a few minutes, and it can save you a service call or tell you exactly when you need one.

This is a plain look at what the gauge measures, where it lives, how to tell a failing gauge from a real pressure problem, and what's involved in swapping it out.

## What the Gauge on Your Pressure Tank Actually Tells You

The well pressure gauge reads the water pressure inside your plumbing system at the point where it's mounted, almost always right at the pressure tank. It doesn't measure how much water is in the tank, how deep your well is, or how the pump is doing. It measures one thing: pounds per square inch (psi) of pressure in the pipe at that moment.

That single number tells a bigger story because your system is built to swing between two set points. The pump turns on at a low pressure (cut-in) and shuts off at a high pressure (cut-out), and the tank stores the difference. Every time you watch the needle climb and fall through a full cycle, you're watching the pressure switch and pressure tank do their job. A gauge that won't move, won't settle, or reads a number that doesn't match what's coming out of the tap is telling you something is off, either with the gauge itself or with the system behind it.

## Where the Gauge Sits, and What Else Shares the Tee

Almost every residential well system has a cluster of fittings screwed into a single point near the top of the pressure tank, usually called the tank tee. On a typical tee you'll find the pressure gauge, the pressure switch, a drain or boiler-drain valve, and sometimes a union or shutoff valve. The gauge is usually a small brass-bodied dial threaded into a 1/4 inch NPT port, and it's often the cheapest single part on the whole tee.

That location matters for two reasons. First, it means the gauge is exposed to the same freeze risk as everything else at the tee. An unheated pump house or an uninsulated crawlspace corner can crack a gauge's internal tube over a cold winter even if the rest of the system survives, which is one more reason [insulating the space around your pressure tank and pump gear](/blog/pump-house-insulation/) pays off in North Central Washington's colder months. Second, it means the gauge sits within reach of live wiring, often 240 volt, at the pressure switch terminals. You can look at a gauge and even unscrew it without touching electricity, but don't assume the whole tee is safe to poke around in. If a job puts your hands near the switch's terminals, shut off the breaker to the pump first and confirm it's dead with a meter before you touch anything inside that switch cover.

## Reading Cut-In and Cut-Out Pressure

Cut-in is the low number, the pressure at which the switch tells the pump to start. Cut-out is the high number, where the switch tells the pump to stop. You'll usually see these written as a pair, like 30/50, and that pair is what the pressure switch is set to, not something the gauge decides on its own. The gauge just shows you where the needle sits relative to those two points as the system cycles.

Washington's Department of Health describes the typical cycle plainly: the pump starts, water fills the tank and compresses the air inside it, and the pump stops when the system reaches the switch's cut-off setting. The same tech tip notes that [common pressure ranges are 30 to 50 or 40 to 60 psi](https://doh.wa.gov/sites/default/files/legacy/Documents/Pubs/331-342.pdf). Older or smaller systems sometimes run a lower pair. Penn State Extension's rundown of low-yield well systems, for instance, describes a tank running to about 40 psi before the switch stops the pump and back down to about 20 psi before it restarts, as one common example of [how a pressure tank and switch work together](https://extension.psu.edu/using-low-yielding-wells).

| Setting pair | Cut-in (pump starts) | Cut-out (pump stops) | Where you'll typically see it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20/40 | 20 psi | 40 psi | Older or smaller residential systems |
| 30/50 | 30 psi | 50 psi | A very common residential setting |
| 40/60 | 40 psi | 60 psi | Multi-story homes, long pipe runs, higher-demand fixtures |

None of these pairs is objectively "right." What matters is that your gauge consistently tracks the pair your switch is actually set to. If you're not sure what your switch is set to, or you want to change it, that's a separate job with its own steps: see [how to adjust a well pump pressure switch](/blog/well-pump-pressure-switch-adjustment/) rather than guessing at the dial.

## Well Pressure Gauge Reads Zero: What It Usually Means

A gauge sitting at zero is one of the more alarming things to see, and it can mean two very different things. Either there's genuinely no pressure in the system, or the gauge itself has failed and is stuck at zero while real pressure sits behind it.

Start with the easy checks. Is the breaker to the pump on? Has the power been out? Is the pressure switch's low-pressure cutoff lever tripped (a real safety feature, not a fault, and one the switch adjustment guide linked above mentions)? If you flip a faucet and get nothing at all, and the gauge reads zero, that's consistent with a genuinely dead system: no water, no pressure, somewhere between the well and the tank. That's a real problem and usually not one to chase yourself. If your taps went dry all of a sudden, [our guide to sudden no-water situations](/blog/no-water-suddenly/) walks through the order to check things in.

But if water is still coming out of the tap, even if it sputters or the pressure feels weak, while the gauge reads a flat zero, the gauge is very likely the thing that's broken, not your water supply. A stuck needle, a corroded internal tube, or a cracked lens that let moisture in can all pin a gauge at zero regardless of the real pressure in the pipe.

## Well Pressure Gauge Not Working: Bad Gauge or Real Problem?

Beyond a flat zero, a gauge can misbehave in quieter ways: a needle that jumps in sudden steps instead of sliding smoothly, one that never quite reaches the switch's known cut-out number, or one that reads noticeably different from what a fixture's actual flow suggests. Here's a short checklist for sorting a bad gauge from a real system issue before you call anyone or buy a part.

1. **Watch a full cycle.** Run water until the pump kicks on, then let it fill until the pump stops. A healthy gauge sweeps smoothly between roughly your cut-in and cut-out numbers. A gauge that's frozen, jumpy, or wildly off from those numbers is suspect.
2. **Tap the glass gently.** A needle that jumps when you tap the lens with a fingernail has a sticking mechanism, not a real pressure swing.
3. **Look for moisture or fogging** under the lens, a bent needle, or a case that's visibly corroded or cracked. Any of those points to the gauge, not the system.
4. **Rule out the tank first.** A waterlogged pressure tank (the internal bladder has failed) can cause rapid, erratic cycling that looks like a bad gauge but isn't. If the tank sounds solid when tapped or cycles the pump every few seconds, check the tank itself. Our [waterlogged pressure tank guide](/blog/pressure-tank-waterlogged/) covers the signs.
5. **Check precharge before you blame the gauge.** A tank with the wrong air precharge can cause short, rapid cycling that mimics a gauge problem. [Checking and setting pressure tank air pressure](/blog/pressure-tank-air-pressure/) is a separate five-minute job worth ruling out.

If your pump is short cycling, turning on and off far more often than it should, that's its own pattern with several possible causes beyond the gauge. [Our breakdown of well pump short cycling](/blog/well-pump-short-cycling/) covers the full list.

## A Simple Way to Test a Gauge You Don't Trust

The most reliable homeowner-level test is a comparison, not a guess. An inexpensive water pressure test gauge that threads onto a hose bib or washing machine outlet lets you check your tank gauge's reading against a known point in the same system at the same moment.

Run the system through a normal cycle with both gauges in place, one at the tank tee and one at a hose bib elsewhere in the house. They won't read identically to the decimal, plumbing has some pressure drop over distance and elevation, but they should track each other and swing through roughly the same range at the same times. If the tank gauge sits flat while the test gauge swings normally, or the two disagree by a wide and consistent margin at every point in the cycle, the tank gauge is the problem.

This test also protects you from an expensive mistake: replacing a pressure switch or hunting for a leak based on a reading that was never accurate in the first place.

## Replacing a Well Pressure Gauge

A well pressure gauge itself is a simple, inexpensive part, and swapping one is one of the more approachable jobs at the tank tee for a handy homeowner, with a few things done in order.

1. Shut off the breaker that powers the well pump. This isn't strictly an electrical job, but the gauge sits inches from live pressure switch terminals, and killing the power removes any chance of the pump kicking on while you're working at the tee.
2. Open a faucet somewhere in the house and let the system drain down to zero pressure. Don't try to unscrew a gauge against live system pressure.
3. Unscrew the old gauge from its port. Most are standard 1/4 inch NPT and come out with a wrench on the gauge's hex fitting, not the dial housing.
4. Wrap the new gauge's threads with pipe thread tape or a thread sealant rated for potable water, then thread it in by hand before snugging with a wrench. Don't overtighten; brass threads strip easily.
5. Close the faucet, restore power, and watch the gauge through a full cycle to confirm it tracks your switch's cut-in and cut-out points.

## When to Stop and Call a Well Pump Company

A bad gauge on its own is a low-stakes problem: annoying, but not urgent, and cheap to fix once you've confirmed it's the gauge and not the system. The EPA's guidance for private well owners recommends [periodically inspecting the exposed parts of your well](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/protect-your-homes-water), such as the casing, cap and surface seal, and a glance at the gauge fits easily into that same walk-around. A few situations are worth calling in help for instead of working through it alone:

- The pump is short cycling rapidly (multiple starts per minute) regardless of what the gauge shows. That pattern is hard on the motor and the switch contacts, and it gets worse the longer it runs.
- You've swapped the gauge, confirmed it's accurate against a second gauge, and the readings still don't match a healthy cut-in/cut-out cycle.
- Any work would require opening the pressure switch cover or wiring, and you're not confident it's fully de-energized. Verify dead with a meter, or don't do that part yourself.
- The pump needs to be pulled to diagnose or replace, which isn't a homeowner job on a deep well.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Where is the well pressure gauge located?

It's almost always mounted directly on the pressure tank tee, the cluster of fittings near the top of the pressure tank where the pressure switch, a drain valve, and often a union all connect. It threads into a small port, usually 1/4 inch NPT, right on that manifold. If your tank and switch are in a pump house, basement, or crawlspace, the gauge is right there with them rather than somewhere else in the plumbing run.

### What should a normal well pressure gauge reading be?

There's no single correct number. What matters is that the gauge tracks your system's actual cut-in and cut-out settings, commonly 20/40, 30/50, or 40/60 psi depending on the setup. Watch the needle through a full pump cycle: it should climb smoothly from the low number to the high number, hold near the high number while water isn't being used, then fall back down and restart the pump near the low number, over and over.

### Why does my well pressure gauge read zero but the pump runs?

If the pump runs and water still comes out of the tap, even weakly, while the gauge sits flat at zero, the gauge itself has almost certainly failed rather than your water pressure actually being gone. A stuck needle, a corroded internal mechanism, or moisture inside the case can all pin a gauge at zero. Confirm it with a second gauge at another fixture before assuming anything else is wrong, since a genuinely pressureless system usually means no water at the tap at all.

### Can I replace a well pressure gauge myself?

Yes, for most homeowners this is a manageable job. Shut off the breaker to the pump, drain the system to zero pressure at a faucet, unscrew the old gauge from the tank tee, tape the new gauge's threads, and thread it in hand-tight plus a light turn with a wrench. The main risk is working carelessly near the nearby pressure switch wiring; if you need to open that switch's cover for any reason, verify it's de-energized with a meter first.
