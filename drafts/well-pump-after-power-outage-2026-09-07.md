# Well Pump Not Working After a Power Outage? Restart It in This Order

The lights came back on at 4 a.m. The fridge started humming, the furnace kicked in, and the shower still ran dry. That is the call we take most often the morning after a windstorm rolls through the valley.

Here is the good news. A well pump not working after a power outage is usually not a dead pump. Nine times out of ten it is a tripped breaker, a switch that did not close again, or air sitting in the line. Those are cheap problems, and some of them you can clear yourself in ten minutes with a flashlight.

The bad news is that the wrong move at the panel can turn a $200 fix into a $3,000 one. Resetting a breaker again and again while a motor sits stalled is one of the fastest ways to cook a healthy pump.

So work through it in order. Below is that order, what the outage most likely damaged, and the point where you stop and call someone with a clamp meter and a pump hoist.

## Why Your Well Pump Is the Last Thing to Come Back

Everything else in your house is designed to ride out a power blip. Your well pump is not.

A submersible pump is an electric motor hanging on the end of a wire, often 100 to 400 feet down a steel casing. It draws three to five times its running current for the first half second of every start. When utility power comes back, it rarely comes back clean. There are surges, dips, and sometimes two or three quick on-off cycles while crews switch circuits back in.

That is the moment things break. A motor hit with low voltage sits there straining instead of spinning, and heat builds in the windings fast. A tired control box capacitor picks that moment to quit. Contacts inside a pressure switch can arc and weld shut, or pit badly enough that they never close again.

The pump itself, the part that costs real money, is usually fine. What fails is the small stuff around it, and that is why the order you work in matters.

## Restart Your Well Pump After an Outage in This Order

Do these in sequence. Do not skip ahead, and do not repeat step four more than twice.

**1. Confirm the power is actually back and stable.**
Check a lamp and an outlet, not just the porch light. If the utility is still switching circuits, you may have partial power or low voltage, which is worse for a pump motor than no power at all. Check your PUD outage map first, and if the neighbors are still dark, wait.

**2. Turn the pump breaker off, then look for a second disconnect.**
Kill the well pump breaker at the main panel first so the motor is not trying to start while you work. Then go find the other one. Most rural properties around here have a second shutoff at the pump house, in a small subpanel, or in a weatherproof box near the wellhead. Fused disconnects on older places blow a fuse and look completely normal from three feet away.

**3. Look at the pressure gauge before you power anything up.**
Note the reading. Zero means the system is empty and the pump has not run. Something like 40 or 50 psi means the tank still holds pressure, and your no-water problem sits between the tank and the tap rather than down the well.

**4. Bring the breaker back on, once, and listen.**
Switch it fully off, then firmly on. Stand near the pressure tank and listen for the pump starting and the gauge climbing. If the breaker trips immediately, you are done. Do not reset it a third time. A breaker that trips on a well circuit is doing its job, and there is a real reason behind it, usually a shorted motor, damaged wire, or water in a splice. Our guide on a [well pump tripping the breaker](/blog/well-pump-tripping-breaker/) walks through what each pattern means.

**5. If the pump hums but does not run, shut it off within about 30 seconds.**
A hum with no pressure rise means the motor is getting power but cannot turn. Every second it hums, it is heating up. Turn it off and read [what a humming well pump means](/blog/well-pump-humming-not-running/) before you try again.

**6. Give the pressure switch a nudge, gently.**
On many systems the switch sits on the tank tee with a small lever or contact arm under the gray cover. If the contacts have stuck open, lifting that lever by hand while the breaker is on can pull them closed and get the pump running. Do this with a dry hand, from the side, standing on dry ground, and only if you can reach it without touching wiring. There is 240 volts inside that cover. If that idea makes you uneasy, it should. Call instead.

**7. Open the lowest tap in the house and let it run.**
Once the gauge is climbing again, expect spitting, coughing and cloudy water for a minute or two. That is air, and it is normal after a system loses pressure. Run a bathtub spout or an outside hydrant rather than a faucet with an aerator screen, so any sediment has somewhere to go. If the sputtering never settles, see [air in water lines](/blog/air-in-water-lines/).

**8. Check the water again in an hour.**
Pressure that climbs and then bleeds away over the next 30 minutes points at a check valve, a leak, or a waterlogged tank rather than the outage itself.

## What the Outage Actually Damaged: Three Common Patterns

**The manufactured home outside East Wenatchee.** A 1990s place on a 220-foot well, three days without power in a January ice storm. When power returned, nothing happened. The breaker held fine, so the motor was not the problem. The pressure switch contacts had pitted badly and would not close under load. A pressure switch replacement runs **$150 to $350**, and that pump had another decade in it.

**The orchard house above Cashmere.** Submersible pump, 44-gallon bladder tank, and power that flickered back on and off four times while crews worked the line. The pump started, hummed for a few seconds, then quit. The start capacitor in the control box had failed, which is exactly what a run of hard restarts does to a component that has been in service twelve years. Capacitor or control box work runs **$200 to $450**. Three-wire systems have that part, two-wire systems do not, which is one of several differences covered in [two-wire versus three-wire pumps](/blog/two-wire-vs-three-wire-well-pump/).

**The cabin near Leavenworth.** Left unheated over Christmas, power out four days in a cold snap. Power came back, the pump ran, the gauge climbed, and not a drop reached the sink. The line between the pitless adapter and the cabin had frozen where it ran shallow under a crawlspace. Nothing was wrong with the pump at all. That one was a **$150 to $250** diagnostic call plus the after-hours premium of **$150 to $300**, because it was a Sunday.

Two of those three were bracing for a replacement quote and did not need one. That is the usual outcome, and it is why the order above starts with the cheap checks.

## When to Stop Resetting and Call Someone

Some of this is homeowner work. Some of it is not, and the line is clearer than most people think.

Stop and call if the breaker trips more than twice, if the pump hums without running, if you smell anything burnt at the panel or control box, if there is standing water anywhere near the wiring, or if the wellhead itself was flooded during the storm. A [flooded wellhead](/blog/flooded-wellhead-spring-runoff/) is a water quality problem as well as an electrical one, and the well should be tested before you drink from it again.

Also stop if you find yourself about to open a control box or a pressure switch cover with the power on. Those enclosures carry 240 volts, and no version of that job is worth a shock in a dark pump house.

Anything below the pitless adapter needs specialist equipment, full stop. Pulling a submersible from a 300-foot well means a hoist, a pipe reel, and somewhere safe to lay out several hundred feet of pipe. It is not a rope-and-two-neighbors job, and people get hurt trying. A pressure tank that lost its air charge months ago makes the pump start and stop far more often than it should, and a motor doing hundreds of cycles a day is much more fragile when the grid hiccups. ## Making the Next Outage a Smaller Problem

Outages here are seasonal: wind and wet snow in the fall, ice in January, the odd summer heat event.

The cheapest insurance is stored water. Fifty gallons in food-grade drums covers toilets, dishes and stock for a couple of days, and it costs almost nothing. The EPA's [private well guidance](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells) is a sensible starting point for storage and post-emergency testing, and the [Washington State Department of Health drinking water pages](https://doh.wa.gov/community-and-environment/drinking-water) cover testing after flooding, which matters if your wellhead went underwater during the same storm.

The next step up is a generator, and this is where people get it wrong. Size it for starting current, not running current, and never backfeed through a dryer outlet. That practice can energize a line a utility crew believes is dead. A proper transfer switch or panel interlock is electrical work for a qualified electrician, and it needs to be permitted. We cover the numbers in [choosing a generator for a well pump](/blog/generator-for-well-pump/) and the wider planning in [a power outage water backup plan](/blog/power-outage-well-water-backup-plan/).

If your place loses power more than a couple of times a year, ask about the condition of your control box and pressure switch at the next service visit. Those are the parts outages kill, and they are the cheap parts. Replacing a twelve-year-old switch in October beats finding out about it on a Sunday in January.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long should I wait after the power comes back before resetting the pump?

Give it about 15 minutes if you can. Utility crews often switch circuits in and out while they finish a repair, and starting a pump motor into unstable voltage is harder on it than leaving it off a while longer. Once the lights are steady and the neighbors are back on, you are clear to reset.

### Can a power outage actually damage a well pump?

The outage itself does no harm. The damage happens when power returns, in the form of voltage surges, low-voltage starts and repeated on-off cycling. The motor is usually the survivor. Pressure switches, capacitors and control boxes are the parts that tend to fail, and those are the cheaper repairs.

### Why is my water cloudy or spitting after the power comes back?

Air got into the system when it lost pressure, and it has to work its way out. Run a tub spout or an outside hydrant for two or three minutes rather than a faucet with a screen on it. If it is still spitting an hour later, or the water carries grit, something else is going on and it is worth a call.

### My breaker trips every time I reset it. What does that mean?

It means there is a fault on the circuit, most often a shorted motor, a damaged wire, or water in a splice down the well. The breaker is doing exactly what it was installed to do. Stop resetting it, leave it off, and call for a diagnosis, because repeated resets into a fault can turn a wire repair into a pump replacement.
