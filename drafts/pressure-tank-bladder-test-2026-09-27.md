# How to Check Your Pressure Tank Bladder at the Schrader Valve

Your pump is cycling on and off faster than it used to, or the pressure at the kitchen faucet swings from strong to weak in the space of a few seconds. Before you assume the pump itself is dying, there's a simpler part worth checking first: the rubber bladder inside your pressure tank. It's a five-minute test with a tire gauge, and it tells you almost everything you need to know about whether the tank is still doing its job.

## What the Bladder Actually Does

Inside a modern well tank there's a rubber diaphragm or bladder that separates a cushion of compressed air from your water. The air side gets pre-pressurized at the factory, and that air cushion is what absorbs the pump's output between cycles, so the pump doesn't have to switch on every time you turn on a tap. Tanks are made in both diaphragm and bladder designs, and the same basic principle applies across brands: air on one side, water on the other, with a flexible membrane keeping them apart.

That air charge has to be set correctly for the tank to work at all. The standard rule, confirmed on more than one tank manufacturer's own support page, is that [the precharge should sit 2 psi below your pressure switch's cut-in setting](https://www.amtrol.com/welltanksupport/). If your switch is set to turn the pump on at 40 psi, the tank should hold 38 psi of air when there's no water pressure on it. Get that number wrong, or lose it entirely because the bladder failed, and the tank stops buffering anything.

## Signs to Check the Bladder, Not Something Else

A few symptoms point specifically at the tank rather than the pump, the switch, or the wiring:

- The pump kicks on and off in a matter of seconds instead of running for a minute or more at a time. This is classic [short cycling](/blog/well-pump-short-cycling/), and a bad tank is one of the most common causes.
- The tank feels heavy for its size and sloshes like it's mostly full of water, with little or no give when you knock on the shell.
- Water pressure surges hard right after the pump kicks on, then fades quickly, instead of holding steady between cycles.
- You've already ruled out [the usual signs of a waterlogged tank](/blog/pressure-tank-waterlogged/) and want to confirm the bladder itself, not just top off the air.

None of these symptoms prove the bladder failed on their own. The gauge test is what actually tells you.

## Shut the System Down Before You Test

Do this before you touch the air valve:

1. Turn off the breaker to the well pump at the panel. You don't want the pump kicking on mid-test.
2. Open a hose bib or a faucet somewhere in the house and let it run until the tank's pressure gauge drops to 0 and water stops flowing. The system needs to be fully depressurized, not just "off."
3. Leave that faucet cracked open while you work, so pressure can't build back up behind you.

If your panel is unlabeled, shared with other circuits, or you're not confident which breaker is the pump's, stop here and call **[(509) 300-5151](tel:+15093005151)** instead of guessing at a live panel. The bladder test itself is simple; finding the right breaker safely is not something to rush.

## The Tire Gauge Test, Step by Step

Once the system is drained and the pump is off, the test itself takes a few minutes:

1. Find the air valve on the tank. It looks exactly like the valve stem on a car tire, usually on top of the tank or on the side near the top.
2. Wipe the valve dry with a rag first. A wet valve from condensation can make you second-guess a good reading.
3. Press a standard tire pressure gauge straight onto the valve, the same way you'd check a car tire.
4. Read the number.
5. Compare it to what it should be: 2 psi below your pressure switch's cut-in setting (see the table below).
6. Pay attention to what comes out when you press the gauge on, not just the number. A short hiss of dry air is normal. Water spitting or dribbling out of the valve is not.
7. If the reading is low but the air is clean and dry, add air with a bike pump, a small compressor, or a hand pump to bring it up to the correct number, then recheck it in a day to see if it holds.
8. Close the faucet you opened, turn the breaker back on, and watch the gauge climb as the pump refills the tank and shuts off at its cut-out pressure.

## What the Number Should Be

Pressure switch settings vary by home, so the "right" precharge number depends on your cut-in pressure, not on a single universal figure. This table applies the 2 psi rule [published by Amtrol for its Well-X-Trol tanks](https://www.amtrol.com/welltanksupport/) to the common switch settings:

| Cut-In (psi) | Cut-Out (psi) | Tank Precharge (psi) |
|---|---|---|
| 20 | 40 | 18 |
| 30 | 50 | 28 |
| 40 | 60 | 38 |
| 50 | 70 | 48 |
| 60 | 80 | 58 |

[Flexcon lists the same 2-psi-below-cut-in rule](https://www.flexconind.com/importance-of-checking-pre-charge/) on its own tanks, with the identical numbers for 20/40, 30/50, and 40/60 systems, so this isn't a brand-specific quirk. The same Flexcon page notes that its tanks ship with a standard 38 psi precharge. That suits a 40/60 switch but is 10 psi too high for a 30/50 one, so a tank that was never adjusted when it went in can be off from day one, with nothing wrong with the bladder at all. If you're not sure what your switch is set to, that's a separate check. We've written up [the full walkthrough on reading and setting tank air pressure](/blog/pressure-tank-air-pressure/) if you want to confirm the switch settings before you trust the precharge number.

## If Water Comes Out Instead of Air

This is the result that actually settles the question. If you press the gauge onto the valve and water comes out instead of air, even a small dribble, the bladder or diaphragm has ruptured. Water has crossed from the water side of the tank into the air side and is sitting in the space where the air charge should be. There is no way to "recharge" your way out of that. Adding air to a tank with a ruptured bladder just pushes air into a space that's already full of water, or the air leaks straight back out through the same tear that let the water in.

Amtrol's own support page lists rapid pump cycling and low system pressure as the symptoms of a tank that needs replacing, and says a tank with low or no air may need to be replaced. A tank in this state will keep short-cycling your pump no matter how you adjust the switch, because there's no working air cushion left to buffer anything. This is also, functionally, [the tank version of being waterlogged](/blog/pressure-tank-waterlogged/): all water, no air, and no fix short of replacing it. If this is what you found, [get a free estimate](/#contact) rather than keep chasing the pressure switch or the pump for a problem that's actually sitting in the tank.

## Why You Usually Can't Just Replace the Bladder

A reasonable question once you know the bladder failed: can you just swap the bladder and keep the tank? On the residential well tanks in most homes here, the answer is almost always no. Diaphragm and bladder-style residential tanks are built as one sealed unit, welded or crimped shut at the factory, with no service opening designed for pulling the membrane out and putting a new one in.

Tanks that genuinely have a replaceable bladder exist, but they're a different product built for a different market. Wessels Company, for example, [sells a line of tanks with heavy-duty, replaceable butyl bladders](https://www.westank.com/replaceable-bladder-tanks/), most of them ASME-rated units for commercial and industrial systems rather than a house on a private well. The residential well tank on your basement floor or in your wellhouse almost certainly isn't built that way, so once its bladder is gone, the tank is gone with it.

That makes the real decision simpler than it feels in the moment: either the precharge is low and dry air comes out, which means a top-off and a recheck, or water comes out, which means [pressure tank replacement](/pressure-tank-replacement-wenatchee/). ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it safe to check a pressure tank bladder myself?

Yes, if you do the two steps that matter: kill the breaker to the pump first, and fully depressurize the system by running a faucet until the gauge reads 0 before you touch the air valve. The test itself is just a tire gauge on a valve stem. What isn't safe to guess at is the electrical panel. If you can't confirm which breaker feeds the pump, or the panel looks questionable, stop and call rather than testing while the pump could still energize.

### How often should I check the tank's precharge?

Once a year is reasonable for most homes, ideally when you're already doing other seasonal checks like winterizing outdoor fixtures. Tanks that are already showing symptoms, short cycling, uneven pressure, a heavy feel, deserve a check right away rather than waiting for the annual pass. A tank that's holding its precharge steady year over year rarely needs attention in between.

### The gauge shows dry air at the right pressure, but pressure still swings. What else could it be?

A good precharge reading rules out the bladder, but not the rest of the system. A stuck or worn pressure switch, a check valve that's letting water drain back down the well between cycles, or a pump that's simply undersized or wearing out can all cause pressure swings with a perfectly healthy tank. At that point the tank is confirmed fine, and the next step is a look at the switch and the pump itself rather than more air in the tank.

### Can I just add air to keep an old tank going longer?

Only if the air coming out at the valve is dry. Topping off precharge on a tank that's simply lost some air over time is a normal, useful thing to do, and it can buy a tank real extra life. Topping off a tank where water is present at the valve does nothing useful; the air you add has nowhere sound to sit, and it won't stop the short cycling or fix the underlying rupture. The valve test itself is what tells you which situation you're in before you spend time on the wrong fix.
