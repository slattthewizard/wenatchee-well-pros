# Shallow Well Jet Pump vs. Deep Well Jet Pump: How to Tell Which One You Have

If the pump on your well is sitting above ground, in a utility room, a well house, or a pit, you have a jet pump, not a submersible. That much is easy. Figuring out which kind of jet pump, shallow well, deep well, or convertible, and whether it is still the right match for your well, takes a closer look. The three types look almost identical from the outside. What is different is hidden in the pipe count and a small assembly called the injector, and that difference decides how deep the pump can actually reach.

## How a Jet Pump Actually Pulls Water Up

A jet pump does not reach down and grab water the way a submersible does. It stays on the surface and creates suction instead. Inside the pump, a small stream of already pressurized water gets diverted back through a nozzle, where it speeds up the same way water speeds up leaving a garden hose nozzle. That fast stream passes through a venturi, a narrow throat that flares back out, and the pressure drop at that narrow point pulls raw well water in behind it. The two streams mix, slow down as the venturi widens, and get pushed on to the pressure tank. That nozzle-and-venturi piece is called the injector, or the jet, and where it sits is what separates a shallow well setup from a deep well one.

## Shallow Well Jet Pumps: One Pipe, One Ceiling

On a shallow well jet pump, the injector is built into the pump body itself, up on the surface with the motor. A single pipe runs from the pump down into the well. The pump relies on suction alone to lift water that entire distance, and suction has a hard ceiling. The [EPA's overview of private wells](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/learn-about-private-water-wells) calls jet pumps the most common choice for shallow wells, 25 feet deep or less, and submersibles the most common choice for deep wells. One widely sold shallow well model's own spec page states the same number, describing itself as [capable of raising water from a depth of 25 feet](https://www.franklinwater.com/products/surface-pumps--packages/jet-pumps/shallow-well-jet-pumps/versajet-cast-iron-shallow-well-jet-pump/).

That 25 foot figure is measured to the water level, not to the bottom of the well. And the level that counts is the pumping level, where the water sits while the pump is running, which is lower than the static level you would measure after the well has rested. A well with a static level of 20 feet can draw down past 25 once the pump runs for a while.

## Deep Well (Two-Pipe) Jet Pumps: Moving the Ejector Down the Well

Once the water sits deeper than about 25 feet, a surface-mounted injector is fighting a losing battle. A deep well jet pump solves that by moving the injector itself down into the well, at or below the working water level, and running two pipes instead of one. One pipe carries pressurized drive water down to the injector. The second, wider pipe carries the mixed stream of drive water and well water back up to the surface pump. Because the injector no longer has to lift water the full distance by suction alone, a deep well jet pump can reach considerably farther than 25 feet, with the practical ceiling set by the specific pump and motor. As one example, a convertible unit's own spec page lists it as [capable of lifting from 100 feet](https://www.franklinwater.com/products/surface-pumps--packages/jet-pumps/convertible-jet-pumps/convertible-pro-rm2-series-cast-iron-convertible-jet-pump/) and delivering up to 23 gallons per minute, a top figure that falls off as the lift gets deeper.

Say your well's pumping water level runs around 60 feet. A shallow well jet pump on that well would struggle constantly, if it primed at all. A two-pipe deep well jet pump, with the ejector set below that 60 foot mark, gives the pump a fighting chance. Flow and pressure both drop as depth increases, though, which is a sizing question as much as a pump-type question. ## Convertible Jet Pumps: Same Pump Body, Two Configurations

A convertible jet pump is a shallow well pump and a deep well pump built around the same motor and housing, with the injector swapped to change which one it is. Pull the internal shallow well injector and drop in a bypass plug, and the same pump is ready to drive an ejector assembly down in the well for deep well duty. The convertible unit linked above describes exactly that: each pump ships with an internal shallow well injector that is replaced with a bypass plug for deep well operation, with the ejector then installed down the well on two pipes.

The appeal is flexibility. If you are not sure yet how deep your water level really sits, or if you are replacing a pump on a well where the paperwork has gone missing, a convertible unit gives some room to get it right without buying a second pump body later. ## Why the 25-Foot Number Isn't Just a Rule of Thumb

The suction side of a shallow well jet pump is limited by something the pump cannot change: atmospheric pressure is what actually pushes water up that pipe, not the pump itself. The pump just lowers the pressure at the top so the atmosphere can do the pushing. That effect has a limit, and friction in the pipe, a long horizontal run to the well, elevation, and warmer water all eat into it further. That is why the same pump that primes fine on one well can struggle on another well that looks similar on paper but has a slightly deeper or more roundabout suction line.

It also means a shallow well jet pump that primed reliably for years can start struggling without anything on the pump itself failing. If the water level drifts down, from a dry summer, a lower-yielding aquifer, or heavier irrigation draw nearby, the pump can end up working right at the edge of what suction can do, and the symptoms show up as intermittent priming and weak pressure rather than a clean failure.

## When a Jet Pump Stops Being the Right Answer

A submersible pump sidesteps the whole suction problem by sitting down in the well and pushing water up instead of pulling it. ## Matching Pump Type to Your Well: A Quick Checklist

If you are trying to work out what you actually have, or what you should replace it with, go through these in order:

1. Find your water level. A well report from when the well was drilled gives the static level and often a pumping level from its flow test, and a sounding tape lowered down the well can check it now. Guessing here is how the wrong pump gets bought.
2. If the level while pumping stays at 25 feet or less, a shallow well jet pump is a reasonable fit, and probably what is already installed.
3. If it is deeper than 25 feet and shallower than roughly 100 feet, a deep well jet pump or a submersible are both on the table. Which one depends on well casing diameter, wiring run, and how much flow you need.
4. If it is deeper than that, or the well casing is too narrow for a two-pipe ejector assembly, a submersible is almost always the practical choice.
5. If you genuinely do not know the water level yet and need to get water running again now, a convertible jet pump buys flexibility while you find that answer.
6. After any pipe change, a long shutdown, or a pump that sat all winter, plan on priming the system before it will run. Our [guide to priming a well pump](/blog/how-to-prime-a-well-pump/) covers the shallow, deep well, and long-idle cases separately, since the steps differ.

## Signs Your Jet Pump Setup Is Struggling

A jet pump running near the edge of what it can do tends to show it in a few consistent ways before it quits outright:

- It loses prime repeatedly, especially after a power blip, and needs to be re-primed to get water moving again.
- Pressure at the faucets is noticeably weaker than it used to be, even though the pressure switch and tank both check out.
- The pump cycles on and off more than it should for the amount of water being used.
- Water pressure drops off during the hottest, driest stretch of summer and recovers somewhat once irrigation season eases up.

None of those symptoms prove the pump itself is bad. They just as often point to a water level that has crept past what the current setup can handle, which is a different fix than a repair. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the real difference between a shallow well and a deep well jet pump?

A shallow well jet pump has its injector, the nozzle and venturi assembly that creates suction, built into the pump body above ground, with a single pipe running to the well. That limits it to water roughly 25 feet down, measured to the level while the pump is running. A deep well jet pump moves the injector down into the well itself and uses two pipes, one carrying pressurized drive water down and one bringing the mixed water back up. Moving the injector closer to the water lets it work in wells considerably deeper than a shallow well setup can reach, at the cost of a more complex installation.

### Can a shallow well jet pump be converted into a deep well jet pump?

Only if it was built as a convertible model. A true shallow well pump has its injector permanently built into the pump body, with no way to relocate it down the well. A convertible pump ships with a removable shallow well injector that can be swapped for a bypass plug and a separate two-pipe ejector assembly, turning the same pump body into a deep well setup. If you are not sure which one you have, the model plate on the pump, or a quick look inside the housing, usually settles it.

### How deep can a jet pump actually pull water from?

A shallow well jet pump tops out around 25 feet to the water level while pumping, a limit set by atmospheric pressure and suction losses rather than the pump's motor size. A deep well or convertible jet pump, with its injector down in the well, can reach considerably deeper, with the exact ceiling depending on the specific model and motor. Flow and pressure both taper off as depth increases, though, so a deep well jet pump rated for a certain depth will not necessarily deliver the same gallons per minute there that it would in a shallower well.

### Why does my jet pump keep losing prime?

Losing prime usually means air has gotten into the suction line or injector instead of a solid column of water. That can happen after a power outage, if a foot valve or check valve is not holding, if a fitting is drawing air, or if the water level has dropped closer to the edge of what the pump can suction-lift. A pump that primed reliably for years and suddenly struggles is often a sign the water level has shifted, not that the pump itself failed. Re-priming will get it running again short term, but repeated priming problems are worth having looked at.
