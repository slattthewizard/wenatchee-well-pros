# Well Pit: What It Is, Why It's a Hazard, and How It Gets Replaced

If your wellhead sits down inside a buried concrete or block chamber instead of standing up out of the yard, you have what's called a well pit. Builders used them for decades as a way to keep the pump controls and fittings below the frost line without extending the casing above grade. Today, that same setup is considered one of the more serious problems a private well can have, and Washington's own well construction rules don't allow anyone to build a new one.

This guide walks through what a well pit actually is, why it causes contamination and safety problems, what state rules say about it, and what it takes to get rid of one for good.

## What a Well Pit Actually Is

A well pit is a below-grade chamber, usually poured concrete, concrete block, or corrugated metal, built around the top of a well casing. Instead of the casing standing up out of the ground the way it should, it stops below grade inside the pit. The pump's pressure switch, the wiring connections, and sometimes the pressure tank itself sit down inside that chamber, usually reached through a hatch or lid set at ground level.

The reasoning made sense at the time: bury the fittings and controls below the frost line so they don't freeze, without having to trench the water line deep enough to escape frost everywhere it runs. It worked, mechanically. It just came with two problems that don't show up until years later: the chamber fills with water, and eventually someone has to climb down into it.

A pitless adapter does the same freeze protection job a different way. It's a fitting mounted inside the casing below the frost line that routes the horizontal line to the house out through the side wall of the casing, sealed and buried, while the casing itself extends up out of the ground and stays capped. No chamber, no hatch, nothing below grade to flood or enter. See [what a pitless adapter is and why your well needs one](/blog/pitless-adapter-explained/) for how the fitting itself works.

## Why Older Wells in This Region Sometimes Have One

Pits show up most often on wells drilled before pitless adapters became the standard approach, which happened gradually over the second half of the last century. In the foothills and orchard country around Wenatchee, Cashmere, and Leavenworth, that usually means an older property: a cabin converted to a year-round home, acreage that's changed hands a few times, or a well nobody has opened up and looked at since it was drilled. If you bought a place with a well and you've never actually seen the wellhead, a pit is one of the first things worth checking for.

## The Contamination Problem

A well pit is a hole in the ground next to your wellhead, and holes in the ground collect water. Snowmelt, irrigation overspray, and heavy rain all find the low spot, and the low spot happens to be exactly where your casing seal, wiring splices, and pipe fittings live. [The EPA's rundown of well water contaminants](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/potential-well-water-contaminants-and-their-impacts) notes that runoff from rain or snowmelt can contaminate a private well by washing microorganisms into the well system or letting them seep in underground, one of the more common ways a well gets fouled from the surface rather than from the aquifer itself. The agency's own [wellhead protection guidance](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/protect-your-homes-water) tells owners to grade the ground so it slopes away from the well, exactly the opposite of what a below-grade pit does by design.

Once surface water gets past fittings that were never designed to sit underwater, you usually can't tell just by looking. The water can run clear and taste fine while still carrying bacteria from the surface. That's the real argument for testing rather than guessing: if your pit has flooded, or you're not sure whether it's stayed dry, treat it as a reason to test, not a reason to assume it's fine. A positive result doesn't mean panic, it means a known next step. See [what a positive coliform bacteria test on a well actually means](/blog/coliform-bacteria-well-water/) for that part.

## The Confined-Space Problem

Set the water issue aside for a moment. The pit itself is a hazard to enter. [OSHA's definition of a confined space](https://www.osha.gov/confined-spaces) is an area large enough to enter and work in, with limited or restricted ways in or out, that isn't built for people to occupy continuously. The hazards it lists for permit-required spaces include a hazardous atmosphere, or the potential for one, and engulfment risk from water or loose material inside the space. A below-grade well pit fits that description closely: one hatch, a tight chamber, and standing water or trapped soil gas are both realistic possibilities depending on how long it's been sealed up.

None of that means every well pit is an emergency. It means you shouldn't treat the lid as just another thing to pop open with a flashlight. If you need to check something inside a pit, especially anything involving the wiring or the pressure switch, shut off the breaker to the pump first and verify it's dead with a meter before touching anything electrical. Anything beyond a visual check, and especially anything that means actually climbing down into the chamber, is a job for a professional rather than a solo project.

## Washington's Rule on Well Pits

Washington's well construction standards address this directly. Under [WAC 173-160-291](https://app.leg.wa.gov/wac/default.aspx?cite=173-160-291), the watertight casing of a water well must extend at least six inches above the ground surface, and pit completion is prohibited outright. Where a site is subject to flooding, the casing has to rise even higher: at least two feet above the estimated water level of a hundred-year flood. Pitless adapters are explicitly allowed as long as they're built with fittings approved by the Department of Health.

In plain terms, the state doesn't allow anyone to build a new well pit today, and the rule describes what a finished wellhead should look like: casing above grade, a secured cap, no chamber below it. What the rule means for an older well that already has a pit, and what kinds of work on it trigger an upgrade, is a question for the [Department of Ecology](https://ecology.wa.gov/water-shorelines/water-supply/wells) or your county rather than something to assume. Either way, if the wellhead is being reworked anyway, that same standard is the sensible target rather than rebuilding the pit. The same section also says that anyone who removes part of a well's surface seal to install a pitless adapter is responsible for having the seal repaired by a licensed or otherwise qualified person.

## How an Old Well Pit Gets Replaced

Getting rid of a pit means extending the casing up out of the ground and moving the connection point below the frost line instead of above it, inside the pit.

| | Old-style well pit | Pitless adapter setup |
|---|---|---|
| Where controls sit | Below grade, inside the chamber | Above grade, at a capped, standing casing |
| Flood risk | Chamber can fill with surface water | Nothing below grade to flood |
| Entry required for service | Yes, climb down into the pit | No, everything is accessible from above ground |
| Meets current WA well construction rule | No | Yes |

The general sequence: excavate around the existing casing, cut and extend it above grade to meet the height requirement, install a pitless adapter fitting at the point where the horizontal line needs to exit below the frost line, reconnect the drop pipe and wiring, backfill, and grade the ground so it slopes away from the casing instead of toward it. A new [sanitary well cap](/blog/well-cap-replacement/) goes on top. Extending a casing and cutting in an adapter is work on the well itself, so ask whoever quotes it whether their license covers it and whether any notice to Ecology is needed. ## Signs Your Well Pit Needs Attention Now

1. Standing water in the pit at any time of year, not just after a storm
2. Cracked, heaving, or shifted concrete or block walls
3. A lid or hatch that no longer seals tight, or has gaps around the edge
4. A musty or sour smell coming from the pit when you open it
5. Water quality that changes noticeably after heavy rain or spring runoff
6. Visible corrosion on wiring, conduit, or fittings inside the chamber
7. A home inspector or well inspection report flagging the pit during a purchase or sale

Any one of these is a reason to get it looked at rather than wait for the next problem to force the issue. A pit that's been dry and sealed for years can still fail the day a wet spring finally overwhelms it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it safe to open my well pit and look inside?

A quick look with the lid open is fine in most cases, but treat it as a look, not an inspection. Don't climb down into the chamber. A below-grade pit can trap soil gas or hold standing water you can't see from the top, and both are real hazards in a tight space with one way out. If you see water, corrosion, or damage and want to know what it means for your system, call **[(509) 300-5151](tel:+15093005151)** rather than going in yourself to check further.

### Do I have to remove a well pit before I sell my home in Washington?

Don't assume either way. A well inspection done as part of buying or selling a home can flag a pit, since pit completion is prohibited under current construction standards, and the buyer's lender may have its own requirements for the well. Whether it has to be fixed before closing often comes down to that inspection report, the lender, and negotiation between buyer and seller. Check with your county health department, your agent, and the lender for what applies to your specific transaction before you list or close.

### Can a pitless adapter be added to an existing well without redrilling?

Yes, in most cases. Adding a pitless adapter to an existing well means working with the casing that's already there, extending it above grade and cutting in the adapter fitting at the right depth, rather than drilling a new well. The well itself, the casing, and the aquifer connection don't change. What changes is where the plumbing exits the casing and how the top of the well is finished. It's wellhead work, not a full well replacement, though any disturbed surface seal has to be repaired by a licensed or otherwise qualified person under Washington's rules.

### What's the difference between a well pit and a pitless adapter?

A well pit is a below-grade chamber that houses the pump controls and fittings, with the casing stopping short of the surface inside it. A pitless adapter is a fitting inside the casing that lets the horizontal line to the house exit below the frost line while the casing itself stands up out of the ground, sealed and capped. Both solve the same freeze-protection problem. A pitless adapter does it without a chamber below grade, which is why it's the current standard and why building a new well pit isn't allowed under Washington's construction rules.
