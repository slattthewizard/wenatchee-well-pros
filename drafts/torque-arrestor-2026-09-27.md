# Torque Arrestor: What It Does on a Submersible Well Pump

If a tech has ever pulled your pump and mentioned a torque arrestor without stopping to explain it, you're not alone in wondering what it is. It's a rubber collar clamped around the drop pipe just above the pump, and it has one job: keeping the pump from banging against the well casing every time it starts. Here's what it actually does, what it does not do, and the other parts riding on that same drop string that are worth knowing about.

## What a Torque Arrestor Is

A torque arrestor is a slotted rubber sleeve that clamps around the drop pipe close to the pump with stainless steel pipe clamps. [Boshart's installation guidance](https://support.boshart.com/what-is-the-function-of-torque-arrestors-in-a-well-water-system) is to clamp the bottom onto the pipe just above the pump, then slide the top down so the rubber bows outward until it is equal to or slightly larger than the inside diameter of the casing. It ends up snug against the casing wall, with gaps between the rubber ribs so water can still move past it in the annular space.

Torque arrestors come in different sizes to match the drop pipe and the casing diameter, so the one on your pump should fit your particular well. Deeper wells sometimes get more than one on the string; how many, and where, follows the pump and arrestor manufacturers' instructions for that installation.

## Why a Submersible Pump Twists When It Starts

A submersible motor spins an impeller shaft at high speed the instant power hits it. That spinning has a reaction: the motor and everything bolted to it, the pump body, the drop pipe, the wire, all get pushed the opposite direction for a fraction of a second. Techs call this starting torque, and it happens every single time the pump cycles on, which on a busy household system can be dozens of times a day.

On a plastic drop pipe especially, that twisting force isn't resisted much by the pipe itself, because poly pipe is flexible. Left unchecked, the pump twists the pipe and swings against the casing wall on every start. Over years, that repeated rubbing wears at the pipe and chafes the wire insulation, which is the wear Boshart's page above describes the arrestor protecting against.

If you're not sure whether your well has galvanized steel or poly drop pipe, our guide to [drop pipe material choices](/blog/drop-pipe-galvanized-vs-poly/) walks through the difference and why it matters when a pump gets replaced.

## What a Torque Arrestor Does, and What It Doesn't Do

Here's the part worth being precise about, because it gets misstated a lot. A torque arrestor centers the drop pipe assembly in the casing and absorbs that twisting energy at the point where it's strongest, right at the pump. That's it. It keeps the pump from slamming sideways into the casing wall on startup, and it keeps the whole assembly running straighter down the well.

What it does not do is hold pipe joints together or stop threaded and glued fittings from working loose over time. Those are two separate problems. A coupling that wasn't glued correctly, a compression fitting that wasn't torqued to spec, or threads that weren't sealed right will still loosen over years of use whether or not a torque arrestor is anywhere on the string. [Boshart's page on the purpose of a torque arrestor](https://support.boshart.com/what-is-the-purpose-of-a-torque-arrestor) describes its job as centralizing the pump and absorbing starting torque, keeping the pump, pipe, and wire from rubbing on the casing. Securing pipe connections isn't part of it.

So if a pump has come loose at a joint, that's a fitting or workmanship issue, not a sign the torque arrestor failed. Two different parts, two different jobs.

## The Rest of the Drop String

A torque arrestor is one piece of a bigger assembly that comes up together whenever a pump is pulled. Each part has a specific job, and since the whole string is out of the well at once, a pump pull is the natural time to check or replace the smaller parts together.

| Component | What It Does |
|---|---|
| Drop pipe | Suspends the pump and carries water up the well, either poly or galvanized steel |
| Torque arrestor | Centers the pump in the casing and absorbs the starting twist |
| Submersible pump splice kit | Waterproof joint connecting the pump's motor leads to the wire run to the surface |
| Well pump safety rope | Backup line tied to the pump and secured at the wellhead in case the pipe or wire fails |
| Check valve | Holds the column of water in the pipe between cycles so it doesn't drain back down the well |
| Pitless adapter | The fitting where the horizontal line to the house exits the casing below frost line |

## Submersible Pump Splice Kit: The Joint That Has to Stay Dry

The pump itself has short motor lead wires coming out of its top. Those get joined to the longer wire run heading up to the surface, and that joint is called a splice. Because it spends the rest of its working life sitting in water, it cannot be made with ordinary wire nuts or electrical tape. Neither one is rated to stay watertight under pressure for years at a time.

A proper submersible pump splice kit uses either heat shrink tubing with an adhesive liner or a two part epoxy that fully encapsulates the connection, sealing out water so the copper underneath never corrodes. [Boshart's splicing instructions](https://support.boshart.com/submersible-pump-cable-splicing-instructions) walk through the steps: stripping the cable jacket back, staggering the individual splices so the bundle doesn't get too thick, crimping each connector properly, and centering the heat shrink before sealing it.

How many wires you're dealing with, and what colors they should be, depends on whether the pump is a 2 wire or 3 wire design; our [guide to that difference](/blog/two-wire-vs-three-wire-well-pump/) covers it if you're not sure which one you have. Either way, this is electrical work at a wellhead, not a kitchen outlet. Shut the breaker off and verify it's dead with a meter before touching any of it, and if the pump needs to come out of a deep well to get at that splice, that's a job for someone with the right equipment.

## Well Pump Safety Rope: Insurance, Not a Repair

A well pump safety rope ties to a stainless steel eyelet at the top of the pump, runs up alongside the drop pipe and wire, and gets secured to the well cap or casing at the wellhead. It's usually taped to the pipe and wire bundle every so often to keep it from tangling on the way down.

Its purpose is simple: if the drop pipe union ever separates, or the wire fails and can no longer support the pump's weight, the safety rope catches it before it falls to the bottom of the well. Recovering a pump that's dropped free is a much bigger job than pulling one that's still attached to something at the surface. Synthetic rope is used for this job; [Boshart's page covering safety rope types](https://blog.boshart.com/all-about-the-different-types-of-safety-rope) compares polypropylene and nylon options and recommends that every pump have a safety rope secured to the wellhead before it's hung in the well.

A safety rope doesn't reduce the twisting force on the pump, and it isn't a substitute for a properly sized torque arrestor. It's the backup for when something else on the string fails, nothing more.

## Signs the Drop String Needs a Look

You can't see any of these parts without pulling the pump, but there are signs from the surface that something down there deserves attention:

1. A banging or knocking sound right when the pump kicks on, felt at the wellhead or heard through nearby plumbing.
2. Any time a pump comes out for another reason and the torque arrestor turns out cracked, undersized for the casing, or missing entirely.
3. A splice on the old wire that was taped instead of properly shrink sealed, or shows greenish corrosion at the crimp.
4. No safety rope on the pump when it's pulled, or one that's frayed, brittle, or rotted through.
5. Startup banging that gets noticeably worse during a season of heavy cycling, like peak irrigation demand or a hidden leak running the pump more than it should.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does a torque arrestor actually do?

It centers the drop pipe and pump inside the well casing and absorbs the twisting force, called starting torque, that happens every time the pump switches on. Without it, that twist pushes the pump and pipe sideways into the casing wall on every cycle. It gets clamped onto the pipe just above the pump and expanded until it fits snugly against the casing. It's a wear prevention part, not a structural fastener, and it works whether your drop pipe is poly or galvanized steel.

### Does a torque arrestor stop pipe joints from loosening?

No, and this is a common mix up. A torque arrestor limits how far the pump and pipe can swing sideways when the motor starts. It does not hold threaded or glued joints together. A fitting that wasn't assembled correctly can still work loose over time whether or not a torque arrestor is present anywhere on the string. If a pump comes up with a loose union, that points to the fitting itself, not a torque arrestor problem.

### What is a submersible pump splice kit and why does it matter?

It's the waterproof connection between the pump's own motor lead wires and the wire run that continues up to the surface. Because that joint sits underwater for the life of the pump, it has to be sealed with heat shrink tubing that has an adhesive liner, or a two part epoxy that fully encases the connection. Standard wire nuts or tape are not rated for that and will eventually let water reach the copper, corrode the connection, and cause the pump to fail electrically well before its motor wears out.

### What does a well pump safety rope do?

It's a backup line, usually polypropylene, tied to an eyelet at the top of the pump and secured to the well cap or casing at the surface. If the drop pipe ever separates or the wire fails and can no longer hold the pump's weight, the rope catches it so it doesn't fall to the bottom of the well, where getting it back out becomes a much bigger job. It doesn't reduce the starting torque on the pump and isn't a stand in for a properly installed torque arrestor. It's insurance for when something else on the string fails.
