## What a Constant Pressure Well System Actually Does

A standard well setup works in cycles. Your pump kicks on, fills a pressure tank, and shuts off when the tank hits a set pressure, usually 60 psi. You use water, the tank drains down to 40 psi, and the pump kicks on again. That 20 psi swing is why your shower surges and sags.

A constant pressure well system replaces that on-off cycle with a variable frequency drive, or VFD. Instead of running at full blast or not at all, the pump speeds up and slows down to match your water use. Open one faucet, the pump loafs along. Fire up the shower, the dishwasher, and a couple of sprinkler zones, and it ramps up to keep pressure within about 1 to 2 psi of your target.

The result feels like city water. Set it at 60 psi and you get 60 psi at 7 a.m. on laundry day and 60 psi at midnight. If you want the full mechanical picture of what's happening downhole, our guide on [how a well pump works](/blog/how-does-a-well-pump-work/) covers the basics in plain English.

### The main parts

Most constant pressure systems around here include three pieces. A variable speed submersible pump or a VFD controller paired with your existing pump. A small pressure tank, often just 2 to 8 gallons, since the drive does the regulating. And a pressure sensor that tells the controller what's happening in real time.

That tiny tank surprises people. Your old 40-gallon bladder tank exists to limit pump starts. With a VFD, the pump barely "starts" in the traditional sense, so the big tank isn't needed.

## Constant Pressure vs. a Standard Pressure Tank Setup

Both systems deliver water. The difference is how steady that water feels and what you'll pay up front and over time.

| Factor | Standard Tank System | Constant Pressure System |
|---|---|---|
| Pressure swing | 20 psi (40/60 typical) | 1 to 2 psi |
| Typical installed cost | $800 to $1,800 (tank + switch) | $2,000 to $5,500 |
| Tank size | 20 to 86 gallons | 2 to 8 gallons |
| Pump starts | Hard starts, full voltage | Soft starts, ramped |
| Electronics | Almost none | Controller, sensor, drive |
| Repair cost when it fails | $150 to $900 | $600 to $2,500 (drive replacement) |
| Lightning/surge sensitivity | Low | Higher, surge protection recommended |
| Works during power outage | No (both need power) | No |

Notice that last row. Neither system pumps water without electricity. If outages are your worry, you want our article on [backup generators for well pumps](/blog/generator-for-well-pump/), not a constant pressure upgrade.

The soft-start advantage deserves a second look, though. Every hard start on a standard pump sends a jolt of current through the motor and a torque snap through the whole assembly. Over 15 years, that's tens of thousands of jolts. VFDs ramp up gently, which is one reason manufacturers claim longer motor life. In our experience that claim holds up, with one big caveat we'll get to in the downsides section.

## Who Actually Benefits From Constant Pressure

Some households get dramatic, every-single-day value from these systems. Others would honestly never notice the difference. You're a strong candidate if you check two or more of these boxes.

### Big or busy households

Three or more bathrooms, teenagers, multi-generational living, or a vacation rental. The more fixtures running at once, the more a standard system's pressure swings show. This is where constant pressure shines brightest.

### Irrigation on the same well

A lot of properties from Cashmere to Quincy run sprinklers, drip lines, or a few rows of fruit trees off the household well. Irrigation zones plus indoor use is exactly the variable demand a VFD handles well. Heavy irrigators should also read up on [irrigation well pumps](/blog/irrigation-well-pumps/), because sometimes a dedicated irrigation setup beats one overworked system.

### Low-yield wells

This one surprises people. Some constant pressure controllers can be set to run the pump slower for longer, drawing water gently instead of in hard pulls. On a well that produces 3 gallons a minute, that gentler draw can mean the difference between steady water and sucking the well down. If your well is marginal, our article on [what to do when a well starts running dry](/blog/well-running-dry/) pairs well with this conversation.

### Pressure-sensitive equipment

Tankless water heaters, reverse osmosis systems, and some water treatment gear all perform better with stable inlet pressure. Tankless heaters in particular can short-fire and throw error codes when pressure swings 20 psi mid-shower.

### Who can skip it

One or two people in the house, a strong well, and no irrigation? A properly sized standard tank will serve you fine for a fraction of the cost. Take Dale in East Wenatchee. He called us last spring convinced he needed a $4,000 constant pressure system because his pressure had gotten "surgy and weird." Turned out his bladder tank was waterlogged, the air charge was gone, and his pump was short cycling every 30 seconds. We replaced the tank for $1,050 and his pressure problems vanished. A [waterlogged pressure tank](/blog/pressure-tank-waterlogged/) mimics a lot of expensive-sounding problems, which is why we diagnose before we quote.

## The Downsides Nobody Puts in the Brochure

We install these systems and we like them. But you should hear the trade-offs from your contractor before you sign, not after.

**Electronics fail, and they're not cheap.** A standard pressure switch costs about $25 in parts. A failed VFD controller costs $600 to $2,500 to replace, and it usually fails after the warranty does. The drives are the weak link in an otherwise reliable system.

**Power quality matters.** Lightning, utility surges, and brownouts are hard on drive electronics. Rural lines in our area take their share of hits, especially up the canyons. We won't install a constant pressure system without a dedicated surge protector, and we recommend whole-house surge protection too.

**Heat is the enemy.** Controllers need to shed heat. A drive crammed into a tiny, unventilated pump house that hits 110 degrees in a Quincy July will die young. Placement and ventilation matter, and so does keeping the same space from freezing in January. Our guide on [pump house insulation](/blog/pump-house-insulation/) covers protecting equipment through both extremes.

**Fewer people can fix them.** Any well contractor can swap a pressure switch. Drive diagnostics take specific training and, often, brand-specific parts. If your installer disappears, you may wait longer for service.

**They don't fix a bad well or a worn pump.** If your static water level is dropping or your pump is at the end of its life, constant pressure electronics just regulate the decline more smoothly. Know the [signs of a failing well pump](/blog/signs-well-pump-failing/) before you invest, because pairing a new controller with a dying pump is the most common way homeowners waste money on these systems.

One more story, because this mistake is expensive. Ray, up near Chelan, bought a VFD controller online for $1,400 and had a handyman wire it to his 16-year-old pump. The pump's worn motor drew erratic current, the drive kept faulting, and within four months both the pump and the controller were dead. His total cost to do it twice, the right way the second time, came to almost $7,000. A pump test before installation would have cost him nothing and saved him about $2,500. Please don't DIY the electrical side of these systems. Resetting a breaker is fine. Wiring a 230-volt drive is not a weekend project.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does a constant pressure well system last?

The pump itself typically lasts 10 to 15 years, similar to a quality standard submersible, and soft starting can stretch that. The controller is the shorter-lived part, usually 8 to 12 years with good surge protection and ventilation. Budget for one drive replacement over the life of the system.

### Can I add constant pressure to my existing well pump?

Often, yes. If your submersible pump is under about 8 years old, properly sized, and a compatible motor type, a retrofit VFD controller runs $2,000 to $3,200 installed. We always test the pump first, because installing a drive on a worn pump tends to kill both. If the pump's marginal, replacing pump and controller together is cheaper than doing the job twice.

### Does a constant pressure system use more electricity?

Usually it's a wash, and sometimes it's a small savings. The pump runs longer but at lower speeds, and eliminating hard starts cuts the inrush current that spikes your draw. Most Wenatchee-area homeowners see no meaningful change on their power bill, within a few dollars a month either way.

### Will constant pressure fix my low water pressure?

Only if your pressure problem comes from normal tank cycling or high simultaneous demand. If your well yield is weak, your pump is failing, or a pipe or filter is restricted, a constant pressure system won't create water that isn't there. That's why we diagnose the cause first, then recommend the fix, not the other way around.
