# Dug Well vs Drilled Well: What Chelan, Douglas, and Grant County Homeowners Need to Know

Most wells in the Wenatchee Valley and the surrounding orchard country are drilled, not dug. But older properties, especially places bought decades ago near a river bottom or a low bench of land, sometimes still have a dug well on the property, hiding under a concrete lid somewhere in the yard. Dug wells and drilled wells are built differently, they pull water from different places, and they behave very differently in a dry August. Here's what actually separates them, and what it means if you own one or are about to buy a property that has one.

## How a Dug Well Is Actually Built

A dug well is exactly what it sounds like. Historically it was excavated by hand with a shovel, and later with a backhoe, down to a point below the water table. The hole is wide, often three feet across or more, and it's lined, or "cased," with stone, brick, tile, or poured concrete rings to keep the sides from collapsing. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency describes dug and bored wells as typically reaching only 10 to 30 feet deep, which is shallow by modern standards ([EPA, Learn About Private Water Wells](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/learn-about-private-water-wells)).

Because the diameter is so large, a dug well exposes a big surface area to the surrounding ground. That was actually the point: in fine sand, silt, or clay, a wide opening could collect enough water even where a narrow pipe couldn't. It's a design built around the limits of hand tools, not around water quality.

## How a Drilled Well Is Actually Built

A drilled well is built with a percussion or rotary drill rig that bores a narrow hole, usually somewhere in the range of 4 to 8 inches across, straight down through soil and into bedrock. Casing, typically steel or PVC, runs from the surface down through the loose upper ground, and the upper part of the space around the outside of the casing is sealed with grout, a surface seal that stops surface water running down the outside of the pipe. The U.S. Geological Survey notes that drilled wells "can be drilled more than 1,000 feet deep," and most residential wells in this region land well short of that, but still far deeper than any dug well ever goes ([USGS, Groundwater Wells](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/groundwater-wells)).

That casing and grout seal is the single biggest structural difference between the two well types. A drilled well is cased through the loose ground and sealed at the top, so its water has to come from deep in the formation. A dug well, by contrast, is often not continuously cased at all, just lined for support.

Here's the comparison side by side:

| Feature | Dug well | Drilled well |
|---|---|---|
| Typical depth | About 10 to 30 feet | Often hundreds of feet; can exceed 1,000 |
| Diameter | Several feet across | Usually 4 to 8 inches |
| Casing | Often not continuous; stone, brick, tile, or concrete lining | Steel or PVC casing with a grouted surface seal |
| Built with | Shovel, backhoe, or auger | Percussion or rotary drill rig |
| Water source | Shallow water table | Often a deeper, more protected aquifer |

## Depth and Water Source: Why It Changes Everything

A dug well sits right at the water table, the level where the ground is naturally saturated. That level moves. It rises after snowmelt and heavy rain and falls through a dry summer, and a dug well's bottom is fixed in place, so its yield rises and falls with the season.

A drilled well usually reaches past that shallow, moving water table into deeper ground, sometimes a confined aquifer sealed above by less permeable rock. Water down there has usually passed through far more soil and rock on its way down. That depth is also what keeps it more insulated from what's happening at the surface, for better and for worse: it's more stable, but if it ever needs work, you're pulling equipment from much farther down.

## Contamination Risk: The Real Difference Between the Two

This is the part that actually matters for drinking water. A shallow, wide, loosely sealed well sits close to everything happening on the surface: a septic drainfield, fertilized ground, livestock, runoff after a storm. The USGS is direct about it: dug and bored wells "are shallow and lack continuous casing, making them subject to contamination from nearby surface sources," and the same is largely true of driven point wells, which are also shallow ([USGS, Groundwater Wells](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/groundwater-wells)). Drilled wells aren't immune to contamination, but the depth and the sealed casing make it a lot harder for surface contaminants to reach the water.

In orchard country specifically, nitrate from fertilizer is the contaminant worth taking seriously, and it moves through soil into shallow groundwater more easily than it does into a deep, sealed well (see our [guide to nitrates in well water](/blog/nitrates-in-well-water/)). Washington's Department of Health recommends every private well owner, dug or drilled, test annually for coliform bacteria and nitrate, and re-test within six months if nitrate comes back at 5 milligrams per liter or higher ([WA Department of Health and Ecology, Private Wells: Information for Owners](https://doh.wa.gov/sites/default/files/legacy/Documents/Pubs/331-349.pdf)). If you own a dug well, that annual test matters more, not less. A positive coliform result doesn't automatically mean the well itself is bad; it can mean a cracked lining, a missing seal, or flooding got in, and our [coliform bacteria guide](/blog/coliform-bacteria-well-water/) walks through what a positive result actually means and what usually comes next.

## Yield and Reliability When the Water Table Drops

Drought is where the difference between the two well types shows up hardest. Because a dug well's bottom sits at a fixed depth close to the water table, the USGS notes plainly that dug and bored wells "go dry during periods of drought if the water table drops below the well bottom." A drilled well reaching a deeper, often confined aquifer is generally far less exposed to that kind of seasonal swing.

That doesn't mean drilled wells are bulletproof. Much of the Wenatchee Valley and the surrounding basin draws on the basalt aquifers beneath the Columbia Plateau, and irrigation is a heavy user of that groundwater across the region, so long-term demand is real even where the aquifer is deep and stable ([USGS, Columbia Plateau Regional Aquifer System](https://www.usgs.gov/mission-areas/water-resources/science/columbia-plateau-regional-aquifer-system)). But a drilled well going through a dry summer and a dug well going through the same summer are not facing the same odds. ## Why Most Homes Here Sit on Drilled Wells, Not Dug Ones

Part of the reason dug wells are rare across Chelan, Douglas, and Grant counties comes down to geology. Much of this region sits on thick basalt lava flows that make up the Columbia Plateau, layered rock that a shovel or a backhoe simply can't get through ([USGS, Columbia Plateau Regional Aquifer System](https://www.usgs.gov/mission-areas/water-resources/science/columbia-plateau-regional-aquifer-system)). Even away from the flats, the foothills tend to hit bedrock or dense, compacted ground at a shallow depth. Digging a wide-diameter well by hand or with a backhoe just isn't practical in that kind of ground, and it wouldn't reach reliable water even if it were. See our guide on [how deep wells typically run in this area](/blog/how-deep-are-wells-wenatchee/) for what "typical" actually looks like here. Drilling is the only realistic way to get a well through that rock and down to water that holds up through a dry season, which is why modern wells here are drilled, not dug.

## Buying (or Already Owning) a Property With a Dug Well

If a property you own or are considering has a dug well, a few things are worth checking before you assume it's fine, or assume it needs to go:

1. **Pull the well record if one exists.** Older dug wells sometimes have no record at all, but check with the county or ask your real estate agent whether a well log was ever filed.
2. **Test the water before you drink it or close on the sale.** At minimum, test for coliform bacteria and nitrate. If you're buying, your lender or county may ask for water test results before closing on a home with a private well, and our [guide to selling a home with a well](/blog/selling-home-well-disclosure-washington/) covers what sellers and buyers are typically asked to provide.
3. **Look at the lining and the lid.** Cracked mortar, a loose or homemade cover, or a lining that sits below grade instead of above it are all ways surface water gets in.
4. **Ask what's actually feeding the tap.** Some properties with an old dug well on paper have already been switched over to a drilled well or a different source. Don't assume the well you can see is the one supplying the house.
5. **Have someone check the pump and pressure system**, whatever the well type, since a failing pump or waterlogged tank can look like a well problem when it isn't. Our [well inspection checklist for buying a house](/blog/well-inspection-buying-home/) goes through this in more detail.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a dug well be converted into a drilled well?

Not exactly. A drilled well is a separate well, drilled in a new borehole with its own casing and seal; it isn't a renovation of the existing dug well. In practice, converting from a dug well to reliable modern water usually means drilling a new well nearby and either decommissioning the old dug well or keeping it for a non-drinking use like irrigation, if local rules and your water right allow it. A licensed well driller can tell you what's realistic for your lot and your existing water right.

### How do I know if my well is dug or drilled?

Look at the diameter first. A dug well is typically several feet across, often with a visible stone, brick, or concrete lining and sometimes a simple lid rather than a sealed cap. A drilled well has a narrow steel or PVC casing, usually 4 to 8 inches across, sticking up out of the ground with a proper sanitary well cap. If you still aren't sure, a well record filed with the county or the state, or a look from someone who does this for a living, will settle it quickly.

### Is water from a dug well safe to drink?

It can be, but it needs to be tested, and tested more carefully than a drilled well because of how exposed it is to the surface. Test at minimum for coliform bacteria and nitrate every year, and re-test sooner if you notice a change in taste, color, or smell, after heavy rain or flooding, or after any repair to the well itself. A clean test result today doesn't guarantee a clean result after the next storm, which is the tradeoff that comes with a shallow, wide-diameter well.

It depends on what's actually being installed. A submersible pump replacement, which is what most modern systems use, generally runs $1,800 to $4,500, and a new pressure tank runs $600 to $1,800, separate from any cost to drill a new well itself.
