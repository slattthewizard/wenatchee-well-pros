# Well Pump Making Noise? What Clicking, Grinding and Humming Mean

A healthy well pump is one you never hear. So if you're standing in your pump house right now listening to a click every 30 seconds, or a grind, or a hum that won't quit, your gut is right. Something's off.

Here's the good news. A well pump making noise is almost always trying to tell you exactly what's wrong, and most of the time the fix is a lot cheaper than a new pump. The trick is knowing which sounds mean "fix it this week" and which ones mean "shut it off right now."

We've pulled pumps from Leavenworth to Moses Lake for years, and the same handful of noises come up over and over. In this guide we'll break down what clicking, grinding, humming, and a few other sounds actually mean, what each repair typically costs in North Central Washington, and the point where you should put the tools down and pick up the phone.

## First, Figure Out Where the Noise Is Coming From

Before you can decode the sound, you need to know what's making it. Most well systems around Wenatchee have three places noise can start.

**The pressure tank and pressure switch.** Usually in your pump house, garage, or basement. This is where clicking almost always comes from. The switch physically snaps contacts open and closed to turn the pump on and off, so one click at pump start and one at shutoff is normal.

**The pump itself.** Most homes in Chelan, Douglas, and Grant counties run a submersible pump sitting 100 to 400 feet down the well. You usually can't hear it directly, but grinding and rattling can travel up the drop pipe to the wellhead. If you have an older above-ground jet pump, you'll hear it loud and clear.

**The pipes.** Banging, gurgling, and vibration often come from the plumbing, not the pump. Air in the lines and water hammer are the usual suspects here.

Quick test: stand by the pressure tank with a faucet running and just listen for two minutes. Note when the sound happens. Constant? Only when the pump kicks on? Every 20 to 40 seconds in a loop? That timing matters more than the sound itself, and it's the first thing our techs will ask about. ## Clicking: The Most Common Well Pump Noise (and Often the Cheapest Fix)

One solid click when the pump starts and one when it stops is just your pressure switch doing its job. The problem is rapid or frequent clicking.

### Clicking every 30 to 60 seconds

This is the classic sign of short cycling. The pump turns on, hits pressure fast, shuts off, pressure drops fast, and it starts again. Round and round. Nine times out of ten the cause is a waterlogged pressure tank. The air charge inside the tank is gone, so there's no cushion, and the pump cycles constantly. We covered the full diagnosis in our post on [well pump short cycling](/blog/well-pump-short-cycling/), but the short version is this: every unnecessary start shaves life off your pump motor.

Dale in Cashmere heard his pressure switch start clicking every half minute in March and figured it could wait until after harvest prep. Two weeks later the pump motor burned out from the constant starts. Instead of a roughly $750 [pressure tank replacement](/pressure-tank-replacement-wenatchee/), he was looking at $2,400 to pull and replace a submersible pump at 220 feet. The clicking was the warning. The dead pump was the bill.

### Rapid chattering or buzzing clicks

A machine-gun chatter from the switch usually means burned or pitted contacts inside the pressure switch itself. Switches run $25 to $50 in parts and they're a common service call for us, usually $150 to $350 installed. Our guide to [pressure switch problems](/blog/pressure-switch-problems/) walks through what fails and why. One safety note: the switch has live 240-volt wiring inside. Looking is fine. Poking with a screwdriver is not.

## Grinding or Rattling: Shut the Pump Off Now

Grinding is the one noise on this list that should stop you in your tracks. It means metal is contacting metal somewhere, and every minute it runs makes the damage worse.

The usual causes:

- **Worn bearings.** Pump motor bearings wear out with age, and they fail faster when a pump short cycles for months. Once bearings grind, the motor's days are numbered.
- **Sand or sediment in the pump.** Common around here, especially in wells near the Columbia and Wenatchee rivers and in heavily pumped irrigation areas. Sand chews through impellers like sandpaper.
- **A damaged or loose impeller.** Broken impeller pieces rattle around inside the pump housing. You'll often see reduced pressure along with the noise.

Rob runs a small orchard property outside Quincy with a 280-foot irrigation well. Last July he noticed a faint grinding at the wellhead but kept watering through a heat stretch. By August the pump was moving half its normal flow. When we pulled it, the impellers were worn paper thin from sand. The repair ran $1,900. Pulling it at the first grind would have meant new impellers and a screen fix for about half that.

## Humming or Buzzing: The Pump Wants to Run but Can't

A steady hum with no water flowing means the motor is getting power but the shaft isn't turning. The motor just sits there drawing current, heating up, and cooking itself.

Three common causes:

1. **A failed start capacitor.** This little part gives the motor the jolt it needs to start spinning. When it dies, you get a hum and no water. It's one of the cheapest pump repairs there is.
2. **A seized or stuck impeller.** Sediment, scale, or a small rock can jam the impeller so the motor can't turn it.
3. **A motor that's seizing.** Worn bearings eventually lock the shaft entirely. At that point you're shopping for a pump.

Marlene in East Wenatchee did this one exactly right. Her pump hummed one morning with no water at the tap, she shut the breaker off, and she called us before lunch. It was a $325 capacitor and control box repair, done that afternoon. If she'd left the breaker on, the thermal overload would have tripped repeatedly until the windings failed, and a comparable pump replacement in her area runs $2,000 to $3,500. Same noise, two very different bills, and the only difference was the breaker.

A humming pump sometimes trips the breaker on its own. If yours does, don't keep resetting it. Repeated tripping means a real electrical fault, and forcing the breaker over and over is the most expensive habit a well owner can have.

## Other Well Pump Noises Worth a Listen

Not every sound is a clicking, grinding, or humming problem. A few more you might hear around a North Central Washington well system:

**Gurgling or sputtering.** Air is getting into the system. Sometimes it's a small leak in the drop pipe or a bad check valve. In dry years, it can mean the water level has dropped near the pump intake, which matters in our climate. After a low-snowpack winter, we see this from Chelan down to Moses Lake by late summer. Sputtering faucets plus a hot summer is worth a water level check.

**Banging or knocking pipes.** Usually water hammer, where fast-closing valves slam moving water to a stop. Annoying, hard on fittings, and fixable with arrestors or pressure adjustments. A failing check valve can also bang once each time the pump shuts off.

**High-pitched squealing or whining.** Bearings again, in an earlier stage than grinding, or a jet pump running dry. Either way it's a "this month, not this year" repair.

**Constant running with a low drone.** If the pump never shuts off, you may have a leak, a bad pressure switch, or a well that can't keep up with demand. That's a different failure pattern, and noise is only part of it. Our rundown of the [signs a well pump is failing](/blog/signs-well-pump-failing/) covers how constant running fits with the other symptoms.

The EPA recommends private well owners do an annual maintenance check of the whole system, and listening to your pump is honestly the easiest part of that. Their [private well guidance](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells) is a solid starting point if you've never had your system inspected.

## What You Can Safely Check Yourself

Before you call anyone, a few safe checks can narrow things down:

1. **Note the timing.** Constant, on startup, on shutoff, or cycling. Write it down.
2. **Check the pressure gauge.** Watch it while the noise happens. Bouncing rapidly between cut-in and cut-out pressure points to a tank or switch issue.
3. **Tap the pressure tank.** Knock on it top and bottom. The top should sound hollow, the bottom dull. If it sounds full of water all the way up, the tank is waterlogged.
4. **Flip the breaker off if you hear grinding or humming.** This is the one move that saves people thousands. A pump that can't run can't destroy itself.

That's where the DIY list ends. Pressure switches carry live 240-volt power, submersible pumps sit hundreds of feet down on equipment most homeowners don't own, and a mis-wired control box can kill a brand new motor in seconds. There's no shame in stopping at the breaker. That's the smart stopping point.

Around here, your well isn't a convenience. It's the only water your house has. In January, a dead pump can mean frozen pipes on top of no water. In July, it can mean a dead orchard row or livestock with no trough. Noises are the cheap warning you get before any of that happens. Take them seriously and you'll almost never face the 2 a.m. emergency.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it normal for a well pump to make some noise?

A little, yes. One click from the pressure switch when the pump starts and stops is normal, and above-ground jet pumps make a steady motor hum while running. What's not normal is rapid clicking, grinding, squealing, humming without water flow, or any sound that's new or getting louder. New noises are the warning stage, and that's the cheap stage.

### Why is my well pump clicking on and off every minute?

That's short cycling, and the most common cause is a waterlogged pressure tank that's lost its air charge. A failing pressure switch or a leak in the system can do it too. Don't let it ride, because frequent starts are the fastest way to burn out a pump motor. A tank recharge or replacement is far cheaper than a new pump.

### Should I turn off my well pump if it's making a grinding noise?

Yes, immediately, at the breaker. Grinding means metal-on-metal contact from worn bearings, sand damage, or a broken impeller, and the damage compounds every minute it runs. Shutting it off can be the difference between a sub-$1,000 repair and a full pump replacement. Then call a well service pro the same day.

### How much does it cost to fix a noisy well pump?

In the Wenatchee area, simple fixes like a pressure switch or start capacitor run about $150 to $600. Pressure tank replacements typically land between $600 and $1,200 installed. If the pump itself is grinding and needs to be pulled and replaced, expect $1,800 to $3,500 depending on depth and pump type. Catching the noise early almost always keeps you at the cheap end.
