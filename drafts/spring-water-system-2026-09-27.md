# How a Spring Water System Works, and When a Well Makes More Sense

If your house gets its water from a spring instead of a drilled well, you already know the setup looks different at the tank and sounds different when the pump kicks on. A spring water system does not have a well casing, a submersible pump sitting three hundred feet down, or a wellhead sticking out of the yard. It has a spring box tucked into a hillside, a pipe running downhill, and usually a storage tank and a pump somewhere between the spring and the house. That difference changes what can go wrong, how exposed the water is to contamination, and what your options are when the system starts to fail.

## What a Spring Water System Actually Is

A spring happens when groundwater moving underground meets a break in the land, a hillside, a valley bottom, a rock fracture, and comes out at the surface instead of staying underground. According to the U.S. Geological Survey, springs range from intermittent seeps that flow only after a lot of rain to huge pools flowing hundreds of millions of gallons a day (see [USGS on how springs form](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/springs-and-water-cycle)). A household spring sits at the very small end of that range.

A spring water system captures that flow at the point where it emerges and routes it to the house instead of drilling down to intercept an aquifer under its own pressure. Instead of a well, the source is a collection structure, usually called a spring box, followed by a pipe, often a storage or cistern tank, and then the same kind of pump and pressure tank arrangement a drilled well would use to get water to the fixtures at a usable pressure. Spring systems tend to belong to older rural properties in hilly country, where developing a hillside spring was simpler than drilling. If you're on one in the foothills around [Leavenworth](/well-service-leavenworth/) or [Cashmere](/well-service-cashmere/), the pump and tank side works the same as it would on a well.

## How a Spring Box Collects and Protects the Water

The spring box is the part that does the actual collecting. It is a small, usually buried chamber built right at the spot where the water comes out of the ground, with a perforated pipe or gravel-packed screen gathering the flow, a watertight lid, and a screened overflow pipe so excess water can drain off without letting insects, rodents, or surface debris in. Penn State Extension's guidance on developing a spring is a solid baseline even outside Pennsylvania: a spring used for drinking water should yield at least 2 gallons per minute throughout the entire year unless water storage is going to be used, in which case a storage tank downstream bridges the gap during the leanest part of the year (see [Penn State Extension on spring development](https://extension.psu.edu/spring-development-and-protection)).

A properly built spring fed water system also diverts surface runoff away from the collection area rather than letting it drain toward the box, and keeps the box itself watertight above ground so rain and snowmelt cannot seep in around the lid. Skip either of those steps and the spring box stops doing its job: it becomes a hole that collects whatever is running across the hillside along with the groundwater.

## From the Spring Box to Your Faucet: Storage, Pump, and Pressure Tank

Once water leaves the spring box, most systems route it into a storage or cistern tank, sometimes by gravity if the tank sits downhill, sometimes with a small transfer pump if it doesn't. From storage, a pump, often a jet pump if the lift is shallow, pushes water into a pressure tank, and the pressure tank cycles the same way it would on any drilled well: the pump kicks on at a low cut-in pressure, fills the tank, and shuts off at a higher cut-out pressure so your fixtures get steady water without the pump running constantly.

This second half of the system, the pump, the pressure tank, the wiring and the switch, behaves and fails the same way regardless of whether the water started at a spring box or three hundred feet down a drilled well. ## Why Spring Water Is More Exposed to Contamination Than a Drilled Well

A drilled well draws from an aquifer that is, at least in theory, sealed off from the surface by casing, grout, and however many feet of rock and soil sit above the water table. A spring, by definition, is groundwater that has already reached the surface. That means whatever is happening on the ground nearby, runoff, livestock, septic systems, road salt, wildfire ash, has a shorter and more direct path to the water you're collecting.

The USGS is blunt about this: spring water often looks clear and cold, but clarity is not the same as safety. Depending on the flowpath and the rock the water has moved through, a spring can carry "microorganisms, viruses, bacteria, toxic algae, and other toxic chemicals" (see the [USGS page on springs](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/springs-and-water-cycle)). The CDC lists springs alongside private wells and cisterns as private water sources that are not covered by the Safe Drinking Water Act and often are not tested on any regular schedule, and it specifically notes that flooding and heavy runoff events can introduce contaminants into springs the way they do wells (see [CDC on private drinking water](https://www.cdc.gov/environmental-health-services/php/water/private-water-public-health.html)). Washington's Department of Health draws a related line for public systems: a source is treated as under the direct influence of surface water, and given extra scrutiny, when it is close enough to nearby surface water, such as a river or lake, to receive direct surface water recharge (see [WA DOH on source water](https://doh.wa.gov/community-and-environment/drinking-water/source-water)). A spring is close to that description almost by definition.

None of this means spring water is automatically unsafe. It means it needs to be tested, and tested again after anything that changes the ground around it, the way [flooding around a wellhead](/blog/flooded-wellhead-spring-runoff/) does for a drilled well. A positive result doesn't necessarily mean the whole system is a loss either; see what a [positive coliform test](/blog/coliform-bacteria-well-water/) actually means before assuming the worst.

## Testing a Spring Water System: What to Check and How Often

At minimum, test what any private water source needs tested, and watch the things that are specific to a surface-fed source.

| Test or check | Why it matters for a spring |
|---|---|
| Total coliform bacteria | The most direct check for surface contamination reaching the collection box |
| Nitrate | A concern near orchard, agricultural, or septic-heavy ground |
| Clarity and color right after rain | A fast change means surface water is reaching the box directly, not filtering through soil and rock first |
| Flow rate in late summer | Tells you whether the source itself is declining, separate from any pump or tank issue |

Penn State's guidance is direct on this point: a roadside spring used for drinking water should be tested for total coliform bacteria and used only if it has been tested and found bacteria-free, and the same caution applies to a spring on your own land. One clean result is not a lifetime pass, so retest at least once a year, and test again any time the water's look, smell, or taste changes, especially after a heavy rain or during spring runoff. If you haven't tested in a while or aren't sure what a past result meant, our [well water testing guide](/blog/well-water-testing-wenatchee/) walks through what to test for and how to read the results.

## Signs Your Spring System Is Struggling

A few patterns are worth watching for on a spring system:

1. Flow drops off in late summer. North central Washington runs hot and dry by August, and a shallow spring in basalt or thin soil can lose flow as the water table falls, even in a year that started with a good snowpack.
2. Water turns cloudy, tea colored, or smells different right after a storm or during spring runoff. That points to surface water reaching the collection box faster than it should.
3. Pressure at the tap drops or the pump starts cycling on and off rapidly, even though the spring is still flowing fine. That's usually the pump, the pressure switch, or a [waterlogged pressure tank](/blog/pressure-tank-waterlogged/), not the source itself.
4. Lines above ground or an exposed spring box freeze solid in a hard freeze, cutting off flow completely until things thaw.

## When a Drilled Well Is the Better Long-Term Answer

Spring systems can run for decades without trouble, but they carry different tradeoffs than a drilled well, and it's worth being honest about both sides.

| Feature | Spring water system | Drilled well |
|---|---|---|
| Source exposure | At or near the surface, directly influenced by runoff | Sealed off from the surface by casing and grout, deeper in the aquifer |
| Yield in a dry summer | Can drop as the water table or recharge falls | Generally more stable, since it's drawing from below seasonal swings |
| Testing burden | Needs more frequent, more vigilant testing | Still needs testing, but starts from a more protected source |
| Fixing a failing source | Redeveloping the spring box or finding a new collection point | Deepening or drilling a new well, site conditions permitting |

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much water does a spring need to produce for a house?

Penn State Extension's general guideline is at least 2 gallons per minute, measured year round, not just in a wet month. If your spring produces less than that but does so consistently, a storage tank sized to cover your peak daily use can often bridge the gap. What matters more than the peak flow on a good day is the low point in late summer, since that's when a marginal spring actually runs short. Measuring flow directly, with a bucket and a stopwatch at the outlet, is more reliable than guessing from how the tap feels.

### Is water from a spring safe to drink without testing it first?

No. Clear, cold spring water can still carry bacteria, viruses, or chemical contaminants depending on the rock and soil the water passed through and what's happening on the surface nearby. Springs are private water sources, which means they are not covered by the Safe Drinking Water Act and are not tested on any required schedule. At minimum, test for total coliform bacteria and nitrate before drinking the water, and retest after any storm, flood, or spring runoff event that could change how surface water reaches the collection box.

### What is a spring box and what does it actually do?

A spring box is the small chamber, usually buried and sealed, built right at the point where groundwater emerges from the hillside. It collects the flow through a screen or perforated pipe, keeps out surface runoff, insects, rodents, and debris, and sends excess water out through a screened overflow pipe so the box doesn't flood or back up. A well built spring box is what separates a usable, reasonably protected water source from an open puddle that happens to be full of groundwater.

### Can a spring water system be converted to a well?

Yes, though it means drilling a new well rather than modifying the spring itself. A licensed driller would locate, drill, case and develop a new well nearby, and what it reaches depends on the ground under your property, not on the spring. We don't drill wells, but once that well exists, the pump, pressure tank, and wiring on your end often follow a similar layout to what a spring system already uses, and in some cases existing storage and distribution piping can be reworked rather than replaced outright. The right call depends on your yield, your budget, and how reliable the existing spring has actually been.
