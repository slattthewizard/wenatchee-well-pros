# What Is a Well Seal? The Cap on Top and the Grout Seal Below

Stand next to most wellheads in Chelan, Douglas or Grant County and you will see one thing: a pipe sticking up out of the ground with a cap on it. That cap is only half the story. A well seal is actually two separate barriers working together, one you can see and one you can't. The visible one is the sanitary cap on top of the casing. The one you can't see is a column of grout running down the outside of that casing, at least eighteen feet deep on a drilled well built to Washington's current standard, keeping surface water from creeping down alongside the pipe and into your aquifer.

Most homeowners only think about the seal when something has already gone wrong: a lab result comes back with bacteria in it, or the ground around the casing looks sunken and cracked after a wet spring. Understanding how the two pieces work, and where Washington draws its lines, makes it a lot easier to tell a real problem from a cosmetic one.

## What a Well Seal Actually Is

"Well seal" gets used loosely, but it really covers two different jobs.

The sanitary seal is the assembly at the top: a cap made of metal or plastic, mechanically fastened or welded to the casing so it can't be lifted off by hand. Any access port for measuring water depth, any pressure gauge, and any vent are built into that same cap and sealed against the weather. A vent is normal here, and it's supposed to be screened, not solid. Wells need to breathe as the water level inside rises and falls, and a screened vent lets air move while keeping insects and debris out. That's a design feature, not a flaw.

The surface seal, usually just called grout, is the barrier around the casing below ground. It fills the gap between the drilled hole and the outside of the casing pipe with a sealing material, most often bentonite clay or neat cement grout, so surface water and shallow, less desirable water can't travel down along the pipe wall and reach the aquifer your well actually draws from. The EPA describes the same idea in plain terms for well owners nationally: the casing keeps dirt and excess water out, and a proper cap prevents debris, insects, or small animals from getting into the well itself ([EPA, Learn About Private Water Wells](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/learn-about-private-water-wells)).

Both pieces have to hold for the well to be sanitary in the strict sense: sealed against anything getting in from above or from the sides.

## The Grout Seal Down the Casing

The grout seal is the part almost nobody thinks about, because you never see it again after the well is drilled. Washington's construction standard for water wells requires that annular space, the gap between the borehole and the permanent casing, to be filled with sealing material from the surface down to a minimum depth of eighteen feet, extending to undisturbed or recompacted soil around the casing ([WAC 173-160-231](https://apps.leg.wa.gov/wac/default.aspx?cite=173-160-231)). The rule also requires the seal to fully surround the casing with no voids, and it specifically prohibits leaving gaps in that grout for equipment to be added later, such as a pitless adapter.

That last point matters more than it sounds like. If anyone removes part of the surface seal to install a [pitless adapter](/blog/pitless-adapter-explained/), the state rule makes that person responsible for having the seal repaired by a licensed or otherwise qualified person, brought back up to land surface (WAC 173-160-291). Grout work isn't a weekend project. It takes the right material, placed correctly, by someone who does it for a living.

When a seal fails over time, ground movement around it is a common culprit: settling, frost heave, or a casing that gets knocked or leaned on during other yard work, opening a gap the grout can't bridge anymore.

## Washington's Casing Height Rule

The other half of "well seal" that homeowners run into is casing height, and Washington is specific about it. The watertight casing has to extend at least six inches above the ground surface, and pit-style completions, where the top of the well sits below grade in a buried vault, are flat out prohibited ([WAC 173-160-291](https://apps.leg.wa.gov/wac/default.aspx?cite=173-160-291)). Where a site floods, the bar is much higher: the top of the casing has to sit at least two feet above the estimated water level of a one-hundred-year flood.

Six inches doesn't sound like much, and it isn't meant to stop a flood. It's meant to keep ordinary ponding, sheet flow after a downpour, and irrigation runoff from ever reaching the cap and its seals in the first place. Any vent opening or observation port has to run by watertight pipe to at least that same six inches above grade and be shielded at the top.

Here's where orchard and hillside properties get into trouble without anyone doing anything wrong on purpose. Land gets regraded for a driveway, a berm goes in for drainage, topsoil gets added around a garden bed near the wellhead, and a few seasons later the casing that used to stand a foot tall is sitting flush with the lawn. Nobody buried the well seal deliberately. The ground came up to meet it.

## How Water and Contaminants Get Past a Failed Seal

Once either the cap seal or the grout seal is compromised, there are really only a handful of ways water finds its way in, and they tend to show up in combination rather than alone:

1. **A cracked or sunken surface seal.** Frost heave, settling soil, or a casing that got bumped can open a channel right next to the pipe.
2. **A casing that's dropped below the six-inch minimum.** Regrading, mulch, or new landscaping raises the ground level around the well instead of lowering it, and the seal ends up underground.
3. **A cap that isn't sealed tight.** A cracked gasket, a cap that was pried off and set back on loosely, or hardware that's rusted through.
4. **An unsealed or damaged vent or access port.** These are supposed to be shielded against foreign material; a broken screen or a missing cover defeats that.
5. **A pitless adapter installation that never got the surface seal properly restored** after the casing was opened to install it.

The EPA's guidance for private well owners lists the same warning signs homeowners should walk out and check for: a broken or missing well cap, a cracked, corroded or damaged casing, and settling or cracking of the surface seal around it ([EPA, Protect Your Home's Water](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/protect-your-homes-water)). None of those are dramatic to look at. They're also exactly the things that let contamination in quietly, long before your water looks or smells any different. ## Signs Your Well Seal or Grout Has Failed

Most of these you can check from the surface, in a few minutes, without opening anything up.

1. The ground around the casing looks sunken, cracked, or has a visible gap next to the pipe.
2. The casing sticks up less than six inches, or you can't tell where it ends because it's flush with the yard.
3. The cap is loose, missing a bolt, or lifts by hand.
4. Water pools right against the wellhead after rain or irrigation instead of draining away.
5. A water test comes back positive for bacteria with no other clear source, especially right after a heavy rain or spring runoff.
6. You find insects, spiders, or debris inside the casing when the cap comes off.

If you're seeing more than one of these at once, treat it as a real problem rather than something to watch. ## Well Seal vs Well Cap vs Grout Seal, at a Glance

| Part | What it is | Where it sits | Who typically handles it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sanitary well seal (cap) | Metal or plastic cover mechanically fastened to the casing, with a screened vent and sealed access ports | Top of the casing, at least 6 inches above grade | Well or pump service work at the wellhead |
| Surface seal (grout) | Bentonite or neat cement grout filling the space between the casing and the borehole wall | From ground level down at least 18 feet along the outside of the casing | A licensed well driller, using specialized grouting equipment |
| Pitless adapter seal | The fitting joining the underground supply line to the casing, plus the surface seal disturbed to install it | Adapter sits on the side of the casing below the frost line (above static water level unless built for below); the seal it disturbs runs up to land surface | A licensed or otherwise qualified well professional |

## Fixing a Failed Seal: What You Can Check and What Needs a Pro

There's a real line between what a homeowner can look at and what has to go to a specialist, and it runs right through this topic.

You can reasonably check, yourself, from outside the well: whether the cap is on tight and bolted down, whether the casing is still standing at least six inches above the surrounding ground, whether the ground slopes away from the wellhead or toward it, and whether there's any visible cracking in the soil or old concrete pad right at the casing.

What you shouldn't try to fix yourself is anything below grade. Re-grouting a settled surface seal, extending casing that's been buried by regrading, or resealing after pitless adapter work all call for equipment and materials most homeowners don't have access to, and Washington's rules put that work on the driller or a licensed or otherwise qualified person, not a DIY patch job (WAC 173-160-231 and 173-160-291).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's the difference between a well seal and a well cap?

The well cap is the visible piece: the metal or plastic cover fastened to the top of the casing, with a screened vent and sealed access ports. "Well seal" is the broader term and usually includes the cap plus the grout seal below ground, the material filling the gap between the casing and the borehole wall down to at least eighteen feet under Washington's rule. The cap keeps things out from above; the grout keeps water from tracking down alongside the pipe.

### How deep does the grout seal need to go around my well casing?

Washington requires the surface seal, the grout around the casing, to extend from the ground surface down to a minimum depth of eighteen feet, fully surrounding the casing with no voids. Shallower minimums apply to certain driven, jetted, or dug wells, which are covered under their own sections of the same rule. For a standard drilled well in Chelan, Douglas, or Grant County, eighteen feet is the baseline you should expect a driller to meet or exceed.

### How high above the ground should my well casing be?

At least six inches above the surrounding ground surface, under Washington's construction standard. Pit-style completions, where the wellhead sits below grade, aren't allowed. If your property floods, the requirement jumps to at least two feet above the estimated one-hundred-year flood level. If regrading, landscaping, or years of mulch have brought the ground up level with your cap, that's worth having looked at, since it puts the seal below where the rule intends it to sit.

### Can I reseal my well casing myself?

You can check and maintain the parts above ground yourself: making sure the cap is bolted down tight, the vent screen is intact, and the ground slopes away from the wellhead rather than toward it. Repairing or replacing the grout seal below ground, or resealing the casing after work like a pitless adapter installation, is a different matter. Washington's rules assign that repair to the driller or a licensed or otherwise qualified well professional, and it takes equipment most homeowners don't have on hand.
