# Metallic Taste in Well Water: What's Causing It and What to Test For

You fill a glass at the kitchen sink and it tastes like you just sucked on a coin. That flavor has a name in the water world, metallic taste, and it's rarely a mystery once you know what to check. The usual suspects are iron, manganese, a low pH eating into copper pipe, and zinc coming off galvanized fittings. Lead can play a role too, though it usually doesn't announce itself by taste at all.

## What a Metallic Taste Usually Means

A metallic taste means dissolved metal is in the water, either picked up naturally as groundwater moves through rock and soil, or added later as the water moves through your plumbing. Iron, manganese, and zinc fall under what EPA calls secondary standards: non-enforceable guidelines set for taste, color, and staining rather than health. Copper and lead also carry health-based limits, covered below. Manganese is the one exception on that list, worth flagging up front: at higher concentrations it's a real health concern, not only a nuisance, which is one more reason to test rather than guess.

Each of the usual suspects has a slightly different fingerprint. Iron tends to run rusty and metallic. Manganese runs bitter-metallic and stains black instead of orange. Copper corrosion shows up with blue-green stains near drains. Zinc from galvanized pipe tastes metallic without much color at all. None of that is a substitute for a lab test, but it's a decent first read on what you're dealing with.

## Iron and Manganese: The Everyday Culprits

Iron is a common cause of a metallic taste in well water. Groundwater moving through rock and sediment can dissolve iron along the way, and by the time it reaches your tap it can carry a rust color and a taste a lot of people describe as sucking on a penny. EPA's secondary guideline for iron sits at 0.3 mg/L, roughly the point where staining and taste both become noticeable, according to [EPA's secondary drinking water standards for nuisance chemicals](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/secondary-drinking-water-standards-guidance-nuisance-chemicals). We've written more on this in our [iron in well water](/blog/iron-in-well-water/) article if you want the full picture on stains and treatment options.

Manganese tends to travel with iron and often gets blamed for the same stains, except manganese leaves black or brown marks instead of rust orange, and EPA describes its taste as bitter metallic. The secondary guideline for manganese is lower, just 0.05 mg/L, because people notice it at smaller amounts, per [Penn State Extension's breakdown of iron and manganese in private water systems](https://extension.psu.edu/iron-and-manganese-in-private-water-systems). As noted above, manganese isn't purely a cosmetic issue at higher levels, so if a test comes back elevated, that's worth acting on rather than living with. Our [manganese in well water](/blog/manganese-in-well-water/) article covers the health side and treatment options in more depth.

## Low pH and a Copper Pipe That's Dissolving

pH measures how acidic or alkaline water is, on a scale where EPA's secondary range for drinking water runs 6.5 to 8.5. Most groundwater around here runs on the hard, alkaline side, which is easy on copper pipe even if it's tough on appliances. But pH varies from well to well, and a genuinely low reading at your particular well is a real corrosion driver where it happens.

When pH drops below that range, water turns aggressive toward copper, one of the most common metals in home plumbing. USGS puts it plainly: pH outside acceptable ranges can give water a metallic taste and corrode pipes, according to [USGS's page on drinking water taste and odor](https://www.usgs.gov/mission-areas/water-resources/science/drinking-water-taste-and-odor). Blue-green stains around drains, faucet aerators, or the bottom of a white sink, paired with that metallic bite, point toward copper corrosion rather than iron or manganese, since copper doesn't turn water orange or black. EPA's secondary taste threshold for copper sits at 1.0 mg/L. Public water systems also have to act on copper under a separate health-based rule once levels climb past 1.3 ppm at customer taps, which is a sign of how seriously corrosive water is taken even though that particular rule doesn't apply to a private well ([EPA's Lead and Copper Rule](https://www.epa.gov/dwreginfo/lead-and-copper-rule)). A high dose of copper can cause nausea and vomiting, and taking in more than recommended every day over time can lead to liver and kidney damage, according to the [CDC's ToxFAQs on copper](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/tsp/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=205&toxid=37).

Here's the catch: copper pipe runs through your house, not down your well, so a corroding copper line is a plumbing problem, not a well problem. If a lab test confirms low pH at the source, a whole-house neutralizer is the usual fix, and sizing and installing that is a water treatment company's job, not ours. ## Zinc From Galvanized Pipe and Fittings

Galvanized steel pipe and fittings carry a zinc coating meant to slow rust. Over years, especially with lower pH water, that coating wears through and starts shedding zinc into the water. The taste people describe is metallic or slightly bitter, without much of the color that iron and manganese produce, since zinc corrosion doesn't stain the way rust does. EPA sets the secondary taste threshold for zinc at 5 mg/L, well above the guidelines for iron or manganese.

If your well system still runs an older galvanized drop pipe rather than poly, that's on our side of the fence, and it's worth knowing the tradeoffs, which we cover in [galvanized versus poly drop pipe](/blog/drop-pipe-galvanized-vs-poly/). Galvanized supply lines running through the house itself are a plumbing question, same as the copper pipe above.

## Lead: Why Taste Won't Rule It Out

Lead is the outlier on this list. Unlike iron, manganese, zinc, or corroding copper, lead usually doesn't give water a distinct taste at the levels that matter for health. That's exactly why you can't taste-test your way to ruling it out. Older lead solder, older brass fittings, and corrosive well water can all put lead into your glass without changing how it tastes at all. If your water tastes fine but you have any of those conditions, it's worth adding lead to your test list anyway rather than assuming taste would have told you. ## A Quick Glass Test You Can Run Tonight

Before you call anyone, a few minutes with a clear glass will narrow things down:

1. Fill a clear glass from the kitchen tap first thing in the morning, before anything else runs, and note the color and taste.
2. Run the same tap for two minutes, then fill a second glass and compare it to the first. Water that sat in the pipe overnight often picks up more metal than water that's already been flowing.
3. Check whether the metallic taste shows up in cold water, hot water, or both. Hot-only points toward the water heater rather than the well.
4. Look at the faucet aerator screen and the area around any drain for a blue-green tint. That's a copper signature.
5. Check toilet tanks and the bottom of a white tub for orange-brown residue, which points to iron, or black-brown residue, which points to manganese.
6. Write down what you find. It saves time on a service call and helps a lab tell you which tests actually matter for your water.

## What to Test For and What the Numbers Mean

Taste alone can flag a problem, but it can't give you a number, and the number is what tells you whether this is aesthetic or something to fix sooner. Here's how EPA's secondary guidance lines up against the usual suspects:

| Contaminant | EPA secondary guideline | What it usually signals |
|---|---|---|
| Iron | 0.3 mg/L | Rusty color, orange staining, metallic taste |
| Manganese | 0.05 mg/L (health advisory at 0.3 mg/L) | Black staining, bitter taste, health concern at higher levels |
| Copper | 1.0 mg/L | Blue-green staining, corroding copper pipe |
| Zinc | 5 mg/L | Metallic taste, aging galvanized fittings |
| pH | 6.5 - 8.5 | Below range: corrosive and metallic tasting. Above range: scale and a soda-like taste |

Source: [EPA's secondary drinking water standards for nuisance chemicals](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/secondary-drinking-water-standards-guidance-nuisance-chemicals); the manganese health advisory is from the Penn State Extension page linked above.

One thing worth knowing before you call a lab: [Washington DOH recommends](https://doh.wa.gov/community-and-environment/drinking-water/contaminants/testing-your-water) that private well owners test every year for coliform bacteria and nitrate. Iron, manganese, copper, zinc, and pH are separate tests, so if you want them on your report, ask the lab for them by name. That's also worth remembering because a private well isn't monitored by anyone else on your behalf. You're the one responsible for knowing what's in it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is metallic-tasting well water safe to drink?

Usually, yes, in the sense that iron, manganese, and zinc are classified by EPA as secondary, or aesthetic, contaminants rather than acute health hazards at typical levels. Manganese is the exception worth remembering: high enough concentrations are a genuine health concern, not just a taste problem. Copper and lead carry their own health-based limits under federal rules written for public water systems. Taste alone can't tell you which one you're dealing with, or how much of it is actually in your water. The only way to know for sure is a lab test for the specific metals, not a guess based on flavor.

### Why does my water only taste metallic from the hot tap?

If cold water tastes fine but hot water has that coin-like edge, the cause is usually downstream of the well, most often the water heater itself. Hot water is more chemically active than cold, so it dissolves more metal from whatever it touches, including a corroding anode rod or an aging tank lining. That points to a water heater question, not a well question. If both taps taste the same, the source is more likely upstream, in the well, the tank, or the plumbing in between, and it's worth testing rather than guessing which one it is.

### Can a new pressure tank or fitting cause a metallic taste?

New galvanized fittings can shed a little zinc when they first go into service, and some people notice a faint metallic edge for a short while afterward. It should fade as the system is flushed with normal use. Say it doesn't fade, or it shows up out of nowhere on a system that's been fine for years: that points to something changing, a low pH reading, a failing component, or corrosion working on older pipe somewhere in the system, not a new tank breaking in. A water test settles which one it is faster than waiting it out.
