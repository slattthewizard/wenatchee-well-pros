# How Deep to Bury a Water Line From Your Well to the House

If you are staring at a trench and wondering how deep to bury the water line between your well and your house, the short answer is: below the frost line for your property, with some cover to spare. The longer answer depends on your county, your soil, your elevation, and what pipe you are using. Here is how to get it right the first time, because redoing a buried line costs a lot more than digging it once, correctly.

## Why Frost Depth Decides Everything

Water expands as it turns to ice, and a pipe with no room to give will crack. You will not find out until spring thaw or the next hard freeze, whichever comes first. That is the entire reason buried water lines exist below a certain depth: get the pipe under the point where the ground actually freezes in a normal winter, and the water inside it never gets cold enough to turn to ice.

This is also why a well casing does not just run a pipe straight up out of the ground and across the yard. A fitting called a pitless adapter connects to the casing below the frost line and carries the water out horizontally to the house, so the whole run stays below grade and below freezing depth. The [EPA's overview of private well components](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/learn-about-private-water-wells) describes the pitless adapter as the connector that lets the water pipe reach the surface while staying below the frost line, which is the whole point of the part. Washington's Department of Health requires pitless adapters and well caps on public systems to meet the Water Systems Council's PAS-97 standard, and that [state policy](https://doh.wa.gov/sites/default/files/2025-08/p-m01.pdf) defines the adapter's job the same way: protecting the water from freezing or temperature extremes as it exits the casing. If your well already has one, the outgoing water line has a fixed departure depth built in, and the trench from there to the house should stay at or below that depth the rest of the way. Older wells sometimes used a below-ground well pit instead of a pitless adapter, which is a separate contamination and safety issue we cover in [our explainer on what a pitless adapter is and why your well needs one](/blog/pitless-adapter-explained/).

## How Deep Is Deep Enough in Chelan, Douglas and Grant Counties

There is no single number that applies to Wenatchee, Cashmere, Leavenworth, Chelan, Quincy and Moses Lake alike. Frost depth is driven by elevation, snow cover, soil type and how exposed a site is to wind, and all four vary a lot across this stretch of the valley and the foothills. A protected yard in East Wenatchee can freeze less deeply than an open field outside Quincy in a dry, low snow winter, and a site on higher ground up a side valley can run colder than one on the valley floor.

Because of that variation, the frost depth your local building department uses for footing and water line requirements is a local number, not a statewide one. Before you dig, call your county building department and ask what depth they use for water service lines. It is a five minute call and it removes the guesswork. If a well report exists for your property, it is worth a look too for the casing and construction details. The depth the pitless adapter sits at is easiest to confirm where the line leaves the casing.

A sensible margin: go deeper than your county's frost line, a foot deeper if the trench allows, not right at it. Frost depth figures are averages, not guarantees, and extra cover costs very little more to dig than a run placed right at the line.

## Choosing Pipe Material: Poly, PVC or Copper

Polyethylene, usually called poly pipe, is a common choice for a buried well to house line. It comes in long coils, so a straight run from the well to the house can go in with zero underground joints, and every joint is a future leak point you do not have. Poly pipe is also flexible enough to shift slightly with ground movement and freeze thaw cycling without cracking. The pipe industry's own installation standard notes that polyethylene's toughness lets it resist failure under the expansive force of freezing water, a useful property for a pipe that should be below the freeze line but does not need to be treated as fragile. Any pipe used for potable water service should carry a certification mark showing it meets [the national standard for potable water piping](https://cdn.ymaws.com/www.ppfahome.org/resource/resmgr/pdf/pe_guide.pdf), which for PE pipe is ANSI/NSF Standard 14. Look for that stamp on the coil before it goes in the ground.

Rigid PVC schedule 40 or 80 also gets used, particularly on shorter runs or where a straight, unbending line matters. It often costs less per foot, but every length needs a solvent weld or a mechanical fitting, and PVC is less forgiving of ground movement and impact in cold weather than poly. Copper shows up on older installations and short indoor runs but is rarely chosen for a new buried service line today given the material cost. Either material needs a valid potable water certification, visible on the pipe itself.

One thing worth separating in your head: the drop pipe hanging inside the well casing from the pump up to the pitless adapter is a different piece of pipe than the buried line running from the adapter to the house, and it faces different stresses. ## Sizing the Line: 1 Inch or 1 and 1/4 Inch

Well water line size usually comes down to two options for a typical house: 1 inch or 1 and 1/4 inch. The difference is not cosmetic. A narrower pipe creates more friction loss as water moves through it, and that loss gets worse the farther the water travels and the faster it flows. The table below uses the pipe industry's published friction loss figures for standard SDR 11, 160 psi polyethylene pipe, per 100 feet of run.

| Flow (GPM) | 1 inch pipe, head loss per 100 ft | 1-1/4 inch pipe, head loss per 100 ft |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 1.4 ft | 0.5 ft |
| 8 | 3.4 ft | 1.1 ft |
| 10 | 5.1 ft | 1.6 ft |
| 12 | 7.2 ft | 2.3 ft |
| 15 | 10.8 ft | 3.5 ft |

At low flow and a short run, 1 inch pipe is fine. Once you are pushing toward 10 or more gallons per minute (two bathrooms running plus a hose bib, for example) or the run from the well to the house is several hundred feet, that friction loss starts eating into the pressure that reaches your fixtures, and 1 and 1/4 inch becomes the better call. A house with an orchard irrigation tie in or a long driveway run to the wellhead is exactly the situation where upsizing pays for itself. ## Trenching, Backfill and Marking the Route

Getting the depth right does not help much if the pipe gets damaged going in, or if nobody can find it again in five years. A few things matter more than people expect:

1. Keep the trench bottom smooth and free of rocks. A sharp stone under the pipe becomes a wear point for years.
2. Use clean fill, sand or fine soil, as bedding directly around the pipe before backfilling with the excavated material. Frozen clods and debris should stay out of the first layer.
3. Leave the pipe with a little slack, snaked gently rather than pulled drum tight, so it can move slightly with ground temperature changes without stressing the fittings at each end.
4. Sleeve the pipe anywhere it crosses under a driveway, a parking pad, or anywhere heavy equipment will drive over it later.
5. Keep at least 18 inches of cover even where frost is not the concern, which is what the plastic pipe industry's installation guide recommends to protect the line from digging and vehicle traffic. Frost depth, where it is deeper, sets the real minimum.
6. Bury a length of tracer wire alongside the pipe, run up to a marker post or valve box at each end. Poly and PVC do not show up on a metal detector, and a locator can trace tracer wire to show exactly where the pipe sits years later, before you plant a tree or set a fence post.

Before any new digging near the route, whether it is your project or a contractor's, Washington's [call before you dig law](https://www.utc.wa.gov/call-you-dig-its-law) requires notifying 811 at least two business days ahead so gas, power and other utility lines get located and marked. That system covers utility company infrastructure, not your own private well line, which is exactly why tracer wire and a marked route on your own property matter: 811 will not find your water line for you.

## What Happens When a Line Is Too Shallow, and What Repair Costs

A line buried at or above the frost line is a slow motion problem. In a hard freeze it can split, and you will not know until you turn on a tap and get nothing, or worse, get water everywhere from a break you cannot see. Short of a freeze, a shallow line nicked by a fence post auger or crushed under equipment traffic shows up as a slow leak: an unusually green patch of lawn, a well pump that seems to run more than it used to, or a pump that kicks on when nothing in the house is running.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How deep should a water line be buried in Washington?

It depends on your county's frost depth, which varies with elevation, snow cover and soil type across Chelan, Douglas and Grant counties. There is no single statewide number for water service lines. Call your county building department and ask what depth they require, then plan for some extra cover beyond that figure. Separately, the plastic pipe industry's installation guide recommends at least 18 inches of cover everywhere, frost or not, to protect the pipe from digging and vehicle traffic above it.

### What size pipe should I use for a well water line?

Most houses run fine on 1 inch pipe for a short distance at typical household flow. Once you are regularly pulling 10 gallons per minute or more, such as two bathrooms plus a hose bib running together, or the run from the well to the house stretches several hundred feet, friction loss in a 1 inch line starts costing real pressure at the fixtures. Stepping up to 1 and 1/4 inch pipe cuts that friction loss by roughly two thirds at the same flow rate, often worth the modest extra cost of the larger pipe.

### Can I use PVC instead of poly pipe for my well line?

Yes, rigid PVC schedule 40 or 80 is a valid option and is used on plenty of buried water service lines. It tends to cost less per foot but needs solvent welded or mechanical joints at every length, unlike poly pipe, which can run in one continuous coiled length with no underground joints at all. PVC is also less forgiving of ground shifting and cold weather impact than poly. Either material needs to be rated and certified for potable water use, with the certification mark visible on the pipe.

### Do I need to call 811 before digging up my water line?

Yes, if you are opening any new trench, Washington's call before you dig law requires notifying 811 at least two business days beforehand so utility companies can locate and mark their lines near your dig site. That said, 811 only marks utility company infrastructure, not your own private well line. If your water line was installed with tracer wire running alongside it, a locator can trace that wire to show you exactly where your own pipe sits before you dig near it.
