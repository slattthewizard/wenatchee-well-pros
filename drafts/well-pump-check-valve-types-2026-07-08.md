# Well Pump Check Valves: Types and Why They Fail

A loud bang in the pipes every time your pump shuts off isn't your house settling. It's water slamming backward through a check valve that's no longer doing its job. That single part, usually smaller than your fist, is the reason your pump doesn't lose its prime every time it stops and the reason your pressure tank doesn't drain back down the casing overnight.

A well pump check valve is a one-way gate. Water flows up toward the house and it's not supposed to flow back down. When it fails, you get symptoms that look like a dozen other well problems: banging pipes, a pump that short cycles, pressure that won't build, or a pump that hums but never seems to catch. In this guide, we'll cover the main types of check valves used in wells across North Central Washington, why they wear out, what failure actually sounds and feels like, and what it costs to fix.

## What a Well Pump Check Valve Actually Does

Think of your well as a tall column of water sitting inside a pipe that runs from the pump, deep in the casing, up to your pressure tank in the house or pump house. Without a check valve, every time the pump shut off, that entire column of water would fall straight back down the pipe under gravity.

That backflow causes three problems. First, it drains your pressure tank fast, which makes the pump kick on again almost immediately, a pattern called short cycling. Second, the sudden stop of falling water slams into the check valve or the pump itself, creating the banging noise plumbers call water hammer. Third, and worst for your wallet, a pump that loses its prime constantly or short cycles nonstop wears out its bearings, seals, and motor windings years before it should.

A well pump check valve solves all three by snapping shut the instant flow reverses. Most systems have at least one valve right at the pump. Deeper wells, especially those over 100 feet, often use a second or third check valve spaced every 100 to 200 feet up the drop pipe to break the water column into shorter, lighter sections. That's less weight and less shock on any single valve.

## Types of Well Pump Check Valves

Not every valve is built the same way, and the type matters for how it fails and what it costs to replace. Here's how the common types compare.

| Check Valve Type | Where It's Used | Typical Lifespan | Replacement Cost (Parts + Labor) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spring-loaded poppet valve | Most submersible pumps, standard choice | 8 to 15 years | $150 to $400 |
| Swing/flapper check valve | Older jet pump systems, shallow wells | 5 to 10 years | $150 to $350 |
| In-line ball check valve | Some jet pump and booster setups | 8 to 12 years | $175 to $400 |
| Foot valve (bottom of drop pipe) | Shallow well jet pump suction lines | 3 to 7 years | $200 to $450 |
| Multiple spaced check valves | Deep wells, 100+ feet of drop pipe | 10 to 15 years each | $300 to $900 for the set |

Spring-loaded poppet valves are what you'll find on almost every modern submersible pump installation around here. A small spring holds a disc against a seat, and water pressure from the pump pushes it open. When the pump stops, the spring snaps the disc shut before gravity can get the water column moving backward. They're compact, reliable, and built to thread right into the pump discharge or drop pipe.

Swing check valves use a hinged flapper instead of a spring. Gravity and flow control the flapper's motion, and they're common on older jet pump systems where the valve sits above ground and is easy to service. The tradeoff is a slightly slower close, which means more homes with swing valves report water hammer than homes with spring-loaded valves.

Foot valves sit at the very bottom of the suction line in a shallow well jet pump setup, below the water line, keeping the suction pipe primed between pump cycles. They take the most abuse because they're constantly submerged and exposed to sediment, and they fail more often than any other type on this list.

On deep wells, we install multiple check valves spaced along the drop pipe rather than relying on one valve at the pump to hold back hundreds of feet of water. This is standard practice recommended by pump manufacturers and it dramatically cuts the water hammer and wear that a single valve would otherwise absorb alone.

## Why Well Pump Check Valves Fail

Every check valve eventually wears out, but a few things speed that up in our part of Washington.

**Sediment and mineral buildup.** The mineral-heavy groundwater common from Cashmere to Quincy leaves scale on valve seats and springs. A little grit is enough to keep the disc or flapper from seating fully, which lets water leak backward even with the pump running.

**Spring fatigue.** Springs cycle every single time the pump starts and stops, sometimes dozens of times a day on a busy household or an irrigation well. Metal fatigue is just a matter of time, and by year eight to twelve most spring-loaded valves are due.

**Debris jamming the seat.** Sand, rust flakes from an aging casing, or bits of scale can lodge under the disc and prop it open just enough to leak. This is more common in older wells or ones that have never been chlorinated or cleaned.

**The wrong valve for the application.** We occasionally find a shallow-well foot valve installed on a deep submersible setup, or a single check valve trying to hold back 300 feet of water column alone. Undersized or mismatched valves fail years earlier than they should.

## Signs Your Check Valve Has Failed

A bad well pump check valve rarely fails quietly. Watch for these:

- **Banging or thumping in the pipes** right when the pump shuts off, the classic water hammer sound.
- **Short cycling**, where the pump kicks on and off every few seconds or minutes even with no water running.
- **Long pump run times before pressure builds**, because the pump is re-priming the column instead of just topping off the tank.
- **A humming pump that struggles to reach normal pressure**, which can point to a lost prime from backflow. We break that scenario down further in [well pump humming but not running](/blog/well-pump-humming-not-running/).
- **A pressure tank that reads low first thing in the morning** even though nobody used water overnight, a sign the tank drained back through a leaking valve all night long.

None of these symptoms are exclusive to a check valve. A waterlogged tank, a failing pressure switch, or a worn pump can all mimic the same signs. That's exactly why a proper diagnostic beats guessing, especially before you spend money replacing the wrong part. ## Preventing Check Valve Failure

You can't stop a check valve from aging, but a few habits slow it down.

1. **Don't ignore small bangs.** A single soft thump when the pump shuts off, caught early, is a $200 fix. Left alone for a year, it can crack fittings and damage the pump housing too.
2. **Match valve count to well depth.** If your well is over 150 feet and you've only got one check valve, ask your tech whether adding a second would ease the strain during your next service visit.
3. **Keep sediment out of the system.** Fine sand and grit accelerate valve wear. If your water runs cloudy or gritty, our post on [submersible vs. jet pump systems](/blog/submersible-vs-jet-pump/) covers how pump type affects sediment intake, and it's worth a read if you're due for an upgrade anyway.
4. **Replace on schedule, not just on failure.** Most valves are good for 8 to 15 years. If yours is past that window during a service call, swapping it proactively costs far less than an emergency repair after it fails.
5. **Have your system checked annually.** A pressure and cycle test during a routine visit catches a leaking valve long before it announces itself with a bang at 2 a.m.

Washington's Department of Health maintains guidance for private well owners on system components and maintenance basics, and the EPA's [private well owner resource page](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells) is a solid general reference if you want to understand how the pieces of your system fit together before you call anyone out.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many check valves does a well pump need?

Most shallow wells need just one, either at the pump or the wellhead. Deep wells, generally over 100 to 150 feet, often use two or three spaced along the drop pipe so no single valve has to hold back the entire water column alone. Your pump's manufacturer specs usually state the recommended spacing for your depth.

### Can a bad check valve damage my well pump?

Yes, and it's one of the more common ways pumps die early. A failed valve causes short cycling and lost prime, both of which put extra wear on the motor, bearings, and seals every single cycle. A $200 to $400 valve replacement now is far cheaper than the $1,800 to $4,500 a full pump replacement can run later.

### What does a failing well pump check valve sound like?

The most common symptom is a bang or thump in the pipes right as the pump shuts off, caused by water slamming backward before the valve seats. You may also hear rapid clicking at the pressure switch or notice the pump running longer than usual to rebuild pressure after sitting idle.

### Is replacing a well pump check valve a DIY job?

A valve at the wellhead or pump discharge above ground is sometimes manageable for a handy homeowner comfortable shutting off power and depressurizing the line first. Anything requiring the submersible pump to be pulled from the casing involves heavy equipment, wiring, and down-hole work, and that's a job for a licensed well professional every time.
