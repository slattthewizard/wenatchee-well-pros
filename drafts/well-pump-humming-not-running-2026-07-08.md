# Well Pump Humming but Not Running? Here's What's Wrong

Your well pump is humming. Not running, not building pressure, just a steady electrical drone from the pump house or the wellhead that won't stop. That sound means power is reaching the motor, but the motor isn't spinning.

Well pump humming not running is one of the more specific problems we diagnose across the Wenatchee Valley, and unlike a lot of well trouble, it usually narrows down fast to one of three things: a failed start capacitor, a jammed impeller, or a motor that's seizing for good. The good news is that two of those three are cheap, quick fixes. The bad news is that a humming pump left running can turn a cheap fix into an expensive one within hours, because the motor is drawing full current with nowhere for that energy to go except heat.

## Why Your Well Pump Hums but Won't Run

A submersible well pump is really a sealed electric motor with an impeller on the end, hanging on a string of pipe somewhere between 100 and 400 feet down your well. When you open a tap and pressure drops, the pressure switch sends power down to the motor. In a healthy system, the motor spins immediately and water starts moving within a second or two.

A hum with no water means the motor is getting that power but the shaft isn't turning. Electrically, the motor is still trying. Mechanically, something is stopping it. That's different from a well pump that's simply not working at all, where there's often no power reaching the pump in the first place. We cover that broader no-power scenario, breakers, switches, and tanks, in our guide to a [well pump not working](/blog/well-pump-not-working/).

It's also different from the general category of pump noise. Clicking, grinding, and squealing point to other parts of the system entirely. We break those down in our full guide to [well pump noise](/blog/well-pump-noise/). Humming with zero water output is specific enough to deserve its own answer, which is the rest of this guide.

## The 3 Real Causes of a Well Pump Humming, Not Running

Every humming-but-not-running call we run traces back to one of three things, roughly in order of how often we see them and how much they cost to fix.

1. **A failed start capacitor.** The cheapest and most common cause. The motor can't get the electrical kick it needs to start turning.
2. **A stuck or jammed impeller.** Sand, scale, or debris physically blocks the impeller from spinning, even though the motor itself is fine.
3. **A seized motor or worn bearings.** The most expensive cause. The shaft has locked up mechanically, usually after months of unnoticed wear.

All three sound identical from the surface. That's the frustrating part. But how the pump behaves over the next few minutes, and what your pump's recent history looks like, usually tells you which one you're dealing with before a tech ever shows up.

## Cause 1: A Failed Start Capacitor (Most Common, Cheapest Fix)

Most single-phase submersible pumps need a jolt of extra current to get the motor spinning from a dead stop. That jolt comes from a start capacitor sitting in the control box, usually mounted near your pressure tank or breaker panel, not down in the well itself. Once the motor is spinning, it doesn't need the capacitor anymore, which is why a bad one produces a hum instead of silence. The motor is trying its best with less than it needs.

Capacitors fail from age, heat, and power surges. Ours take a beating from summer thunderstorms and the occasional grid blip that rolls through the valley. A capacitor typically lasts 5 to 10 years, and when it goes, it usually goes without warning. One morning the pump runs fine. The next, it hums and does nothing.

This is genuinely good news as far as well pump failures go. A capacitor swap is one of the cheapest, fastest repairs in the well system, often done in under an hour because the part lives in the control box above ground, not down the well.

Because she caught it fast and killed power right away, the motor itself never got hot enough to suffer any damage.

## Cause 2: A Stuck or Jammed Impeller

The impeller is the small spinning wheel inside the pump that actually pushes water up the pipe. If something physically blocks it, sand, a mineral scale chunk, a bit of casing debris, or even a small rock that worked its way in, the motor spins up and hums against the resistance but the impeller won't turn.

This cause shows up most in wells with sediment problems, and we see it a lot in the sandier ground around Quincy and Moses Lake, along with older wells where scale has built up over the years. If your water has been gritty, cloudy, or leaving sand in the bottom of a glass before the pump quit, a jammed impeller should be near the top of your suspect list.

Ray farms an orchard block outside Quincy on a 240-foot irrigation well. Mid-July, right at peak watering, his pump started humming instead of running. He'd noticed a little sand in the drip lines about a week before but hadn't thought much of it. When we pulled the pump, the impeller was packed solid with fine sand and had partially fused to the housing from mineral buildup. Freeing and cleaning it, plus a new screen at the intake, ran $950 and saved him a full pump replacement mid-harvest. Left running against that jam another week or two, the motor windings likely would have cooked, and that bill jumps past $2,500.

A jammed impeller isn't something you can safely clear yourself. It means pulling the pump out of the well, which takes a winch, proper pipe handling, and two trained people, not a rope and a truck bumper.

## Cause 3: A Seized Motor or Worn Bearings (The Expensive One)

If the capacitor is fine and nothing is physically blocking the impeller, the hum usually means the motor itself has locked up. Internal bearings wear down over years of normal use, especially on a pump that's been short cycling or running past its expected lifespan. Once bearings fail badly enough, the shaft simply won't turn anymore, no matter how much current the motor pulls trying.

This is the outcome nobody wants, because at this point you're typically shopping for a new pump rather than a repair. Most submersible pumps run 10 to 15 years under normal conditions. A seized motor on a pump already past that mark is often the writing on the wall, not a fluke.

Here's the part that matters most. A pump doesn't usually seize out of nowhere. It gives warning signs for weeks or months first: intermittent humming, a faint growl before full failure, pressure that's been slowly weakening. ## What You Can Safely Check, and When to Call a Pro

There's a real, safe role for a homeowner here, and it happens entirely above ground.

**Do this:**
- **Shut the breaker off the moment you notice the hum.** This is the single most valuable thing you can do. A pump that can't run can't overheat or destroy itself further.
- **Note the pattern.** Does it hum continuously, or start and stop every time a faucet opens? Does it hum for a few seconds then go quiet, or run indefinitely?
- **Think back on recent water quality.** Sand, grit, or cloudiness in the days before the pump quit points toward a jammed impeller rather than a capacitor.
- **Check for a burning smell** near the control box, which can point to a capacitor or wiring problem overheating.

**Don't do this:**
- Don't open the control box or test the capacitor yourself. It runs on 240 volts and can hold a charge even with power off.
- Don't pull the pump. Submersible pumps hang on hundreds of feet of pipe and wire, and dropping one into the casing turns a repair into a much bigger problem.
- Don't keep resetting a breaker that trips repeatedly. That's an electrical fault protecting you, not a glitch to push through.

Anything involving the wellhead, the wiring, or work down the casing is licensed-tech territory. Improperly resealing a well cap or mishandling downhole components can also let surface contaminants into your water supply, which is exactly the kind of risk the [EPA's private well guidance](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells) warns homeowners to leave to professionals.

A humming pump is rarely a true middle-of-the-night emergency the way a burst pipe is, since your pressure tank usually holds some reserve water. But a few situations move it up the priority list fast:

- The breaker trips repeatedly when you try to reset it.
- You smell burning near the control box or panel.
- You've got livestock, an orchard block, or a household that genuinely can't wait on water.
- The hum started right after your water turned gritty, sandy, or cloudy.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why does my well pump hum but not run?

It means the motor is receiving power but the shaft isn't turning. The three most common reasons are a failed start capacitor, a stuck or jammed impeller, and a seized motor from worn bearings. A capacitor is the cheapest and most common cause, while a seized motor usually means a full pump replacement.

### Should I turn off my well pump if it's humming and not pumping water?

Yes, right away, at the breaker. A humming motor is drawing full current with nowhere for that energy to go, which builds heat fast and can turn a small, cheap problem into a burned-out motor within hours. Shutting off the breaker protects the pump while you figure out the cause.

### Can a bad capacitor make a well pump hum?

Yes, and it's the most common reason a pump hums instead of running. The start capacitor gives the motor the initial jolt it needs to begin spinning. When it fails, the motor tries to start, draws power, and hums, but never gets moving. It's typically a $250 to $600 repair done in under an hour.

### How much does it cost to fix a well pump that's humming but not running?

It depends on the cause. A failed start capacitor usually runs $250 to $600. A stuck impeller that needs clearing typically costs $600 to $1,800. If the motor has fully seized, a full pump replacement runs $1,800 to $4,500 depending on your well's depth. A diagnostic visit will tell you which category you're in before any work starts.
