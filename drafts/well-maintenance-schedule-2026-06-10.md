## Your Monthly Well Maintenance Checklist: The Five-Minute Walk-Through

Once a month, walk out to your wellhead and pump house and run through this list. It takes five minutes. Pick a day you'll remember, like the first of the month or the day you pay the power bill.

**Listen to the pump cycle.** Open a hose bib and let it run until the pump kicks on, then off. You want one smooth cycle, on at around 40 psi, off at around 60. If the pump clicks on and off rapidly, that's short cycling, and it's the number one pump killer we see. Our post on [well pump short cycling](/blog/well-pump-short-cycling/) covers what causes it.

**Check the pressure gauge.** Note the cut-in and cut-out pressures. Write them down. A gauge that's drifting from last month's numbers is telling you something, usually about the pressure switch or the tank's air charge.

**Look at the wellhead.** The cap should be tight, undamaged, and sitting at least 12 inches above grade. No cracks, no missing bolts, no rodent nests, no standing water pooling around the casing. A bad cap lets insects and surface water straight into your drinking supply.

**Scan the pump house or well pit.** Look for damp spots, dripping fittings, rust streaks on the tank, and chewed wiring insulation. Mice love pump houses, and they love wire insulation more.

**Tap the pressure tank.** The top half should sound hollow, the bottom solid. If it sounds full of water all the way up, the bladder may be shot. That's a tank on its way out, and catching it now is the difference between a planned swap and a dead pump.

**Run your senses on the water.** Fill a clear glass from the tap. Cloudiness, sediment, a metallic taste, or a rotten egg smell are all early warnings worth chasing down.

None of this requires tools. It just requires showing up once a month.

## The Annual Well Maintenance Schedule, Season by Season

North Central Washington is hard on wells. We swing from 100 degree July afternoons to single digit January nights, and irrigation season puts every pump in the valley under load. Here's how to spread the annual work across the year so nothing sneaks up on you.

### Spring (March to April): Test and Inspect

Spring is water testing season. Snowmelt and spring runoff push surface water down through the soil, and that's when bacteria and nitrates are most likely to show up in a well. Test for coliform bacteria, nitrates, and anything specific to your area. The [Washington State Department of Health](https://doh.wa.gov/community-and-environment/drinking-water) keeps guidance on what private well owners should test for and where to find certified labs. We wrote a local guide to [well water testing in the Wenatchee area](/blog/well-water-testing-wenatchee/) that walks through the process step by step.

Spring is also when we recommend the annual professional inspection. Get it done before irrigation season, when your pump goes from light duty to running hours a day.

### Summer (June to August): Watch the Workload

Summer is stress season. If you irrigate a lawn, pasture, or orchard block off your well, watch your recovery. If pressure sags in the evening or the water runs cloudy after long pumping sessions, your well may be getting pulled down faster than it can recover. Ease off the irrigation schedule and pay attention, because running a pump dry will destroy it in minutes.

Check your pump house ventilation too. Pressure switches and control boxes don't love 110 degree enclosed spaces.

### Fall (September to October): Winterize

Before the first hard freeze, insulate exposed pipes, check the pump house heat source, and seal up drafts. A frozen pipe in a pump house is one of the most common emergency calls we run every December, and almost every one traces back to a missing heat lamp bulb or a door that wouldn't latch. Our [guide to winterizing your well pump](/blog/winterize-well-pump/) covers the full fall checklist.

Marie in Quincy skipped this step in 2024. A $9 heat lamp bulb had burned out in her pump house sometime in November, and nobody noticed until the pipes froze solid during the December cold snap. The thaw split a fitting, flooded the pump house, and shorted her pressure switch. Total bill: $1,640. The bulb was on the shelf at the hardware store the whole time.

### Winter (November to February): Monitor

Winter is mostly about your monthly walk-through, done faithfully. Check that heat sources are working every time you're out there. After any cold snap below 10 degrees, run a faucet and confirm normal pressure. Catching a partial freeze early usually means a hair dryer and an hour, not a plumbing repair.

## The Big-Ticket Items: Know Your System's Age

A maintenance schedule isn't just tasks. It's also knowing where every major component sits in its lifespan, so replacements happen on your terms.

**Submersible pumps** last 10 to 15 years on average around here, longer with clean water and a healthy pressure tank, shorter with sand or short cycling. If yours is past year 10, learn the warning signs. Our post on the [signs your well pump is failing](/blog/signs-well-pump-failing/) lists the big ones: pressure swings, air spitting from faucets, a breaker that trips, and a power bill creeping up.

**Pressure tanks** run 8 to 15 years. The bladder fails first, and the tap test above catches it.

**Pressure switches and gauges** are cheap parts that fail often. Budget for a switch every 5 to 10 years.

**The well itself** can produce for 50 years or more, but yield can decline as screens plug or the aquifer changes. If you've noticed your well struggling to keep up, a yield test tells you whether it's the well or the equipment.

Keep a simple log: install dates, service dates, pressure readings, test results. When something acts up, that log turns guesswork into a fast diagnosis, and it's gold when you sell the property.

Hector, who runs a small orchard outside Chelan, is our favorite example of the log paying off. His monthly notes showed cut-out pressure slipping from 60 to 52 psi over three months in 2025. That pattern pointed us straight to a worn pressure switch and a small bladder leak. Total fix: $480, scheduled on a Tuesday. Without the log, the next chapter of that story is a dead pump during August irrigation, and on an orchard that's not just a repair bill, it's fruit on the line.

## When to Stop and Call a Pro

Most of this schedule is eyes, ears, and a notepad. But know where the line is.

Call a professional when:

- Anything electrical is involved beyond flipping a breaker. Well pumps run on 240 volts. Don't open control boxes or splice wiring.
- The pump needs to come out of the well. Pulling a pump from 200 feet down takes equipment and experience, and a dropped pump turns a repair into a recovery job.
- Your water tests positive for bacteria. Shock chlorination needs to be done right, with the right concentration and contact time, or it doesn't work.
- The well casing or cap is damaged. That's your aquifer's last line of defense, and Washington has rules about well construction and repair through the [Department of Ecology](https://ecology.wa.gov/regulations-permits/permits-certifications/wells).
- You've lost water entirely. Diagnosing no-water situations means working through power, switch, tank, pump, and well in order, and a pro does it in an hour.

There's no shame in the phone call. The most expensive repairs we see are the ones where somebody almost fixed it themselves.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How often should a private well be professionally inspected?

Once a year is the standard, and spring is the best time in North Central Washington, before irrigation season loads up your pump. An annual inspection covers the pump's amp draw, pressure tank charge, switch operation, wellhead condition, and flow. Pair it with your annual water test and you've covered both the equipment and the water in one season.

### What should be on a monthly well maintenance checklist?

Five things: listen to a full pump cycle for short cycling, record the pressure gauge readings, inspect the wellhead and cap, look over the pump house for leaks and rodent damage, and check a glass of water for changes in clarity, taste, or smell. The whole routine takes about five minutes. Writing the numbers down matters more than people think, because trends reveal problems months before failures do.

### How much does annual well maintenance cost?

Plan on $400 to $900 a year all-in for the Wenatchee area: $150 to $300 for a professional inspection, $50 to $150 for water testing, and a cushion for small parts like switches and gauges. Many years you'll spend less. Compare that to $2,500 to $5,500 for an emergency pump replacement and the schedule pays for itself the first time it catches something.

### Will regular maintenance really make my well pump last longer?

Yes, and the effect is bigger than most owners expect. The top pump killers, short cycling from a waterlogged tank, running dry, and sediment wear, all show warning signs months in advance. A pump that might die at year 8 in a neglected system routinely reaches 15 years in a maintained one. You're not babying the pump, you're just removing the things that kill it early.
