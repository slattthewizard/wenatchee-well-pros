# Well Recovery Rate: What It Means When Your Well Runs Low and Comes Back

Say you run a load of laundry, wash a sink full of dishes, and someone takes a shower, all within an hour or two. The pressure drops, maybe a faucet sputters, and by the time you check again that evening, everything's back to normal. That swing, water dropping in the well and then climbing back up, is your well's recovery rate at work. It's one of the least understood numbers in a private water system, and it explains a lot of "my well is fine most of the time" complaints.

This is about the refill side of the equation: how fast water re-enters your well after you've drawn it down, how to get a rough number for your own well, and what to do if that number is small. If you're worried your well might be running dry for good rather than just recovering slowly, our guide to [warning signs of a well running dry](/blog/well-running-dry/) covers that side of the question, and we won't repeat it here.

## Recovery Rate and Yield Are Related, But Not the Same Number

Well yield is the maximum rate, in gallons per minute (GPM), that a well can be pumped continuously without dragging the water level down to the pump intake. Recovery rate is different: it's how fast the water level climbs back up once you stop pumping, or once you're using less than the well can currently deliver. Penn State Extension's guide to [managing a low-yielding well](https://extension.psu.edu/using-low-yielding-wells) defines well yield this way and walks through why a well that can't sustain enough GPM for peak household demand becomes a low-yielding well, even if it eventually delivers plenty of water over a full day.

Here's the practical difference. A well with a low yield but a fast recovery rate might struggle for twenty minutes during a big draw and then be full again by the time you need it next. A well with a slightly better yield but a slow recovery rate might take hours to climb back to normal after the same twenty minutes. Neither number alone tells you how the well will actually behave for your household. Our article on [well yield testing](/blog/well-yield-test/) walks through how a flow test measures the pumping side of this; recovery is the mirror image, measured while the well sits and refills.

## What's Actually Happening Underground

A well doesn't hold a fixed tank of water waiting to be pumped out. It's a hole into an aquifer, a layer of rock or sediment that's saturated with groundwater. Water moves into the casing through the screened or open section down near the bottom, at a rate set by the aquifer, not by your pump. The U.S. Geological Survey's explainer on [aquifers and groundwater](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/aquifers-and-groundwater) describes how precipitation slowly adds water back into that saturated rock over time, and how pumping faster than the aquifer can recharge draws the water level down until the well yields less, or runs dry.

In much of the Wenatchee Valley and the surrounding foothills, that aquifer is basalt: layered volcanic rock with water moving through cracks and fractures rather than through open pore space like sand or gravel. That's a very different recharge picture than a well set in river-bottom gravel. A well in a well-connected fracture network can recover quickly. A well that only intersects a few thin, poorly connected fractures can be slow to refill even if the aquifer overall has plenty of water, because the water simply isn't reaching that particular borehole fast enough.

## The Casing Itself Is a Small Reservoir

Between pumping cycles, some of your buffer comes from water sitting in the casing above the pump, not from instant recharge. That volume depends only on the diameter of the casing and how many feet of water sit in it. It's simple cylinder geometry, and it's worth knowing roughly what you're working with.

| Casing diameter | Water stored per foot of water in the casing |
|---|---|
| 4 inch | about 0.65 gallons |
| 5 inch | about 1.0 gallon |
| 6 inch | about 1.5 gallons |
| 8 inch | about 2.6 gallons |
| 10 inch | about 4.1 gallons |

Penn State Extension uses the 6-inch figure to show the scale of this buffer: 100 feet of standing water in a 6-inch casing holds about 147 gallons, and the same 100 feet in a 10-inch casing holds around 408 gallons. Their [low-yielding well guide](https://extension.psu.edu/using-low-yielding-wells) also points out that this sounds like a lot until you compare it to actual household draws: in a home without water-saving fixtures, a two-hour morning peak of several laundry loads, dishes, showers, toilets and sinks can top 300 gallons, and a standard 42-gallon pressure tank only delivers around 8 usable gallons before the pump has to kick back on. Casing storage helps, but on a well with a genuinely slow recovery rate, it's a buffer measured in minutes, not hours.

## How to Estimate Your Well's Recovery Rate

You don't need lab equipment to get a workable number. A few notes and a stopwatch will get you close enough to know whether you have a real problem.

1. **Check your well log first.** The Department of Ecology's [well report search tools](https://ecology.wa.gov/water-shorelines/water-supply/wells) can often pull up the original driller's report for your property. Those reports record how the well was built and the quantity of water it produced when drilled, and often the static water level and depth. That's your baseline, not necessarily today's reality.
2. **Pick a low-demand stretch.** Do this on a day when nobody else in the house is running water, ideally early morning before anyone's up.
3. **Draw the well down.** Run an outside spigot or a hose bib for a set number of minutes, until the flow weakens or the pump starts to sputter or spit air, which means the level has reached the pump intake. Shut the hose off right away when that happens, since a pump running without water can overheat. If neither happens after a long run, your well is keeping up with that draw.
4. **Shut it off and start timing.** Note the time you stop drawing water.
5. **Check back at intervals.** Every 15 or 30 minutes, run a tap briefly. When the pump builds back to its normal cut-out pressure at its usual pace and shuts off without sputtering, that's a rough sign the well has caught back up. A more precise version uses a water level sounder in the wellhead to track the actual level, not just pressure at the tank.
6. **Repeat it in a different season.** Recovery rate isn't fixed. A well that refills in twenty minutes in April can take much longer in late August after a dry summer has pulled the regional water table down.

## Signs a Slow Recovery Rate Is the Real Problem

A slow-recovering well tends to show a specific pattern, different from a pump or tank that's simply failing:

- Pressure is normal in the morning and gone by evening, especially after laundry or watering.
- The problem gets worse through a dry summer and eases up after fall rain or snowmelt.
- Running two things at once (a shower and a hose, say) makes the pressure drop faster than running just one.
- Waiting an hour or two, or overnight, brings the pressure back without anyone touching the equipment.

That last one is the tell. A pump or pressure switch that's actually failing doesn't fix itself by sitting idle overnight. A well that's simply slow to recover often does, because given enough time without a draw, the aquifer catches back up to the casing. If instead your well never seems to recover, no matter how long you wait, that's a different and more serious situation, closer to a well genuinely running dry than a slow refill, and it's worth treating it that way rather than assuming it's just a slow recovery day.

## Living With a Slow-Recovery Well

A genuinely slow recovery rate is manageable, but it usually means changing how the household uses water rather than just replacing a part.

- **Spread out big draws.** Running the dishwasher, a load of laundry, and a long shower back to back is the fastest way to outrun a slow well. Spacing them out by an hour or two lets the casing refill between draws.
- **Add non-pressurized storage.** Rather than relying on casing storage and a small pressure tank, a larger atmospheric storage tank fills slowly during recovery and holds a reserve for when you need a real draw, with a booster pump moving water from the tank to the house on demand. We cover sizing that kind of system, along with the booster pump side of it, in [cistern water storage systems for low-yield wells](/blog/cistern-water-storage-systems/).
- **Expect it to be worse in late summer.** Water tables in this region fall through the hot, dry months, and a well that recovers fine in spring can slow down considerably by August. ## When the Well Itself Deserves a Second Look

Recovery rate that's slowly gotten worse over several years, rather than just varying with the season, points to something changing underground rather than a bad day. That can mean the regional water table has dropped, a screen or open section of casing has started to clog with fine sediment or scale, or the well was simply drilled into a marginal spot for water in the first place. None of that is something you fix from the pump side. Improving the well itself, whether that means cleaning it out or going deeper, is a licensed driller's job, not ours. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long should it take for a well to recover?

There's no single normal number. It depends on the aquifer, the well's construction, and the season, and a well that recovers in twenty minutes in one house can take hours in another a mile away. Rather than comparing to a stated average, measure your own well using the drawdown-and-timing method above, then repeat it a few months later. What matters most is whether that number is stable, getting slowly worse, or swinging a lot with the season, since that tells you what kind of problem, if any, you're dealing with.

### How many gallons does a well hold?

The water sitting in the casing above the pump is roughly 0.65 gallons per foot for a 4-inch casing up to about 4 gallons per foot for a 10-inch casing, based on simple cylinder volume. A 6-inch casing with 100 feet of standing water holds around 147 gallons. That's a real buffer, but it's small compared to a household's daily use, and it isn't the same thing as your well's ongoing recovery rate, which is what determines whether that buffer refills fast enough to matter.

### What does it mean if my well runs low and refills overnight?

That pattern usually points to a slow but real recovery rate rather than a failing pump. The well can meet your needs across a full day but can't keep up with a concentrated burst of use, so the level drops during the day and climbs back once demand stops. It tends to get more noticeable in late summer, when regional water tables are lowest. Spacing out heavy water use and adding storage ahead of a big draw usually solves it without any well or pump work at all.

### Can a slow-recovery well be fixed?

Sometimes, and sometimes not, since the limiting factor is underground. A driller may be able to improve things by cleaning the well or, on some sites, deepening it, but that's outside what a pump company does. On the equipment side, adding a properly sized storage tank and booster pump lets a slow well keep up with peak household demand even though the recovery rate itself hasn't changed. If you're not sure which category your well falls into, a service call and flow test is the fastest way to find out, and the cost of that visit gets credited toward any repair you decide to move forward with.
