# Chlorinator for Well Water: When Continuous Chlorination Is the Right Call

If someone has mentioned a "chlorinator" for your well, they mean a small pump that feeds a metered dose of chlorine into your water all the time, not the one-time treatment most people picture when they hear "chlorinate the well." That one-time treatment is shock chlorination, and it solves a different problem. A chlorinator is a piece of equipment you live with, and it's worth understanding what it actually does before anyone puts one on your system.

## What a Well Water Chlorinator Actually Does

A chlorinator is a chemical feed pump that injects a chlorine solution into your water continuously, either into the well itself or into the line before the pressure tank. According to [University of Nebraska-Lincoln Extension's guide to continuous chlorination](https://extensionpubs.unl.edu/publication/g1496/na/html/view), public water systems typically hold a free chlorine residual of about 0.3 to 0.5 parts per million, and household systems often need a higher concentration because of how little contact time home plumbing provides. The chlorine does two jobs at once: it disinfects bacteria, viruses and other pathogens, and it oxidizes iron, manganese and hydrogen sulfide so they can be filtered out or so the rotten-egg smell goes away.

That second job is why continuous chlorination gets brought up for problems that have nothing to do with drinking-water safety on paper, like a sulfur smell or an iron stain that keeps coming back no matter what you try.

## Continuous Chlorination vs. Shock Chlorination

These get confused constantly because they use the same chemical. The difference is dose and duration.

| | Continuous chlorination (chlorinator) | Shock chlorination | UV treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| How it runs | A feed pump doses chlorine into every gallon, all the time | A strong one-time dose flushed through the well and plumbing, then rinsed out | A UV lamp treats water as it passes through, no chemical added |
| What it targets | Ongoing bacteria, iron, manganese, hydrogen sulfide | An active contamination event, like a positive coliform test | Bacteria and viruses as water passes the lamp; leaves no residual downstream |
| Upkeep | Chlorine solution refills, feed pump calibration, contact tank | None between treatments | Bulb replacement, sleeve cleaning, power required |
| Where it falls short | Won't destroy protozoan cysts; needs contact time to work | Doesn't prevent the problem from coming back | Doesn't touch iron, manganese or sulfur odor |

We've written separately about [shock chlorination as a one-time treatment](/blog/well-chlorination-shock/) and about [UV treatment as a chemical-free option](/blog/uv-treatment-well-water/). This article is specifically about the "always on" version.

## Why Contact Time Is the Part Most People Skip

Chlorine doesn't kill anything instantly. It needs time in contact with the water to do its job, and that's the part a lot of simple chlorinator installs get wrong. UNL Extension points out that home plumbing gives chlorine "very limited contact time," often a minute or less between the pump and the nearest faucet. That's not enough time for chlorine to finish oxidizing iron or knocking down a bacteria count.

That's why a properly designed system usually includes a contact tank (sometimes called a retention tank): a holding tank sized to give the water several minutes of dwell time with the chlorine before it reaches the pressure tank or the house. Some setups instead inject the chlorine down into the well itself, using the well casing as the contact chamber. Either way, "install a chlorinator" really means "install a chlorinator plus whatever gives it the contact time it needs," not just a pump strapped to a pipe.

## When a Chlorinator Is the Right Answer

Continuous chlorination earns its place when a problem keeps returning after a one-time treatment, or when the source is something chlorine handles well as an ongoing job rather than a single event. Say your water tested positive for coliform bacteria, you shocked the well, and three months later it's positive again. That pattern, not a single positive test, is the real signal.

Situations where a chlorinator is worth discussing with a water treatment professional:

1. Bacteria keeps testing positive after shock chlorination has already been tried more than once.
2. You have [iron bacteria](/blog/iron-bacteria-well-slime/) slime that returns within a few months of shocking. [Penn State Extension](https://extension.psu.edu/iron-and-sulfur-bacteria-a-slimy-problem) notes that shocking more often than roughly every six months starts to be hard on the system itself, which is exactly the case continuous chlorination is meant to address.
3. A [sulfur, rotten-egg smell](/blog/well-water-smells-rotten-eggs/) comes back within days or weeks of a shock treatment, rather than staying gone. [Washington's Department of Health names iron and sulfur-reducing bacteria](https://doh.wa.gov/community-and-environment/contaminants/hydrogen-sulfide) as the most common sources of hydrogen sulfide in drinking water.
4. Your water test shows iron or manganese levels the household actually notices, on top of a bacteria or odor issue, and one treatment step needs to handle both.

## What a Chlorinator Won't Fix

It's worth being blunt about the limits, because a chlorinator gets sold sometimes as a fix for problems it doesn't touch. UNL Extension is direct that chlorination "will not adequately destroy protozoan cysts such as Giardia and Cryptosporidium," and it does nothing for nitrate, heavy metals or hardness minerals. If your test results show nitrate or hardness as the concern, a chlorinator is the wrong tool entirely.

There's a second limit that matters more for iron and sulfur bacteria specifically. Penn State Extension explains that continuous chlorination at the point of use can control bacteria and reduce iron and hydrogen sulfide in the water you actually drink, "but will not control populations within the well itself." Iron bacteria build a slime layer that resists chlorine penetration, so a well that's heavily colonized may still need periodic mechanical cleaning or targeted shock treatments even after a chlorinator is running. Continuous chlorination downstream and cleaning the well itself are often both part of the answer, not a choice between them.

## Chlorine Taste, Odor, and the Carbon Filter That Usually Follows

Run chlorine through your plumbing continuously and you will taste and smell it at the tap unless something removes it first. UNL Extension notes that "activated carbon filtration following chlorination may be used to remove residual chlorine and the taste and odor associated with it," and a carbon stage also knocks down some of the disinfection byproducts that chlorination creates.

Those byproducts are worth a plain explanation, because the phrase sounds scarier than the reality. When chlorine reacts with naturally occurring organic matter in water, it forms compounds called trihalomethanes and haloacetic acids. [Washington's Department of Health page on disinfection byproducts](https://doh.wa.gov/community-and-environment/drinking-water/disinfection/disinfection-byproducts), which covers these in public systems, states plainly that "the benefits of chlorination outweigh" the byproduct risk, and that reducing contact time and chlorine concentration in storage helps limit how much forms in the first place. That's another reason a contact tank and dosing rate get sized deliberately instead of just cranked up: too little chlorine and it doesn't finish the job, too much and you're forming more of what a carbon filter then has to clean up. A treatment company sizes that balance for your specific water; it isn't a guess.

## Living With a Chlorinator

Once a chlorinator is running, it needs attention on a schedule, not just a set-and-forget install. The feed tank needs chlorine solution refilled on a regular basis, the feed rate needs to stay calibrated to your actual flow, and the [water itself should keep getting tested](/blog/well-water-testing-wenatchee/) rather than assumed fixed. Chlorine can also be hard on some rubber and gasket components over years of continuous exposure, which is one more reason a pressure tank or check valve near the injection point should get looked at during any well-side service call.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's the difference between a chlorinator and shock chlorination?

A chlorinator is equipment that doses chlorine into your water continuously, day after day. Shock chlorination is a one-time, high-strength treatment flushed through the well and plumbing, then rinsed out, usually done in response to a specific event like a positive bacteria test. Shock chlorination treats an incident; a chlorinator manages an ongoing condition. Many wells only ever need shock treatment. A chlorinator gets considered when the same problem, bacteria, iron, or sulfur odor, keeps returning after shocking rather than staying resolved.

### Will a chlorinator get rid of iron bacteria for good?

Not by itself. A chlorinator can reduce iron and hydrogen sulfide in the water you use day to day, but continuous chlorination at the point of use does not necessarily clear an established iron bacteria colony living inside the well casing, since the slime layer resists chlorine penetration. Wells with heavy colonization often still need periodic mechanical cleaning or shock treatment alongside a chlorinator, not instead of one. Ask a water treatment professional whether your test results and well history suggest that combination.

### Do I need a carbon filter if I install a chlorinator?

Almost always, yes, if anyone in the house will be drinking or cooking with the water. Continuous chlorination leaves a chlorine taste and smell at the tap, and activated carbon filtration after the chlorinator is the standard way to remove that residual along with some of the byproducts chlorination creates. A water treatment company will typically size that carbon stage as part of the same install rather than as an afterthought, because a chlorine taste at every tap is hard to live with.

### Can continuous chlorination handle a positive coliform test on its own?

It can help going forward, but it doesn't undo an existing contamination event. The standard first response to a positive coliform test is shock chlorination and a retest. Continuous chlorination becomes the conversation when bacteria keeps coming back after that, suggesting an ongoing source rather than a one-time event. Either way, don't rely on a chlorinator's presence to skip retesting; confirm with an actual water sample rather than assuming the equipment is handling it.
