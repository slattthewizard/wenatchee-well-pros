## Why Pump Houses Freeze in North Central Washington

Your well itself almost never freezes. The water table around Wenatchee typically sits 100 to 400 feet down, where ground temperature holds steady in the low 50s year-round. The danger zone is everything above ground: the pitless adapter connection, the supply line where it enters the building, the pressure tank, the pressure switch, the gauges, and all the copper or PEX plumbing inside that little shed.

Here's what makes our area tough on pump houses:

- **Big temperature swings.** A sunny 38-degree afternoon in Wenatchee can fall to 8 degrees overnight when a cold front slides down the Columbia. Buildings that coast through mild cold get caught off guard.
- **Wind.** East Wenatchee bench properties and the open ground around Quincy and Moses Lake get steady winter wind. Wind doesn't just lower the temperature, it forces cold air through every gap in the siding.
- **Arctic outbreaks.** Two or three times most winters, we get a stretch of single digits or below zero that lasts three to five days. That's the event your insulation has to be built for, not the average December night.
- **Neglect.** Most pump houses were slapped together decades ago from scrap plywood. Nobody looks inside until the water stops.

Water expands about 9 percent when it freezes. Inside a rigid pipe or a pressure switch housing, that expansion has nowhere to go. The pipe splits, the switch cracks, and you find out at 6 a.m. when the shower runs dry. ## What Pump House Insulation Actually Needs to Do

A pump house has one job in winter: hold the inside temperature above 32 degrees, every hour of every night, including the worst night of the year. Insulation does that by trapping two free heat sources you already have.

**Ground heat.** The earth under your pump house radiates heat all winter. A dirt or gravel floor is actually your friend here. That's why old-timers built pump houses small and low. Less air to heat, more ground influence per cubic foot.

**Equipment heat.** Every time your pump runs, the motor and the pressure switch contacts give off a little warmth. A pump that cycles normally adds meaningful heat to a small, tight building.

Good pump house insulation keeps that heat in. The targets we recommend for our climate:

- **Walls: R-13 minimum.** R-19 if you're in Leavenworth, Plain, or anywhere above 1,500 feet where cold pools in the valleys.
- **Ceiling or roof: R-19 to R-30.** Heat escapes up. The roof is the most important surface in the building and the one most often skipped.
- **Air sealing everywhere.** Insulation without air sealing is a sweater with no windbreaker. Foam or caulk every gap, especially where pipes and wires pass through walls.
- **An insulated, weatherstripped door.** A plywood door with daylight around the edges will erase everything else you did.

One thing insulation can't fix: a pressure tank that's already failing. A waterlogged tank makes the pump short cycle constantly, and while that extra run time technically adds heat, it's burning up your motor to do it. If your pump clicks on every time you open a tap, read up on [waterlogged pressure tank symptoms](/blog/pressure-tank-waterlogged/) before winter, or have us check it during an insulation visit.

### Don't Forget the Floor Perimeter

If your pump house sits on a slab or skids, cold attacks from below and from the edges. Rigid foam board around the perimeter, buried 12 inches or skirted down to grade, blocks the wind from washing heat out from under the building. On dirt floors, leave the center open to the ground but seal the rim where walls meet earth.

## Best Insulation Materials for a Pump House

Not all insulation belongs in a damp little building full of plumbing. Here's how the common options stack up for insulating a pump house in our area.

| Material | Cost (8x8 pump house, walls + roof) | R-value per inch | Moisture tolerance | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rigid foam board (XPS/polyiso) | $250 - $450 | R-5 to R-6.5 | Excellent | Walls, roof, door. Our top pick. |
| Closed-cell spray foam (pro installed) | $600 - $1,200 | R-6 to R-7 | Excellent | Odd-shaped or leaky buildings |
| Fiberglass batts | $120 - $250 | R-3.2 | Poor when damp | Dry, well-built pump houses only |
| Mineral wool batts | $200 - $350 | R-4 | Good | Solid all-around choice |
| Foam pipe sleeves + tank blanket | $40 - $90 | R-2 to R-4 | Good | Always, on top of wall insulation |

Rigid foam board is the workhorse. It shrugs off the humidity a pump house generates, mice don't nest in it the way they tunnel through fiberglass, and a homeowner can cut it with a utility knife. Glue or screw it to the studs, tape the seams, done.

Fiberglass is cheap but risky. A pump house is a humid space, and wet fiberglass loses most of its R-value and sags off the wall. If you go that route, cover it with a vapor-smart membrane and make sure the building sheds water properly.

Whatever you choose for the walls, also wrap the vulnerable parts directly. Foam sleeves on every exposed pipe, an insulating blanket on the pressure tank, and a foam box or insulated cover over the pressure switch (leave service access). Those small parts freeze first because they have the least thermal mass.

## Insulation Alone Isn't Always Enough: Add a Heat Source

In a typical Wenatchee or East Wenatchee winter, a tight, well-insulated pump house with a dirt floor often holds above freezing on its own. But "often" isn't the standard. The standard is the minus-10 night with a 25 mph wind. For that, most pump houses need a small backup heat source.

**Thermostatically controlled utility heater.** A 250 to 500 watt heater with a built-in thermostat set around 40 degrees is the gold standard. It only runs during real cold, so it might cost you $10 to $30 in electricity for the whole winter. Buy one rated for unattended use, keep it off the floor, away from anything flammable, and plug it into a proper outlet, never a cheap extension cord coiled on the ground.

**Heat tape on pipes.** Self-regulating heat tape is excellent insurance on the supply line and any pipe run against an exterior wall. Install it under the pipe insulation, not instead of it, and replace it every 3 to 5 years. Old heat tape is a known fire starter.

**The classic heat lamp.** A 250-watt heat lamp works, and half the pump houses in the valley have one. The problem is the single point of failure. Here's how that goes wrong.

Ray, who runs a small orchard outside Quincy, had a heat lamp that had kept his pump house safe for 11 winters. The bulb burned out on January 14th. He didn't know until January 16th, when his pressure gauge, switch, and 4 feet of one-inch pipe had all split. The repair came to $1,180, plus two days hauling water for his house and shop. A $35 thermostat heater with a pilot light he could see from the kitchen window now backs up the lamp.

If you do use a lamp, use two bulbs on separate fixtures, or add a remote thermometer that alerts your phone. A $25 WiFi sensor is the cheapest well insurance you'll ever buy.

One safety line we won't cross, and you shouldn't either: don't rewire circuits, add outlets, or hardwire heaters yourself unless you're qualified. Checking a breaker is fine. Beyond that, bring in an electrician. A burned-down pump house freezes very effectively.

## Five Mistakes That Undo Good Pump House Insulation

We inspect a lot of pump houses between October and March. The same five problems show up over and over.

**1. Insulating the walls and skipping the roof.** Warm air rises and leaves through an uninsulated roof like a chimney. If you only have budget for one surface, do the ceiling.

**2. Sealing the building so tight the equipment can't breathe, in summer.** A pump house needs to be tight in winter but ventilated in summer, or condensation rusts your tank and fittings. Install a closable vent. Shut it in November, open it in April. Put it on your fall list along with the rest of our [well winterization checklist](/blog/winterize-well-pump/).

**3. Ignoring rodents.** Mice destroy fiberglass, chew heat tape, and pack nesting material against heat lamps. Carl in East Wenatchee found that out when his heat tape failed in February. Mice had stripped the jacket off an 8-inch section. Steel wool and foam in every gap, and check inside twice a winter.

**4. Leaving the door loose.** Weatherstrip it, insulate it, latch it. A door that blows open on a windy night defeats the whole building. We've seen exactly that cause a freeze-up on a property near Chelan where everything else was done right.

**5. No monitoring.** You can't see cold. A $25 wireless thermometer on a shelf in the pump house tells you the system is working, and warns you the night it isn't. Pair it with a phone alert set at 36 degrees and you'll always have a few hours to react.

One more thing worth saying: if the pipes between the pump house and your home run shallow, insulating the building won't protect them. Supply lines in our area should be buried at least 4 feet down. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What temperature should I keep my pump house at?

Set your thermostat or heater to hold 40 degrees. That gives you an 8-degree buffer above freezing without wasting electricity. The equipment doesn't need to be warm, it just needs to stay above 32 every hour of the coldest night.

### Can I just use a heat lamp instead of insulating?

You can, but you're betting your plumbing on one bulb and one uninterrupted power supply. Insulation works with no electricity and no moving parts, then a small heat source covers the extreme nights. Do both, and add a temperature sensor so you know if the heat fails.

### Does the well itself need insulation?

No. The well bore and the pump down in it sit far below the frost line where ground temperature stays in the 50s. Insulation matters from the pitless adapter up: the supply line entry, the pressure tank, the switch, and the exposed plumbing inside the pump house.

### What if my pump house already froze and I have no water?

Shut off power to the pump at the breaker so it doesn't run dry against a frozen or split line. Never thaw pipes with a torch or open flame.
