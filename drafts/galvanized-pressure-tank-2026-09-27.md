# Galvanized Pressure Tanks: How They Work and Why Most Get Replaced

If your well system has a plain gray steel tank standing next to the pump, with no rubber liner you can see or feel through the shell, you probably have a galvanized pressure tank. These were the standard on private wells for decades before bladder and diaphragm tanks took over, and a lot of them are still running in older homes around Wenatchee, Cashmere and the foothills. They work fine when the air control is doing its job. They cause grief when it isn't.

## What a Galvanized Pressure Tank Is

A galvanized tank is a plain steel shell with one job: hold a cushion of compressed air on top of the water that comes up from your well. There's no bladder or diaphragm separating the two. Air and water sit in the same space. When the pump pushes water in, the air pocket compresses and that pressure is what pushes water through your house when you open a faucet.

That single detail, air and water sharing one chamber, is what makes these tanks different from almost everything sold today, and it's the reason they need more attention. Air in direct contact with water slowly dissolves into it. Left alone, a galvanized tank eventually loses its air cushion entirely.

## How the Air Volume Control Kept the Balance

To keep the air-to-water ratio right, galvanized systems use a device called an air volume control, usually shortened to AVC. On a deep well system it mounts to the side of the tank and works off a float: when the water level is low and air has built up, the float drops and opens a valve that vents the excess air to the outside. As the water level rises, the float lifts and closes the valve again, trapping whatever air is left. A [manufacturer's technical explanation of how the float and valve interact](https://support.boshart.com/deep-well-air-volume-controls-avc-a.k.a.-air-release-valves) lays out the mechanism in more detail if you want to see it.

Shallow well setups use a different style, a small diaphragm valve tied into the pump's suction line that meters air into the system on startup. Either way, the AVC is a wear part. It's a small mechanical valve sitting in well water, and hard, mineral-heavy groundwater, common in this region, does it no favors. Boshart's page names iron buildup clogging the valve as the usual failure, along with the float rod corroding off inside the tank after years in aggressive water.

## Why Galvanized Tanks Waterlog (and Sometimes Go the Other Way)

"Waterlogged" is the term for what happens when a galvanized tank loses too much of its air cushion. Water fills nearly the whole shell, there's almost no compressible air left, and the practical effect is that the pump starts and stops constantly, sometimes every few seconds, because there's no cushion left to smooth out the pressure swing. That rapid pump cycling is hard on the motor, the pressure switch contacts and the check valve, on top of being loud and annoying.

The opposite failure, "air-bound," happens less often but is worth knowing: if the AVC lets in too much air, the tank stores less usable water than it should and you'll notice pressure dropping off fast between pump cycles. Either failure traces back to the same cause: the air volume control isn't holding the balance it's supposed to hold anymore.

## Signs Your Galvanized Tank Needs Attention

A few signs point specifically at the tank and its air control rather than the pump itself:

1. The pump cycles on and off in quick bursts, even with no fixture running.
2. Water pressure surges and drops noticeably as you use it, instead of holding steady.
3. You hear hissing from the AVC's vent port that never used to be there, or hissing that's stopped when it used to be normal.
4. The tank feels heavy and solid all the way up when you thump the shell, with no hollow, air-filled sound near the top.
5. The pressure gauge cycles between cut-in and cut-out faster than it used to, over a period of weeks or months.

None of these prove the tank is the problem on their own. A pressure switch on its way out or a check valve that's stuck can produce some of the same symptoms. ## Bladder and Diaphragm Tanks: What Replaced the Air Volume Control

Almost every pressure tank sold new today is a bladder or diaphragm design, and the difference from a galvanized tank is simple: air and water never touch. A rubber diaphragm or a separate bladder physically divides the tank into an air side and a water side, so there's no dissolving air charge to babysit and no AVC to clog. A [university extension comparison of pressure tank designs](https://extensionpubs.unl.edu/publication/g2152/2012/html/view) explains that in a plain steel tank water absorbs some of the air, which has to be replaced to keep the tank from waterlogging, while a bladder design prevents air and water contact, so no air injection or air release device is needed.

That doesn't mean these tanks need zero attention. The air side still needs to be at the right precharge pressure, and that number matters more than people expect: a [manufacturer's own installation and maintenance instructions for a widely used bladder tank line](https://fwwebbimage.fwwebb.com/ProductInfo/9016868_MC10185_09_16_WXT_IO.pdf) call for setting precharge about 2 psi below your pressure switch's cut-in setting, checked with the tank fully drained of water. If you've never checked yours, we've covered [how to check your tank's air pressure and what to set it at](/blog/pressure-tank-air-pressure/) in its own post.

## Bladder vs Diaphragm: The Practical Difference

Both designs solve the same problem, but they're built differently and that changes what happens when they eventually fail.

| | Diaphragm tank | Bladder tank |
|---|---|---|
| How it separates air and water | A rubber diaphragm fixed inside the shell, splitting it into an air chamber and a water chamber | A separate rubber bladder holds the water, suspended inside the steel shell |
| If the rubber fails | The diaphragm is sealed into the tank; the whole tank typically gets replaced | Some models are sold with a replaceable bladder, which can avoid a full tank swap |
| What you'll notice on failure | Waterlogging symptoms return, similar to a failed galvanized AVC | Same symptoms; whether it's fixable without a full tank swap depends on the specific model |

A [manufacturer's own product specifications for diaphragm and bladder models](https://www.amtrol.com/product/well-x-trol-pressure-tanks/) is a reasonable place to see how a given line describes this trade-off, though the exact construction and replaceable-bladder options vary by brand and model, so it's worth checking the specific tank in front of you rather than assuming.

## Living With a Galvanized Tank in the Meantime

Not every galvanized tank needs to come out today. If yours is cycling normally and the AVC is doing its job, there's no reason to rush a working system. A few things are worth doing in the meantime:

- Listen for the AVC's normal hissing when the pump runs, and note if that sound changes or disappears.
- Watch your pump's cycling pattern over a few weeks. A gradual shift toward shorter, more frequent cycles is the earliest sign of a slipping air charge.
- Some older galvanized tanks use a floating wafer to separate air from water instead of an AVC. The extension comparison cited above says those need air recharged by hand every six to twelve months, so check your owner's documentation or ask a well contractor if you're not sure which type you have.
- Don't ignore rapid short cycling. It's hard on the pump motor and the pressure switch, and the fix, whether it's an AVC repair or a tank swap, is cheaper before something else fails alongside it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a galvanized pressure tank be repaired instead of replaced?

Sometimes. If the tank itself isn't rusted through and the only failure is the air volume control clogging or corroding, replacing just the AVC can restore normal cycling for a fraction of what a new tank costs. But if the AVC has failed repeatedly, or the tank shell shows rust or pitting, repeated patching becomes a losing game. A diagnosis at the tank tee will tell you which situation you're actually in.

### How long do galvanized pressure tanks usually last?

There's no single number that applies to every tank, since it depends on water chemistry, how well the AVC has been maintained, and how hard the system cycles. What's fair to say is that galvanized tanks are steel vessels holding water under pressure for years at a stretch, and internal corrosion eventually catches up with them even when the air control keeps working. If yours is original to an older home, it's worth having it looked at rather than assuming it will keep going indefinitely.

### Is a galvanized tank unsafe to keep using?

A galvanized tank that's cycling properly isn't inherently dangerous, but a badly waterlogged one puts constant stress on the pump, the pressure switch and every fitting on the tank tee, and a tank shell that's corroded internally can eventually develop a leak or a weak spot. If you notice rust-colored water, a leak at the shell itself, or cycling that's gotten dramatically worse, that's worth a service call rather than waiting it out.

### Do bladder or diaphragm tanks ever waterlog like a galvanized tank does?

Not for the same reason. Because the rubber diaphragm or bladder physically keeps air and water apart, there's no dissolving air charge to lose over time. What does happen is the air side losing precharge pressure slowly through the valve stem or a pinhole in the rubber, which produces similar symptoms, short cycling and uneven pressure, even though the underlying cause is different from a galvanized tank's air volume control failing.
