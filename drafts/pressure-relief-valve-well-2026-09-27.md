# The Pressure Relief Valve on Your Well Tank: What It Does and Why It Drips

Look at the tee of pipe fittings where your pressure tank meets the plumbing and you will usually find three or four things threaded together: a pressure gauge, a pressure switch, a drain, and a small valve with a short pipe pointed down at the floor. That last one is the pressure relief valve. It is the smallest part on the tee and the one homeowners notice least, right up until it starts dripping. Then it is the only thing anyone wants to talk about.

This is a plain explanation of what that valve does, where it belongs, why it drips, and why you should never plug it, cap it, or wrap it in tape to make the dripping stop.

## What a Pressure Relief Valve Actually Does

A pressure relief valve is a small spring loaded valve. Under normal pressure it stays shut and does nothing at all. It only opens once the pressure at that point in the piping climbs past the valve's rated set point, and when it opens, it dumps water out through its own short discharge pipe until the pressure drops back down.

Manufacturer installation manuals for residential pressure tanks are blunt about why it exists. [Amtrol's installation and operation instructions](https://fwwebbimage.fwwebb.com/ProductInfo/9016868_MC10185_09_16_WXT_IO.pdf) for its Well-X-Trol tanks state that a relief valve "should be installed which is set to open at excessive pressures" and that this "will protect the Well-X-Trol and other system components should the [pressure switch](/blog/pressure-switch-problems/) malfunction and fail to shut the pump off." In other words, the relief valve is not there for everyday operation. It is there for the one day your pressure switch does not do its job.

## Where It Sits on Your System

The relief valve is not off on its own somewhere. It threads into the same tank tee or street tee as the pressure switch and pressure gauge, right at the point where the tank connects to the rest of the plumbing. Manufacturer piping diagrams for both jet pump and submersible pump setups show it in that same cluster of fittings, as close to the tank as the switch itself, so it sees the same pressure the switch and gauge see at all times.

Installation manuals are specific about the discharge side too. The valve should point down, the discharge opening has to stay clear, and no other valve or obstruction can sit between the relief valve and the tank, or anywhere in its discharge line. If your [pressure tank replacement](/pressure-tank-replacement-wenatchee/) is happening in a pump house or a crawlspace where that discharge line runs to the outside, it is also worth [insulating the space against hard freezes](/blog/pump-house-insulation/): a discharge line plugged solid with ice cannot do its job any better than one that has been capped on purpose.

## What It Is Protecting Against: A Stuck Pressure Switch

To understand the relief valve, it helps to understand the part it is backing up. Your pressure switch is what starts and stops the pump. [Nebraska Extension's guide to private well mechanical components](https://extensionpubs.unl.edu/publication/g2153/2012/html/view) explains the cycle plainly: "When pressure in the water system drops to a predetermined set point, often 30 or 40 pounds per square inch (psi), the pressure switch tells the pump to start working," and the pump then runs until the tank reaches a stop pressure "usually about 20 psi higher than the start level, often 50 or 60 psi." A 30/50 or 40/60 switch is the norm on most residential systems.

That cycle depends on the switch's contacts opening cleanly at cutout, every time. Contacts pit and wear with age, and once in a while they weld or stick shut instead of opening. When that happens, the pump does not stop at 50 or 60 psi. It keeps running, and pressure keeps climbing, because nothing else in the system is telling the motor to quit. Pressure tanks are rated for a maximum working pressure, commonly somewhere in the 100 to 150 psi range depending on the model, and a pump that will not shut off can push pressure past that number. That is the exact failure the relief valve exists to catch. If you are chasing a switch that will not cut off cleanly, our [guide to pressure switch problems](/blog/pressure-switch-problems/) covers the troubleshooting; we will not repeat it here.

## Why the Valve Drips

A relief valve that is doing its job under normal conditions should be dry. It is built to stay shut at the pressures your system runs at day to day and only crack open once pressure goes above that. So when you find water dripping or weeping from it, that is the valve telling you the pressure at the tee reached its set point, at least for a moment. The drip is not the problem. It is a symptom.

A few things commonly cause it:

- **A pressure switch cutout that has crept up, or contacts that are starting to stick.** Say your gauge normally cuts the pump off at 60 psi, but lately it is running to 65 or 70 before it stops. The relief valve set well above that will not open on every cycle, but it may start weeping on the cycles where the switch is slowest to respond.
- **A [waterlogged pressure tank](/blog/pressure-tank-waterlogged/).** When a tank loses its air precharge, the pump short cycles and there is little air cushion left to absorb pressure changes, so sharp swings at the tee are more likely. Combined with a switch that is slow to cut out, that can be enough to make an aging relief valve weep.
- **[Water hammer](/blog/water-hammer-well-system/) from a fixture or valve elsewhere in the house closing fast.** A sharp pressure spike traveling back through the piping can trip the relief valve for a moment even on an otherwise healthy system.
- **A relief valve that is simply worn out.** Springs weaken, seats corrode, and a valve that used to hold until 100 psi can start seeping at normal operating pressure. At that point the fix is a new valve, not a diagnosis of everything else on the tee.

None of these are things to guess at from the drip alone. A gauge reading, a look at the switch, and a check of the tank precharge will usually sort out which one you are dealing with.

## Never Plug It

This is the one rule that matters more than any other in this article: do not plug, cap, tape over, or otherwise block the discharge on a pressure relief valve, even temporarily, even if the drip is driving you crazy.

Manufacturer installation instructions are not subtle about this. [A.O. Smith's diaphragm well tank installation manual](https://www.aosmithatlowes.com/media/1390/aosmith_well-tank-manual-100305320.pdf) states flatly that the discharge opening "must not be blocked or reduced in size under any circumstances," and that no valve or other obstruction is to be placed between the relief valve and the tank or anywhere in the discharge line. Amtrol's manual puts its relief valve requirement under the heading "explosion or rupture hazard." That language is there because the relief valve is the only pressure escape route your system has if the pressure switch fails. Block that outlet and you have removed the one safeguard standing between a stuck switch and a tank that has nowhere left to release pressure.

## Relief Valve, Pressure Switch, and Precharge: How They Work Together

These three parts share one job: keeping the system somewhere between "enough pressure to run the house" and "too much pressure for the tank to handle safely." Here is roughly how the numbers line up on a typical residential setup, based on manufacturer installation guidance and Nebraska Extension's description of pressure switch operation.

| Component | What it does | Typical range |
|---|---|---|
| Pressure switch | Starts the pump at cut-in, stops it at cut-out | 30/50 psi or 40/60 psi are the common factory settings |
| Tank precharge (air side) | Cushions each cycle and sets usable drawdown | Set about 2 psi below the switch's cut-in point, empty and at ambient temperature |
| Pressure relief valve | Opens only if pressure exceeds a safe limit, to protect the tank and piping | Set well above normal cutout and no higher than the tank's rated maximum; Amtrol's residential manual calls for 125 psi or more on a 150 psi tank, and A.O. Smith's for 150 psi or less depending on model |
| Tank maximum working pressure | The ceiling the whole system is designed never to reach | Commonly 100 to 150 psi depending on tank model, per the manufacturer's data plate |

Notice the gap between a normal 60 psi cutout and a relief valve set well above it. That gap is intentional. It gives the switch room to do its job on every normal cycle without the relief valve ever opening, while still leaving a hard ceiling in place if the switch ever fails to do that job.

## Maintenance Checklist and When to Call

A relief valve is cheap insurance and it does not ask for much. A short annual look keeps it, and the parts around it, honest.

1. Watch the gauge on a normal cycle and note where the pump actually cuts off. Compare that to what your switch is rated for.
2. Check the tank's air precharge once a year with the system drained and the tank empty, following the [correct precharge and setting process](/blog/pressure-tank-air-pressure/) rather than guessing.
3. Look at the relief valve's discharge opening. It should be clear, pointed down or away from foot traffic, and never capped, taped, or piped into a dead end.
4. If the valve drips once, right after someone slams a hose bib shut or a hydrant closes fast, that is more likely water hammer than a failing switch. A drip that keeps recurring on its own is different.
5. If the gauge is climbing past the normal cutout, or the valve is dripping steadily rather than occasionally, shut off the breaker to the pump and verify it is dead with a meter before doing anything else, then call for service.
6. Replace a relief valve that looks corroded, has started seeping at normal pressure, or has been in place for a long time without being checked. A new valve costs far less than dealing with the alternative.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a pressure relief valve on a well system?

It is a small spring loaded valve threaded into the piping next to your pressure tank, usually on the same tee as the pressure switch and gauge. Under normal pressure it stays fully closed and does nothing. It only opens if pressure in that part of the system climbs above its set point, at which point it discharges water to relieve the excess. Its job is narrow and specific: back up the pressure switch if the switch ever fails to shut the pump off before pressure climbs into a range the tank was not built to handle.

### Why does my well tank's relief valve keep dripping?

A properly functioning relief valve should stay dry during normal operation, so a drip means pressure at the tee reached the valve's set point at least briefly. Common causes include a pressure switch that is cutting off later than it should, a waterlogged tank that has lost its air precharge and is short cycling with sharper pressure swings, water hammer from a fixture closing fast elsewhere in the house, or a relief valve that has simply worn out and is now opening below its rated pressure. A gauge reading and a look at the switch and precharge usually narrow it down.

### Can I plug the pressure relief valve if it will not stop dripping?

No. Manufacturer installation instructions are explicit that the discharge opening must never be blocked, capped, or reduced in size, and that no valve or obstruction should ever sit between the relief valve and the tank or in its discharge line. The relief valve is the only pressure escape route if your pressure switch ever fails to shut the pump off. Plugging it removes that safeguard entirely, which turns a minor annoyance into a real risk to the tank, the piping, and anyone standing nearby if pressure keeps climbing with nowhere to go.

### Where should the pressure relief valve be installed on my system?

It belongs on the same tank tee or street tee as the pressure switch and gauge, right where the tank connects to the rest of the plumbing, so it sees the same pressure those parts see at every moment. Manufacturer piping diagrams show it positioned to discharge downward with a clear, unobstructed opening, never behind another valve. If that discharge line runs through an unheated space, keep it protected from freezing too. A relief valve encased in ice is just as unable to do its job as one that has been physically capped.
