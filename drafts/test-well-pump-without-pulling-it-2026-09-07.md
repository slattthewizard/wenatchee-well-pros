# How to Test a Well Pump Without Pulling It Out of the Well

Pulling a submersible pump out of a 300-foot well takes a hoist, a truck, two people and most of a morning. Doing all of that and finding a healthy pump on the end of the pipe, because the real problem was a $200 pressure switch, is a bad day for everyone standing at the wellhead.

That is why learning how to test a well pump without pulling it is worth an hour of your time. Almost every no-water call has a set of surface checks that come first, and they run in order of cost: breaker, switch, tank, wiring, then the pump itself. Most of the time the answer shows up long before anybody reaches for the hoist.

This guide covers what you can safely check yourself, what a technician checks with a meter, what each result usually means, and the point where testing stops and the pump has to come up.

## Why You Test a Well Pump Without Pulling It First

A submersible pump hangs at the bottom of the well on drop pipe and wire. Bringing it up means clear access over the wellhead, a spooler or hoist, and a few uninterrupted hours. On a 220-foot well that is routine work. On a 500-foot well up in the foothills it is a longer, more expensive day.

The pump is also the least likely part to have failed. Across our service area, most no-water calls end at the pressure switch, the pressure tank, the control box, a tripped breaker, a frozen line, or a well that has drawn down in a dry August. Surface testing finds all of those before anyone touches the pipe.

There is a second reason worth naming. The surface tests do not just tell you whether the pump is dead. They tell you why, which decides whether the replacement lasts twenty years or fails the same way in three. A pump that burned out from low voltage on a long undersized wire run will burn out again if only the pump gets replaced.

## The Five-Minute Checks Anyone Can Do

Start at the electrical panel. Well pumps run on a double-pole 240-volt breaker, and a breaker can trip on one leg while still looking closed. Push it firmly to off, then back to on. If it trips again immediately, stop. Repeated resets on a faulted circuit are how a pump motor gets cooked and how a house fire starts.

Next, look for a separate disconnect or a switch on the pressure tank line. Many systems have one, and it gets bumped, or shut off during work in the pump house and never turned back on. It is a two-second check that has ended more than one emergency call.

Then walk the house. Turn on a fixture at the far end and a hose bib outside. If one fixture is dry and everything else works, the well is fine and the problem is between the manifold and that fixture. Check whether a water softener, filter housing or irrigation zone valve has been left in bypass or closed.

Finally, listen at the pressure tank with the water running. A working system makes noise: the pump kicks in, the gauge climbs, the switch clicks off. Silence with a dead gauge is a different problem from a pump that hums and never starts.

## Reading the Pressure Gauge and Pressure Tank

The gauge on the pressure tank is the single most informative thing on a private well system, and reading it correctly answers half the questions people call about.

Open a tap and watch the needle. It should fall to the cut-in pressure, usually 30 or 40 psi, then hold steady while the pump runs and climb back to cut-out, usually 50 or 60 psi. That cycle is the system working as designed.

A needle sitting at zero with the pump silent means no water is being delivered at all: a power, switch, motor or supply problem. A needle stuck at pressure while taps run dry usually means a closed valve or a blockage downstream of the tank, not a pump fault.

Now check the tank itself. Rap your knuckles down the side. A healthy bladder tank sounds hollow in the top third and dull toward the bottom. If it sounds solid all the way up, it is waterlogged, and the pump will short cycle, clicking on and off every few seconds. That kills motors fast. Our guide to [a waterlogged pressure tank](/blog/pressure-tank-waterlogged/) walks through the air-charge check in detail.

An orchard house above Cashmere came to us running a submersible and a 44-gallon bladder tank that had gone completely solid. The pump was cycling roughly every eight seconds under a running shower. The pump itself tested fine on amps, and a tank replacement in the $600 to $1,800 range solved it. Left alone another season, that cycling would very likely have taken the pump with it, which is a job several times the price.

## Electrical Tests That Find a Bad Pump Without Removing It

This is where a homeowner's testing ends and a technician's begins. The terminals inside a pressure switch and a control box carry 240 volts, and the tests below are done with power live. If you are not trained on live electrical work, this section is for understanding what your technician is doing, not a to-do list.

**Voltage at the pressure switch.** Confirms power is actually reaching the switch and passing through the contacts. Pitted, burned or welded contacts are extremely common and cheap to fix.

**Amp draw against the motor nameplate.** A clamp meter on one conductor while the pump runs shows whether the motor is loafing, working normally, or straining. High amps point to a worn pump end, binding sediment or low voltage. Very low amps or zero can mean an open winding or a broken wire. We cover the readings in [well pump amp draw testing](/blog/well-pump-amp-draw-testing/).

**Insulation resistance to ground.** A megohmmeter reads how well the motor windings and the wire down the well are still insulated. A reading that has collapsed toward zero means the motor or the drop cable has failed, and that is the test that most often justifies pulling the pump.

**Control box checks on three-wire systems.** Capacitors and relays fail far more often than motors do, and they sit up at the surface. A pump that hums but will not start is a classic control box or capacitor symptom, covered in [control box troubleshooting](/blog/well-pump-control-box-troubleshooting/).

A 1990s manufactured home on a 220-foot well outside East Wenatchee lost water overnight with a dead gauge and a breaker that held. Voltage was present at the switch and absent past the contacts, which were badly pitted. The pressure switch replacement landed inside the $150 to $350 range and the pump, which the owner had already been quoted to replace, was never touched.

## The Drawdown Test: Pump Problem or Well Problem

If the motor runs but little or no water arrives, the next question is whether the pump is failing or the well is. These are different problems with very different price tags.

A technician runs water at a known rate and watches how pressure behaves over ten to twenty minutes. Pressure that holds steady and then collapses after several minutes usually means the well is being pumped down faster than it recovers, which is a yield issue, not a pump issue. Pressure that never builds at all points to the pump end, a failed check valve, or a break in the drop pipe. The USGS explains the basics of how wells draw from an aquifer in its [groundwater wells overview](https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/groundwater-wells).

Static water level can also be sounded from the surface with a water level meter through the well cap vent, on wells built to allow it. Comparing today's level to the level on the original well log tells you whether the water table has dropped or the pump is simply no longer reaching it.

A cabin near Leavenworth left unheated over Christmas showed the opposite pattern. The pump ran, amps were normal, and not a drop reached the house. The fault was in the buried line above the pitless adapter, not in the well, and it fell into the wire or drop pipe repair range of $300 to $1,200. For the symptom pattern in general, see [well pump runs but no water](/blog/well-pump-runs-but-no-water/).

## When Testing Ends and the Pump Has to Come Up

Surface testing has a hard limit. When insulation resistance to ground has failed, when there is no continuity through the motor windings, or when confirmed voltage at the wellhead produces no motor at all, the fault is below the water line and no meter at the surface will tell you more.

At that point the job needs a hoist, a clear working area, and a water outage window of several hours. It is also work that Washington regulates: well construction and pump installation fall under the Department of Ecology's [water well rules](https://ecology.wa.gov/water-shorelines/water-supply/wells), and the well log for your property is the reference for depth, casing and original pump setting.

The useful thing is that by the time you get there, the testing has already paid for itself. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I test a well pump myself without any tools?

Yes, up to a point. You can check the breaker and any disconnect, watch the pressure gauge through a full cycle, listen for the pump starting, tap the pressure tank for a waterlogged sound, and confirm whether the problem affects every fixture or just one. Those checks solve or narrow down a large share of no-water calls. Anything past that involves live 240-volt terminals and belongs to someone trained for it.

### How can a technician tell if the pump is bad without pulling it?

Mainly through electrical readings taken at the surface. Amp draw compared to the motor nameplate shows whether the motor is straining or open, and an insulation resistance test shows whether the windings or the drop cable have failed. Those two readings, combined with how pressure behaves during a timed run, identify most pump failures before anything is lifted out of the casing.

### Does a pump that runs but delivers no water always need replacing?

No, and this is one of the most common false alarms we see. A running motor with no flow can mean a failed check valve, a break in the drop pipe, a frozen or split line between the well and the house, or a well drawn down below the pump intake. Each of those is a different repair, and several are far cheaper than a new pump. Testing tells you which one you have.
