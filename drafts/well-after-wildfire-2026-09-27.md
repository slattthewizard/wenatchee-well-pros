# Well Water After Wildfire: What to Check Before You Turn the Pump Back On

Fire season in Chelan, Douglas and Grant counties can leave a well system standing but hurt in ways you can't see from the yard. The casing might look fine. The pump house might still have four walls. And the water coming out of the tap can still be unsafe. Heat, ash and a few days without power do more damage to a well system than most homeowners expect, and the fixes are not always obvious.

This is a walk through what a wildfire actually does to a well, what to check before you flip the breaker back on, and what to test for before anyone drinks the water.

## What a Wildfire Actually Does to a Well System

A well system has more exposed parts than people picture: the wellhead and cap, the wiring that runs from the panel to the pump, the above-ground pipe leading into the house, the pressure tank, and any pump house or shed around all of it. Fire and heat can hit every piece of that differently.

The Washington State Department of Health's wildfire guidance tells homeowners to check for [damage to the electrical wires and connectors that supply power to the well](https://doh.wa.gov/community-and-environment/drinking-water/wildfires-and-safe-drinking-water), above-ground PVC pipe bringing water into the home, well house equipment, and pressure tanks that may have been damaged by exposure to heat even without direct flame contact. Storage tanks, vents and overflow pipes are on the same list.

The part that surprises most people is plastic. PVC and poly pipe, wire insulation and plastic fittings do not need to burn to fail. Heat alone can soften, warp or crack them, and the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) notes that degraded plastic piping can release volatile organic compounds (VOCs) into the water it carries, on top of any physical leak. That means a pipe that looks intact from a few feet away can still be compromised.

## Power and Electrical Safety First

Before you touch anything at the well, shut the breaker off at the panel and verify it is dead with a meter. Do not assume power is safe just because the lights are on somewhere in the house, and do not assume it is off just because a breaker looks tripped. Fire can damage wiring in ways that make a circuit behave unpredictably.

Once the breaker is confirmed dead, look at the wiring that runs to the pump and control box for melted insulation, scorching, or exposed conductor. If you see any of that, or if the panel itself took heat or smoke damage, this is electrical work for a licensed electrician, not a DIY fix. We work on the well side (the pump, the wiring from the wellhead to the control box, the pressure switch and tank), but anything inside a live panel or a damaged main service needs its own qualified pro.

If the outage has left you without power for the well at all, a portable generator can run the pump temporarily, sized correctly for the pump's starting load. ## Check for Pressure Loss Before You Drink Anything

Even if the well and wiring look fine, do this simple check before anyone drinks the water: turn on a faucet in the house and watch how it flows. If the water spurts, sputters, or comes out mixed with air instead of running smoothly, the system has lost pressure somewhere. [CDC guidance on private wells after a wildfire](https://www.cdc.gov/environmental-health-services/php/water/private-wells-after-a-wildfire.html) points to spurting water specifically as a sign of pressure loss in the well and household plumbing, and pressure loss is how outside contaminants, not just ash on the surface, can find a path into the system.

Pressure loss doesn't tell you what got in, only that something could have. Treat any pressure loss as a reason to stop drinking the water and move straight to testing, covered below, rather than trying to diagnose it by taste or smell.

## Ash, Runoff, and the Wellhead: How Contamination Actually Gets In

A wellhead is designed to keep surface water out: a tight seal at the cap, a screened vent instead of an open one, and a grout seal running down the casing. Fire and firefighting activity around a well pad, heavy equipment, hose lines, or a scorched and warped cap, can all compromise that seal without an obvious hole appearing anywhere.

Once that seal is compromised, ash-laden runoff from burned hillsides is exactly the kind of contamination it's supposed to stop. The mechanism is similar to what we cover in our guide to a [flooded wellhead after spring runoff](/blog/flooded-wellhead-spring-runoff/): surface water reaching the casing carries bacteria and sediment into a system that was built to be sealed against it. If your cap or vent looks damaged, read our piece on [well cap replacement](/blog/well-cap-replacement/) for what a sound seal actually looks like.

There's a second contamination path that has nothing to do with surface water: VOCs from heat-damaged plastic components leaching directly into the water inside the pipe. An EPA factsheet on drinking water systems after wildfires, written after utilities found elevated benzene and other VOCs in water lines following California wildfires, lays out why this matters even for a system that never took direct fire: benzene has a federal drinking water limit of 5 parts per billion, and it is not something you can smell or taste your way into confirming. Read the [EPA's guidance on VOC contamination after wildfires](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2021-09/addressing-contamination-of-drinking-water-distribution-systems-from-volatile-organic-compounds-after-wildfires_508.pdf) if you want the full technical picture; it was written for public utilities, but the mechanism applies to a private system with plastic components just the same.

## Before You Turn the Pump Back On: A Checklist

Work through this in order. Don't skip ahead to running water through the house just because the pump starts.

1. Shut the breaker off at the panel and verify it's dead with a meter before touching any wiring.
2. Walk the wellhead, pump house and any exposed pipe run. Look for melted or warped plastic, scorched wire insulation, and a cap or vent screen that isn't sitting flush anymore.
3. Check the pressure tank and the fittings on its tank tee (gauge, switch tubing, relief valve) for scorching, bulging, or melted parts. Heat can affect a tank's bladder and fittings even if the tank itself never caught fire.
4. If everything visible checks out, restore power and turn on one faucet before using water normally. Watch for spurting or air in the line, which signals pressure loss.
5. If you saw damaged wiring, a warped cap, melted pipe, or pressure loss at any step, stop and don't drink the water. Call for an inspection and get a water sample tested before resuming normal use.
6. If nothing looks damaged and pressure is normal, still test the water before drinking it. Fire damage upstream of your property, or a seal failure you can't see, won't always show up as a symptom.

## What to Test For, and When

Washington's guidance is direct on the minimum: test for coliform bacteria before resuming normal use, and if the well is free of nitrate, boiling tap water for one full minute is an acceptable stopgap while you wait on lab results, since boiling concentrates nitrate rather than removing it. CDC guidance adds a second track for anywhere fire damage was more than cosmetic: broader lab methods that screen for a range of volatile and semi-volatile organic compounds, plus metals like lead and copper where synthetic pipe was involved or heat exposure was significant nearby.

| Situation | Minimum test | Consider adding |
|---|---|---|
| No visible damage, water looks and smells normal | Coliform bacteria | Nitrate, especially near livestock or a septic system |
| Melted, warped or scorched plastic pipe or fittings | Coliform bacteria, plus a VOC/SVOC panel | Lead and copper |
| Ash-heavy runoff reached the wellhead or pad | Coliform bacteria | A second round of testing a few weeks later |
| Faucet spurts or loses pressure | Coliform bacteria, don't drink until cleared | A fuller contaminant panel if the loss was significant |

Use a lab certified for drinking water testing, not a home test strip, for any of this. A strip can tell you hardness or a rough pH. It can't confirm bacteria or VOCs at the levels that matter here.

## Protecting Your Well System Before the Next Fire Season

Once things are repaired and tested clean, a few habits make the next fire season less costly. Keep dry brush, debris and stored fuel cleared away from the pump house and wellhead itself, not just the house. A wellhead cap should stay vented and screened, not sealed airtight; a vented, screened cap is correct and doesn't need to be swapped for a solid one out of caution. If your pump house has exposed wiring runs, metal conduit (installed by whoever does your electrical work) protects them from heat better than bare cable clipped to a wall stud.

None of this stops a fire that reaches the property. It does reduce how much of the well system takes damage when smoke and heat move through without direct flame contact.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it safe to drink well water right after a wildfire?

Not until you've checked for pressure loss and tested the water. Washington's guidance calls for testing at least for coliform bacteria before resuming normal use, and boiling tap water for one minute is a reasonable stopgap only if the well is free of nitrate, since boiling concentrates nitrate instead of removing it. If your faucet spurts, sputters or loses pressure, treat that as a sign contamination could have entered and don't drink the water until a certified lab clears it. Our guide on [what to test for in North Central Washington well water](/blog/is-well-water-safe-to-drink/) covers the broader testing picture beyond fire damage.

### How do I know if ash or runoff got into my well?

Look at the wellhead cap and vent screen for heat damage or a seal that isn't sitting flush, and run a faucet to check for spurting or air in the line, which signals lost pressure somewhere in the system. Cloudy, ashy or off-smelling water is another sign, though water can carry contamination without any visible or noticeable change. When in doubt, test rather than judge it by appearance, the same approach we cover for a wellhead exposed to spring runoff.
