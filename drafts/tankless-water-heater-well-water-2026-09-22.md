# Tankless Water Heater on Well Water: Sediment, Scale and Pressure Swings

Your tankless water heater doesn't care that you're on a well. It heats whatever comes through the pipe, scale, sand and all, and in Chelan, Douglas and Grant counties that pipe often carries hard water. A tankless water heater on well water works fine in plenty of houses. It fails early in others, and when it does, look at the water before you blame the heater.

We're a well and pump company, not a plumber, and we don't install water heaters. But three well-side problems cause most of the grief with these units: scale, sediment and pressure swings. We handle the pressure side, and we can tell you whether sand is coming up from the well. Sort all three out before the heater goes in, or catch them early if it's already installed, and the heater has a fair shot at a full life. Ignore them and you'll be calling your plumber every year about a unit that was sold as the long-life upgrade.

## Why Tankless Water Heaters Struggle on Well Water

A tank water heater buys itself time. Forty or fifty gallons sit there getting reheated slowly, so a little scale or a little grit in the water doesn't matter for years. A tankless unit gets no such cushion. Water crosses the heat exchanger, the coiled tubing where the actual heating happens, in a matter of seconds, and the unit fires on demand every time a tap opens.

That changes what well water problems do to it. Scale lands directly on the exchanger instead of settling harmlessly at the bottom of a tank. Sediment hits the inlet screen and the flow sensor, the part that tells the unit water is moving, right away instead of over years. Pressure and flow that a tank heater never notices become temperature swings a person in the shower feels immediately, because the unit is reacting in real time to whatever is coming through the pipe.

None of that is a reason to skip a tankless heater on a well. It's a reason to fix the well side first. ## Scale: The North Central Washington Well Water Problem

A lot of groundwater in this part of the state runs hard, and [hard well water](/blog/hard-water-well/) is the main reason a tankless heater on a well needs more attention than one on city water. Hardness is dissolved calcium and magnesium. Heat drives those minerals out of solution, and they plate onto the hottest metal surface they touch, which in a tankless unit is the heat exchanger. The [USGS explains the chemistry](https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/hardness-water) if you want the full picture.

Scale narrows the exchanger's internal passages a little at a time. Less water touches hot metal per pass, so the unit has to work harder to hit your set temperature. Eventually that shows up as reduced flow, an error code, or an exchanger that needs replacing years before it should have.

Two fixes here, and both are plumbing and water treatment work rather than well work. A softener or conditioner ahead of the heater keeps the hardness from ever reaching the exchanger, and [a softener and a conditioner aren't the same thing](/blog/water-softener-vs-conditioner-well/), so know which one you're buying. Second, the descale flush. Manufacturers call for one periodically, and on hard water that often means yearly. It's done through the isolation valves your installer should have put on the unit, with a small pump circulating white vinegar or the maker's own descaler. Follow your manual on the interval. Skip it on hard well water and the exchanger scales up years early.

## Sediment and Sand: What Clogs a Tankless Heater First

Well water carries fine sand and sediment, more of it after a new pump goes in, after any well work, or as a screen or aging casing starts to let material through. [Well water sediment](/blog/well-water-sediment/) settles at the bottom of a tank heater and mostly stays out of the way. In a tankless unit it clogs the inlet screen first, then works into the flow sensor and starts giving the unit false readings.

The symptom is usually blunt: the heater won't fire even with a tap wide open, because the sensor can't tell that water is moving. Sometimes it's subtler, short cycling or an intermittent code that clears itself and comes back a week later.

Fix it at the source, not at the heater. A sediment or spin-down filter ahead of the unit, sized for your well's grit level, catches the material before it reaches the screen ([filter options for well water](/blog/well-water-filtration-systems/)). If you're seeing grit at every fixture, not just at the heater, that's a well question, and [where sand in well water comes from](/blog/sand-in-well-water/) is worth reading before you spend money on filtration alone. A pump set too close to the bottom, or one pulling harder than the well can supply, can drag sand up with every cycle.

## Pressure Swings: Why a Tankless Heater on Well Water Runs Hot and Cold

Standard well systems cycle a pump between a cut-in and a cut-out pressure, commonly 30/50 or 40/60 psi around here. Pressure and flow are never perfectly flat, they move through that range constantly as the pump starts and stops. A tank heater never notices any of it.

A tankless heater notices every time, because the flow rate through it is what tells the unit how hard to fire. Flow changes when another fixture opens, and it changes as the pressure tank runs toward the bottom of its cycle. Each time, the heater's electronics catch up a beat late, and the person in the shower feels it. At the far end of a run, an upstairs bathroom or an orchard house well down the line from the pressure tank, low pressure can drop flow below the heater's minimum activation rate, the least flow the unit needs to sense before it fires at all. That number's different for every model, so check your unit's spec sheet rather than trust a number here.

Two well-side fixes help. A pressure tank that's the right size and correctly charged smooths out the swing between pump cycles far better than an old or [waterlogged tank](/blog/pressure-tank-waterlogged/) ever will. For a house that really needs flatter pressure than a standard tank delivers, a [constant pressure well system](/blog/constant-pressure-well-systems/) holds a narrow pressure band instead of cycling through a wide one, which a tankless heater tends to like a lot better. Before you spend money on either, rule out the plainer causes covered in our [low water pressure guide](/blog/low-water-pressure-well/), because sometimes the fix is smaller than a new tank.

## Iron, Manganese and Air: The Smaller Well-Side Culprits

Some wells carry iron or manganese, and they leave their own mark: reddish-brown or black-gray staining in sinks and tubs, and deposits that build inside plumbing and the heat exchanger much the way scale does. If you see that staining on fixtures or laundry, test for both before you assume the problem is only hardness, because the treatment is different. [Iron in well water](/blog/iron-in-well-water/) covers the options.

Air is the one people miss. After a pump swap, a pressure tank replacement, or any work at the wellhead, air works its way through the lines for a while. A tankless heater can read that as unsteady flow and throw an error code that has nothing to do with the heater. Run the taps until the sputtering stops ([why well lines spit air](/blog/air-in-water-lines/)) before you call the manufacturer about a fault.

One outage note while we're here. A gas tankless heater still needs electricity to fire, and so does your well pump, so a power cut takes hot water down on a well whichever heater you pick. Don't count on a gas unit as your outage backup. The bigger plan is in [keeping water on a well through a power outage](/blog/power-outage-well-water-backup-plan/).

## What Your Warranty Says About Well Water

Read the water quality section of your tankless heater's warranty before you buy it, not after something fails. A lot of manufacturers set limits on hardness or total dissolved solids, or require a softener or conditioner installed as a condition of coverage, and some exclude scale damage outright regardless of what caused it. We won't quote a brand or a number here because those limits vary by manufacturer and by model, and they change from one product line to the next.

Ask your plumber or the dealer to show you that section in writing before the unit goes on the wall. If it names a hardness limit, get your water tested against it first. A lab number in hand beats an argument with a warranty department three years from now.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need a water softener to run a tankless water heater on well water?

Not always, but if your water tests hard, which is common across Chelan, Douglas and Grant counties, a softener or conditioner ahead of the heater is the best protection the heat exchanger can get. Some manufacturers require one as a warranty condition, so check that before you decide it's optional. A plumber or water treatment company can size one to your actual hardness number.

### Why does my tankless water heater run cold for a second when someone flushes a toilet or starts a load of laundry?

That's usually a flow and pressure problem on the well side, not the heater failing. When another fixture opens, flow through the heater changes and the unit reacts to it in real time, unlike a tank heater that already has forty or fifty gallons hot and waiting. A correctly sized and charged pressure tank, or a constant pressure system in houses that need one, usually fixes it.

### Can sediment in well water damage a tankless water heater?

Yes, and it tends to be the first thing that goes wrong. Sediment clogs the inlet screen and can foul the flow sensor, which can stop the heater from firing at all or make it cycle oddly even though the unit itself is fine. A sediment or spin-down filter ahead of the heater, sized for how much grit your well produces, is the usual fix.

### Does a tankless water heater need more pressure from my well system than a tank heater does?

It needs steadier pressure more than it needs higher pressure. A tank heater tolerates the normal swing between a well pump's cut-in and cut-out pressure without you ever noticing. A tankless heater reacts to that same swing as a change in flow, which you feel as a change in water temperature, so the fix is usually about smoothing the pressure band rather than raising the pressure itself.
