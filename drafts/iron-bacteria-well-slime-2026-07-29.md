## Iron Bacteria in Well Water vs. Iron in Well Water: How to Tell Them Apart

Homeowners mix these two up constantly, and it costs them money every time. Plain iron in well water is a mineral, either dissolved (clear water that turns rusty after sitting out) or already oxidized (water tinted orange right at the tap). Either way, it's a chemistry problem, and the fix is a filter matched to the form. We walk through both of those forms in our guide to [iron in well water](/blog/iron-in-well-water/), including what a lab test should show and which filter fits which reading.

Iron bacteria is a different animal. It's a microorganism that feeds on the iron in your water and leaves behind a reddish-brown biofilm as waste. No filter cartridge stops a living colony from growing back. You're not filtering out a mineral, you're dealing with an infestation, and the fix has to kill it, not strain it.

A few field tests separate the two quickly:

- **The bucket test.** Fill a white bucket and let it sit overnight. Iron alone turns the water reddish and cloudy. Iron bacteria leaves stringy clumps or a floating film, not just discolored water.
- **The smell test.** Plain iron has little odor. Iron bacteria often carries a musty, "ditch water" smell, different from the rotten-egg smell of sulfur bacteria.
- **The tank test.** Pop the lid on a rarely flushed toilet tank. Rust-colored slime on the walls is the signature sign of a live colony.

## Signs You're Dealing With Iron Bacteria, Not Just Iron

Iron bacteria tends to announce itself in a handful of predictable spots around the house before it gets bad enough to hurt your equipment.

- **Toilet tanks and back corners of the bowl.** These are quiet, low-flow reservoirs where slime builds undisturbed.
- **Sprinkler and irrigation heads.** Bacteria clogs small orifices, so heads sputter, spray unevenly, or stain the lawn and siding orange.
- **Water softener brine tanks.** Slime coats the resin bed and salt, and the softener stops working as well even though nothing else changed.
- **A musty smell that gets stronger in warm weather.** Bacteria growth speeds up as water temperature rises through summer.
- **Water that runs clear for months, then suddenly turns rusty and stays that way.** Iron alone usually stains gradually. A colony can bloom fast once it establishes.

Catch it at this stage and you're usually looking at a shock chlorination job, not a repair bill. Left alone, iron bacteria keeps building biofilm inside the casing and drop pipe until it reaches the pump, and that's where the real money starts to disappear.

## Clearing Iron Bacteria: Shock Chlorination and What Comes Next

The starting point for almost every iron bacteria case is the same disinfection process used for a positive coliform test: shock chlorination. The difference with iron bacteria is that a routine coliform-strength shock often isn't enough. Biofilm protects bacteria colonies from chlorine the way plaque protects bacteria on teeth, so a light dose can knock back the visible slime without killing the colony underneath. Heavier infestations need a stronger concentration and sometimes a repeat treatment two to three weeks later to catch what the first round missed.

## Keeping Iron Bacteria From Coming Back

Shock chlorination kills what's colonizing the well today. It doesn't change the conditions that let bacteria move in, so a few habits go a long way toward keeping it from returning.

The Foster family near Cashmere dealt with repeat infestations for two summers, shocking their well three times with the slime creeping back within a couple months each time. After the third round, they had a continuous chlorination injection system installed for about $1,800. It feeds a small, steady dose of chlorine into the line instead of relying on periodic shocking, and they haven't seen slime since. For a property with a documented history of reinfection, that upfront cost often beats paying for repeat service calls and lab fees for years.

A short list of habits helps most households avoid the problem in the first place:

- **Shock the well any time it's opened**, whether for a new pump, pressure tank swap, or cap replacement.
- **Don't leave a seasonal property closed for months without a plan.** Shock before closing up and again before reopening after a full season unused.
- **Test annually**, and don't skip the full panel. Iron bacteria thrives in the same iron-rich water that shows up on a standard test, so a routine test often flags the risk before the slime does.
- **Keep the wellhead sealed and inspected.** A cracked cap or missing vent screen is an entry point for organic matter as much as it is for surface water.

## When to Call a Professional Instead of Handling It Yourself

A first-time, mild case is often a fair DIY project if you're comfortable with the basic shock chlorination steps and your wellhead is in good shape. Several situations call for a professional instead:

- **The cap is corroded shut, buried, or sits in a pit.** Prying on stuck hardware or working in a confined space is how people get hurt.
- **The colony keeps coming back after two proper shock treatments.** You're likely looking at biofilm thick enough to need mechanical cleaning, not another bucket of bleach.
- **You're considering a continuous chlorination system.** These involve chemical feed pumps and calibration worth having installed correctly the first time.
- **Anything involves the wellhead's electrical connections.** Flipping the breaker before opening the cap is fine. Touching the wiring or pressure switch is not, since well circuits run on 240 volts.
- **Your pump's performance has already changed**, with dropping pressure or unusual noise. That's a sign the colony may have reached the intake, and pulling a pump isn't a beginner task.

Iron bacteria rarely announces itself the way a burst pipe does, but it doesn't fix itself either, and every month it sits untreated is another month it works on your pump. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I know if it's iron bacteria and not just iron in my well water?

Check a toilet tank or another low-flow fixture for reddish-brown, stringy slime or a floating oily film, since plain dissolved iron only discolors water without leaving that texture behind. A musty or swampy smell is another strong indicator, different from the rotten-egg odor of sulfur bacteria. If you're only seeing rust-colored water with no slime or smell, you're more likely dealing with ordinary iron, which is covered in our separate guide to iron in well water.

### Is iron bacteria in well water dangerous to drink?

No. Iron bacteria isn't classified as a health hazard by the EPA, and drinking water with it won't make a healthy person sick. The real cost is what it does to your equipment, since biofilm clogs pump intakes, coats pressure tank components, and can shorten the life of a submersible pump by years if it's left untreated.

### Will one shock chlorination treatment get rid of iron bacteria for good?

Sometimes, especially if you catch it early and the colony hasn't had time to build thick biofilm. Established colonies often survive a single shock because the biofilm shields bacteria from the chlorine, which is why a second treatment two to three weeks later is common practice. If slime keeps returning after two proper treatments, the well likely needs mechanical cleaning or a continuous chlorination system instead of repeated shocking.

### How much does it cost to get rid of iron bacteria in a well?

A mild, first-time case usually runs $150 to $400 for standard shock chlorination, while a heavier or repeat infestation runs $300 to $700. If biofilm has built up on the casing walls or reached the pump, mechanical cleaning or a pump pull adds $600 to $2,500 depending on what's needed. Chronic cases that keep coming back often make more financial sense with a continuous chlorination system installed for $1,200 to $2,800 rather than paying for repeat shock treatments year after year.
