## What Your Well Casing Does (and Why Damage Is a Big Deal)

Your well casing is the steel or PVC pipe that lines the borehole from the surface down to the water-bearing zone. Around here it's usually 6-inch steel, driven or grouted in when the well was drilled. Most wells in the Wenatchee Valley run 100 to 400 feet deep, and the casing might line all of that or just the upper section before the bore hits solid basalt.

The casing has three jobs. It keeps the borehole from collapsing. It keeps surface water, dirt, bacteria, and fertilizer runoff out of your drinking water. And it gives your submersible pump a clean, protected column to hang in.

When a casing cracks, rusts through, or gets crushed, all three jobs fail at once. Sediment pours in and chews up your pump. Shallow groundwater carrying whatever's on the surface, septic effluent, orchard spray residue, road salt, leaks straight into your supply. That's not a cosmetic problem. That's a health problem, which is why the [Washington Department of Ecology](https://ecology.wa.gov/water-shorelines/water-supply/wells) sets minimum well construction standards in the first place.

### Why North Central Washington is hard on casings

Our region beats up well casings in a few specific ways. The freeze-thaw cycle works on the top few feet of casing every winter. Mineral-heavy groundwater, common in Chelan, Douglas, and Grant counties, corrodes steel from the inside. Orchard irrigation keeps the soil around shallow casings wet and chemically active. And older wells were sometimes cased with thinner-wall pipe than today's code allows.

## 7 Signs Your Well Casing Is Damaged

Casing damage hides underground, but it leaves fingerprints in your water and your equipment. Watch for these.

**1. Sand or grit in your water.** The number one sign. If your aerators clog with fine sand or you feel grit in the shower, sediment is getting past the casing somewhere. Our post on [sand in well water](/blog/sand-in-well-water/) covers the other causes, but a breached casing is high on the list.

**2. Cloudy or muddy water after rain or snowmelt.** Clean casings don't care about weather. If your water turns turbid every spring melt, surface water is finding a path in through a crack or a bad seal.

**3. Sudden change in water taste or smell.** A new metallic, earthy, or chemical taste can mean shallow groundwater is mixing into your aquifer water.

**4. Coliform bacteria that keeps coming back.** You shock chlorinate, the test comes back clean, and three months later coliform is back. That pattern screams casing breach. The [EPA's private well guidance](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells) recommends annual bacteria testing for exactly this reason.

**5. Pump failures ahead of schedule.** Submersible pumps should run 10 to 15 years. If you're replacing pumps every 4 or 5 years because sand keeps destroying the impellers, fix the casing or you'll keep paying for pumps.

**6. Dropping well yield.** Sediment piling up in the bottom of the well buries the pump intake and chokes off flow.

**7. Visible damage at the wellhead.** Rust scale, a casing that wiggles, a top that's been clipped by a tractor or snowplow, or a casing that sits less than 12 inches above grade. Surface damage usually means trouble below too.

## Well Casing Repair Options Compared

There's no single fix for a damaged casing. The right repair depends on where the damage is, how bad it is, and the condition of the rest of the casing. A downhole camera inspection, usually $300 to $600, tells us which of these will actually work.

### Casing liner (sleeve)

The workhorse of well casing repair. We lower a smaller-diameter PVC or steel liner inside the existing casing, past the damaged section, and seal it. The liner bridges cracks, holes, and corroded zones in one shot. You lose a little well diameter, which occasionally means downsizing the pump, but for most residential wells it's a non-issue.

### Casing patch (point repair)

For a single, well-defined breach, an expandable patch or short sleeve can seal just that spot. Cheaper than a full liner, but only worth it if the camera shows the rest of the casing is solid. Patching one hole in a casing that's rusting everywhere is throwing money down the well.

### Top section replacement

Freeze damage, vehicle strikes, and shallow corrosion often hit just the top 5 to 20 feet. We excavate around the wellhead, cut off the bad section, and weld or thread on new casing with a proper grout seal. Often paired with a new well cap, which is its own cheap insurance. See our guide on [well cap replacement](/blog/well-cap-replacement/).

### Re-drilling or deepening

If the casing is failing along most of its length, lining it may not buy enough life. Sometimes the answer is drilling a new well next to the old one and decommissioning the original properly, which Washington law requires. We cover that process in our post on [well decommissioning in Washington](/blog/well-decommissioning-washington/).

## Repair or Replace: When a Liner Isn't Enough

Most casing problems are repairable. But sometimes we tell homeowners straight up that lining the well is a bad investment. Here's how we make the call.

**Repair makes sense when** the damage is localized, the well still produces good yield, the casing diameter can spare an inch for a liner, and the well meets current setback rules from septic systems and property lines.

**Replacement makes more sense when** the casing is corroded along most of its length, the well yield has dropped below what your household needs, the well is too shallow or too close to contamination sources to ever meet code, or the bore itself is collapsing below the casing.

Age is a factor but not the deciding one. We've lined 50-year-old wells that should run another 25 years, and we've condemned 30-year-old wells with thin-wall casing that looked like Swiss cheese on camera.

There's also a middle path. If your well is marginal and your pump is old anyway, sometimes the smart move is a liner plus a new properly sized pump, refreshing the whole system for a third of the cost of drilling. ## How to Protect Your Casing (and Catch Damage Early)

You can't stop steel from aging, but you can slow it down and catch problems while they're still cheap.

Keep 12 inches of casing above grade, more if your spot drifts snow. Grade the soil so surface water runs away from the wellhead, never toward it. Keep vehicles, livestock, and heavy equipment away from the casing. A glancing blow from a tractor bucket cracks more casings than corrosion does in orchard country.

Put a real cap on it. A cracked or loose well cap lets insects, mice, and surface water straight into the casing. Test your water every year for bacteria and nitrates, since changes in water quality are often the first sign of a breach.

And get a camera down the well every 10 years or whenever the pump comes out for service anyway. The rig is already set up, so the marginal cost is small, and the footage gives you a baseline to compare against later.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I know if my well casing is cracked?

The most reliable signs are sand or sediment in your water, cloudiness after rain or snowmelt, and bacteria tests that fail repeatedly after chlorination. The only way to confirm a crack is a downhole camera inspection, which runs $300 to $600 and shows exactly where and how bad the damage is.

### How much does well casing repair cost?

In North Central Washington, expect $800 to $2,000 for a single patch, $1,500 to $4,000 to replace a damaged top section, and $2,000 to $7,000 for a full casing liner. Depth, access, and any pump damage caused by sediment all affect the final number. A new well, by comparison, starts around $15,000.

### Can I repair a well casing myself?

No, and not just for liability reasons. Casing repair requires a pump hoist or drill rig, downhole cameras, and in Washington the work generally must be done by a licensed well contractor under Department of Ecology rules. Pulling a pump and handling casing without the right equipment is genuinely dangerous, and a botched seal can contaminate the aquifer.

### How long does a repaired well casing last?

A properly installed liner typically lasts 20 to 30 years, and a replaced top section can outlive the rest of the well. A point patch on otherwise sound casing should give you 10 to 20 years. Pair any repair with annual water testing so you'll know early if anything changes.
