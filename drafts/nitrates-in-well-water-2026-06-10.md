# Nitrates in Well Water: A Real Risk in Orchard Country

Your well water can look crystal clear, smell like nothing, and taste perfectly fine, and still carry enough nitrate to put an infant in the hospital. There's no warning at the tap. None.

That's what makes nitrates in well water different from the contaminants people usually worry about. Iron stains your sink. Sulfur smells like rotten eggs. Nitrates give you nothing to notice, and around here, in orchard and farm country from Cashmere to Moses Lake, they're one of the most common problems we see on private well tests.

The good news is this problem is completely manageable. A test costs less than a tank of gas, and proven treatment options exist at every budget. In this article we'll cover where nitrates come from, what the numbers on a test report actually mean, who's most at risk, and exactly how to fix it if your well comes back high.

## Why Nitrates in Well Water Are a Bigger Deal Here Than Most Places

Nitrate is a form of nitrogen, and nitrogen is what makes things grow. That's the whole problem. North Central Washington has been growing apples, pears, and cherries commercially for over a century, and the Columbia Basin around Quincy and Moses Lake is some of the most intensively farmed ground in the state.

Decades of fertilizer, both synthetic and manure, have left nitrogen in the soil. Irrigation water carries it down through that soil and into the aquifer. Once it's in the groundwater, it moves with the water, sometimes for miles, sometimes for decades.

Add in rural septic systems and livestock operations, and you've got three steady nitrate sources sitting on top of the same aquifers that feed our wells. Grant County sits inside the Columbia Basin Groundwater Management Area, which was created specifically because of nitrate contamination. This isn't a theoretical risk. The Washington Department of Health tracks nitrate as one of the most common contaminants in the state's groundwater, and you can read their guidance at [doh.wa.gov](https://doh.wa.gov/community-and-environment/contaminants/nitrate).

Shallow wells get hit hardest. If your well is less than 100 feet deep, sits downhill from orchard ground, or is within a few hundred feet of a septic drainfield or corral, your odds of elevated nitrate go up fast.

## What Nitrates Do to Your Health, and Who's Most at Risk

For most healthy adults, moderate nitrate exposure isn't an emergency. The real danger is concentrated in a few groups, and for them it's serious.

### Infants under six months

This is the big one. Babies' digestive systems convert nitrate to nitrite, which binds to the hemoglobin in their blood and blocks it from carrying oxygen. The condition is called methemoglobinemia, or blue baby syndrome, because the baby's skin can literally take on a bluish tint. It can be fatal, and it can develop fast.

Formula mixed with high-nitrate well water is the classic exposure route. If there's a baby in your house, or one on the way, testing your well isn't optional. Do it before the baby comes home.

### Pregnant women

Pregnancy changes how the body handles nitrate, and some studies link high nitrate exposure during pregnancy to birth defects and other complications. Most doctors tell pregnant patients on private wells to test and, if nitrate is elevated, switch drinking water immediately.

### Everyone else, over time

Long-term consumption of high-nitrate water has been studied for links to thyroid problems and certain cancers. The science is still developing, but the EPA didn't set its limit casually. The federal maximum contaminant level for nitrate is 10 milligrams per liter (measured as nitrate-nitrogen), and you can see the full standard in the [EPA's National Primary Drinking Water Regulations](https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water/national-primary-drinking-water-regulations).

One thing nitrates won't do is hurt you in the shower. Nitrate is a drinking and cooking water problem. You don't absorb meaningful amounts through your skin, which matters when we get to treatment, because it means you usually don't need to treat every drop in the house.

## How to Test Your Well Water for Nitrates

You can't shortcut this with your senses. Testing is the only way to know, and it's cheap and fast.

A basic nitrate test at a state-certified lab runs $20 to $40. Most labs in our area turn results around in two to five business days. You can collect the sample yourself with the lab's bottle and instructions, or have a pro pull it so there's no question about technique.

Here's our standing advice for wells in Chelan, Douglas, and Grant counties:

- **Test nitrate and bacteria every year.** Spring is the best time, after snowmelt and the start of irrigation season, when nitrate levels tend to peak.
- **Test immediately** if you're pregnant, bringing home a baby, buying a home with a well, or if a neighbor's well tested high.
- **Keep every result.** A single number tells you where you are. Five years of numbers tell you where you're headed, like Gary's well did.

## Reading Your Results: What the Numbers Mean

Nitrate results are usually reported as nitrate-nitrogen (NO3-N) in milligrams per liter, which is the same as parts per million. Here's how to read yours:

- **Under 2 mg/L:** Normal background. Retest annually.
- **2 to 5 mg/L:** Above natural background, which means human activity is reaching your aquifer. Not dangerous, but watch the trend.
- **5 to 10 mg/L:** Elevated. Retest in six months, and start pricing treatment if the trend is upward. Some health agencies advise caution for infants even in this range.
- **Over 10 mg/L:** Exceeds the federal limit. Stop using the water for infant formula, drinking, and cooking right now. Use bottled water until treatment is in place.

One critical warning: **do not boil the water.** Boiling kills bacteria, but it does the opposite for nitrates. As water boils off as steam, the nitrate stays behind and gets more concentrated. Boiled high-nitrate water is worse than what came out of the tap.

For most families, the under-sink RO is the right call. Since nitrate only matters in the water you swallow, treating the kitchen tap covers 95% of the actual risk for a fraction of whole-house cost. Plan on $100 to $200 a year in replacement filters and membranes.

## Protecting Your Well Going Forward

Treatment fixes the water. Prevention protects the well. A few practical moves:

**Check your well cap and casing.** A cracked cap or corroded casing lets surface water carry contaminants straight down the borehole, bypassing the soil filtration that normally protects the aquifer. If your wellhead looks rough, get it looked at.

**Mind your setbacks.** Washington code calls for at least 100 feet between a well and a septic drainfield. Keep fertilizer, manure piles, and chemical storage well away from the wellhead too.

**Go easy on lawn fertilizer near the well.** Your own yard can be a nitrate source. Whatever the grass doesn't use heads down toward your water.

**Consider deepening or sealing if numbers keep climbing.** Nitrate usually rides in the upper part of an aquifer. Sometimes a deeper well, or a deeper seal on the existing one, pulls from cleaner water below. That's a bigger conversation, and well construction details like depth matter a lot around here. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a safe level of nitrates in well water?

The federal drinking water standard is 10 mg/L measured as nitrate-nitrogen. Below 2 mg/L is normal background for groundwater. Anything between 2 and 10 means human sources are reaching your aquifer, so retest regularly and watch the trend. Over 10, stop drinking the water until treatment is installed, especially if infants or pregnant women are in the home.

### Can I boil well water to remove nitrates?

No, and boiling actually makes it worse. As water evaporates, the nitrate stays behind and becomes more concentrated in what's left. The only proven home treatments are reverse osmosis, nitrate-selective anion exchange, and distillation. Carbon filters and standard water softeners do not remove nitrate at all.

### How often should I test my well for nitrates?

Once a year at minimum, ideally in spring when levels around here tend to peak after snowmelt and the start of irrigation season. Test immediately if you're pregnant, bringing home an infant, buying a property with a well, or if a nearby well has tested high. Keep every result so you can spot a rising trend before it crosses the limit.

### Do orchards really cause nitrates in well water?

Fertilized agriculture is the largest nitrate source in groundwater across farm country, and that includes orchards, row crops, and livestock operations. Septic systems add to it in rural neighborhoods. That said, today's orchardists fertilize far more precisely than past generations did, and much of the nitrate in our aquifers reflects decades of accumulation. The source matters less than your number, so test and treat based on results.
