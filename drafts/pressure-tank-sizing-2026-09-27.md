# What Size Pressure Tank Do I Need?

The honest answer is: it depends on what your pump can put out, not on the biggest tank that fits in the pump house. A pressure tank isn't a water storage tank in any real sense. It holds a small cushion of compressed air over the water, and that cushion is what pushes water to your fixtures between pump cycles. Size it wrong and you get a pump that short-cycles itself to an early grave, or a tank that costs more than it needed to and still doesn't fix a slow well.

This is the math and the reasoning behind it, not a single number, because the right size genuinely depends on your pump.

## What a Pressure Tank Is Actually Sizing Around

Your well might run a submersible pump set down in the casing, or a [jet pump](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/learn-about-private-water-wells) mounted above ground for a shallower well. Either way, the tank does the same job: it stores a cushion of compressed air over the water, and that air pressure is what delivers water to the house between pump starts. The amount of water the tank gives up before the pump has to kick back on is called drawdown, and drawdown, not the tank's total gallon rating, is the number that actually matters.

A bare pressure gauge and switch tell you when the pump turns on and off. The tank tells you how long it takes to get there. Too little drawdown for the pump's output and the pump starts and stops every few seconds. Every start heats the motor windings, which makes rapid cycling one of the quickest ways to wear a pump out early. If a tank is already showing that pattern, our post on [well pump short cycling](/blog/well-pump-short-cycling/) walks through the other causes worth ruling out first, like a lost air charge or a bad pressure switch.

## Drawdown Is the Number That Matters, Not Total Tank Volume

Every pressure tank has a rated total volume and a much smaller usable, or drawdown, volume. The rest of the tank is the compressed air cushion that never leaves. As a rough illustration, [Penn State Extension](https://extension.psu.edu/using-low-yielding-wells) notes that about 20 percent of a pressure tank's total capacity is usable water: a 42-gallon tank discharges roughly 8 gallons before the pump restarts, an 82-gallon tank about 16 gallons, and a 120-gallon tank around 24 gallons. Tank manufacturers publish their own drawdown figures per model and pressure setting, since the exact percentage shifts with the pressure switch's cut-in and cut-out points, but the shape of it holds: a tank's nameplate gallons overstate what you actually get by a wide margin.

That's why "get the biggest tank you can fit" is the wrong question. The right question is how much drawdown your pump's flow rate actually calls for.

## Matching Tank Size to Your Pump's Flow Rate

The working idea, and the one [Amtrol's own commercial sizing guide](https://www.amtrol.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/9017-102-03_19-WXT-Sizing.pdf) walks through step by step for larger systems, is a minimum run time: the tank should hold enough drawdown that the pump runs for at least a set number of minutes each time it starts, rather than kicking on and off in seconds. A small single-phase pump generally needs a shorter minimum run time than a large multi-horsepower one; in Amtrol's own worked example for an 85 GPM submersible, they size the tank to give the pump roughly two minutes of continuous run time at its average flow rate through the pressure range, which for that pump works out to nearly 200 gallons of drawdown before accounting for the tank's own acceptance factor.

Household pumps are nowhere near that scale. A common rule of thumb for a residential pump is at least one gallon of drawdown for every gallon per minute the pump delivers, which works out to about a minute of run time per cycle; the pump or motor maker's own minimum run time for your model is the figure to check against. The exact number still depends on the pressure switch settings and the specific tank model's acceptance factor, which is why the pump's own flow rate, not a guess, is the starting point.

## Finding Your Pump's Actual GPM

You can't size a tank to a pump you haven't measured. The pump's rated flow (in gallons per minute) comes from its nameplate or a flow test at the pressure switch's cut-out setting, since output drops as pressure rises. If you're starting from scratch on a replacement or a new install, our post on [well pump sizing](/blog/well-pump-sizing/) covers how GPM, horsepower and well depth interact, and how to check what your well can actually deliver, which sometimes turns out to be the real limit rather than the pump or tank.

If your well's yield is genuinely marginal, more tank volume will not manufacture water the aquifer doesn't have. It buys you a slightly longer buffer between pump cycles, nothing more. Penn State Extension makes the same point about low-yielding wells: a bigger tank provides a bit more stored water, but the added storage isn't enough to solve a well that simply can't keep up with demand.

## Quick Reference: Tank Size vs. Usable Water

Here's the illustration from Penn State Extension showing how little of a tank's rated volume is actually usable between pump cycles:

| Tank's Total Rated Volume | Approx. Usable Water (Drawdown) |
|---|---|
| 42 gallons | about 8 gallons |
| 82 gallons | about 16 gallons |
| 120 gallons | about 24 gallons |

Treat these as illustrative, not a shopping list. Your actual drawdown depends on the specific tank model, its precharge, and where the pressure switch is set to cut in and out. A dealer or the installer sizing your replacement should be working from the manufacturer's drawdown chart for the exact model, matched to your pump's flow rate, not this rough table.

## Why Bigger Isn't Always the Right Call

A larger tank does buy you a few real things: fewer pump starts over the life of the motor, and a bit more of a buffer if the power blinks or a fixture is left running. But it isn't free, and it isn't automatically better.

A few honest trade-offs:

1. **Cost scales with size.** A pressure tank replacement runs $600 - $1,800 depending on the model and size (see our [pressure tank cost guide](/blog/well-pressure-tank-cost/) for how prices break down by tank size). Going two sizes bigger than your pump calls for is money that doesn't buy proportional benefit.
2. **Bigger tanks take more room and more weight.** A large tank in service holds a lot of water on a small footprint. A pump house or crawlspace laid out for a 42-gallon tank may not have the floor space, headroom or solid base for a much larger one.
3. **It won't fix a low-yield well or a failing pump.** If the real problem is a well that can't sustain flow, or a pump on its way out, a bigger tank buys a little time and hides the symptom. It doesn't solve either one.

## When a Different System Skips the Question Entirely

Traditional tank sizing assumes a pump that runs at full output, cuts off, and waits. Some households run a variable-speed pump with a small tank, or add a cycle stop valve to a conventional pump, instead. Both hold pressure nearly constant and keep the pump running at reduced flow during small draws rather than full-on, full-off. That changes the sizing math considerably, since the pump isn't cycling the same way. Our post on [constant pressure well systems](/blog/constant-pressure-well-systems/) covers what that setup does differently and whether it's worth the added cost over a conventional tank.

## Getting the Replacement Set Up Right

Sizing the tank is half the job. The precharge, the air pressure in the tank before any water enters, has to match the pressure switch. As a rule, the precharge should be set about 2 psi below the switch's cut-in pressure; on a 40/60 switch, that's roughly 38 psi. Our post on [pressure tank air pressure](/blog/pressure-tank-air-pressure/) covers how to check and set that number with a tire gauge, and if the switch itself needs new cut-in and cut-out points to match the new tank, see [adjusting a pressure switch](/blog/well-pump-pressure-switch-adjustment/) before assuming the tank is the problem.

Getting the precharge wrong on an otherwise correctly sized tank will produce the same short-cycling symptoms as an undersized tank, and the two are easy to confuse. If a newly installed tank is still cycling fast, check the precharge before concluding the tank itself is too small.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What size pressure tank do I need for a typical house?

Start from your pump's flow rate. A common rule of thumb for a household pump is at least one gallon of drawdown per gallon per minute of pump output, which gives about a minute of run time per cycle and keeps cycling reasonable for a small pump. The precise number depends on the pump's actual flow rate at your pressure switch's cut-out setting and the specific tank model's drawdown chart, not a single household-wide figure. Bigger homes, irrigation demand, or multiple fixtures running at once can push that number higher.

### Can a pressure tank be too big for my pump?

Not in the way undersizing hurts you, but yes in terms of value. An oversized tank doesn't damage a pump the way an undersized one does through rapid cycling. It just costs more and takes more space and weight. If your pump and well can support it and the space and budget allow, erring larger is safer than erring small, but it isn't necessary once you're comfortably past the minimum drawdown your pump calls for.

### Will a bigger pressure tank fix my low water pressure?

Usually not. A pressure tank sets how often the pump cycles and smooths out delivery between cycles, but it doesn't create pressure on its own. If pressure is genuinely low throughout the day, the more likely causes are the pressure switch settings, a pump that's aging or undersized for the house's demand, or restrictions in the piping. A bigger tank can mask short-term dips but won't correct a system that's actually underpowered.

### How do I know if my current tank is undersized versus just failing?

Short-cycling, the pump starting and stopping every few seconds, points at both possibilities, so the precharge is the first thing to check. Switch off the pump breaker, open a faucet to drain the tank, then check the air pressure at the Schrader valve and confirm it's about 2 psi below the pressure switch's cut-in point. If water comes out of the air valve, or the charge won't hold after you top it up, the bladder has failed and the tank needs replacing regardless of size. If the precharge is right, the bladder is sound and cycling is still fast, compare the tank's rated drawdown with the pump's GPM: a big shortfall means the tank is undersized.
