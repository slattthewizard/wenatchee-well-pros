# Well Cleaning and Rehabilitation: What It Actually Fixes

Your well used to fill the pressure tank fast. Now it takes longer, the pump runs more, and on a hot August afternoon the flow at the hose bib is noticeably weaker than it was last summer. Somebody mentioned "well cleaning." Somebody else said you need a new well. Neither is automatically true.

Well cleaning, more precisely called well rehabilitation, is work done to the well itself: the casing, the screen, and the borehole around it. It's a different job from pump repair, and it doesn't always solve a low-yield problem. Here's what it actually involves, what it can and can't fix, and how to tell whether your well needs it or whether the real problem is upstream of the well entirely.

## Why Well Yield Declines: Encrustation, Biofouling, and Sediment

A well that produced 12 gallons per minute when it was drilled doesn't stay at 12 GPM forever. [Penn State Extension](https://extension.psu.edu/water-well-maintenance-and-rehabilitation) breaks the decline down into a short list of causes, and most wells have more than one working at once:

- **Encrustation.** Calcium and magnesium salts, along with iron and manganese compounds, precipitate out of the water and build up on the well screen, narrowing the slots the water has to pass through.
- **Biofouling.** Iron bacteria form a slimy biomat on the screen. Penn State's extension specialists note it can clog a screen badly enough to make a well "virtually useless" within months once it takes hold.
- **Sediment plugging.** Fine sand and silt migrate from the aquifer into the well, often because the well wasn't fully developed (flushed clean) when it was first drilled.
- **Corrosion and sand pumping.** An aging metal casing or screen corrodes, the openings enlarge, and abrasive sediment starts moving through the pump, wearing it out faster than normal use would.

None of this is the same thing as a well that's simply running out of water because the aquifer itself is dropping. If your well has always produced modestly and the whole area is drawing down in a dry summer, that's a different problem with different fixes, covered in [our post on a well running dry](/blog/well-running-dry/). Cleaning a screen that isn't clogged doesn't create water that isn't there.

## Mechanical Rehabilitation: Surging, Brushing, and Jetting

The first line of attack on a clogged well is physical. A well contractor pulls the pump and drop pipe, then works the screen with equipment mounted on a drilling rig: wire brushes scrub the inside of the screen, high-pressure water jets blast material loose from the slots, and surging (repeatedly forcing water in and out of the screen) dislodges fine material into the well so it can be bailed or pumped out.

This is specialized rig work for a water well contractor, not something you'd do with a garden hose. Pulling the pump is the first step either way, the same first step as [replacing a submersible pump](/blog/how-to-replace-a-well-pump/), which is also the point where a straightforward pump problem, worn bearings, a bad motor, a corroded splice, often gets found and solved without touching the well itself.

## Chemical Treatment: Acid for Scale, Chlorine for Iron Bacteria

Mechanical agitation alone often isn't enough against mineral scale or a well-established bacterial biomat, so it's paired with chemical treatment. Strong acid solutions dissolve the calcium, magnesium, iron, and manganese deposits that mechanical brushing can't fully remove. For iron bacteria, Penn State Extension notes that chlorine is used at much higher concentrations than a routine disinfection: about 50 mg/L is the general disinfection dose, while severe iron bacteria problems are treated at 500 to 2,000 mg/L, held in the well long enough to break down the biomat.

One rule from that same source is worth repeating exactly: chlorine and acid must never be in the well at the same time. They're used in sequence, with the well flushed between treatments. This is a stronger, longer-contact treatment than the annual disinfection covered in [our guide to shock chlorination](/blog/well-chlorination-shock/), which is meant for routine bacterial contamination rather than a clogged screen.

## When Rehabilitation Works, and When It Doesn't

Timing matters more than most homeowners expect. Penn State Extension recommends treating a well once its output (measured as specific capacity, the pumping rate divided by drawdown) has dropped by roughly 25 percent from its baseline. Waiting past that point doesn't just cost more; the same source notes that delay "can significantly increase costs and in some cases make rehabilitation impossible," because encrustation that's had years to harden and cement in place responds much less well to brushing and acid than encrustation caught early.

There's also a geology factor specific to this part of the state. Much of the Columbia Plateau, including a lot of the ground east of the river around Quincy and Moses Lake, sits on stacked layers of basalt, [more than 350 individual lava flows](https://pubs.usgs.gov/fs/2015/3063/) with sediment beds between some of them, rather than open, uniform gravel. A well drilled into a naturally modest layer in that basalt was never going to produce more than it does, no matter how clean the screen is. Rehabilitation restores a well to its original capacity. It doesn't raise that capacity above what the formation ever supported.

## Rehabilitation vs. Hydrofracturing vs. a New Well

These three get confused because they all show up in the same "my well doesn't produce like it used to" conversation, but they solve different problems.

| Approach | What it targets | What it won't fix |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanical cleaning (brush, jet, surge) | Loose sediment and soft encrustation on the screen | Scale that's cemented in place; a naturally low-yield formation |
| Chemical treatment (acid, high-dose chlorine) | Mineral scale and an iron bacteria biomat | Sediment from a collapsing borehole; corroded casing |
| Hydrofracturing | A tight rock formation that never produced much, by opening new fractures | A clogged screen on an otherwise good producer; contamination sources |
| New or deeper well | A formation that has permanently lost yield, or casing beyond repair | A clog that cleaning would have solved for less money |

If cleaning and rehabilitation aren't the answer because the formation itself is the limit, [hydrofracturing a low-yield well](/blog/hydrofracking-low-yield-well/) is the next option worth understanding, and it works on a completely different mechanism than anything described above.

## What Happens After the Well Is Cleaned

Rehabilitation isn't finished when the brushing and chemical treatment stop. The well has to be flushed clear of the treatment chemicals and any material that was loosened, which usually means pumping it to waste for a stretch before it's put back into service.

After that, the well should be disinfected, and the water should be tested rather than assumed safe. The [Washington State Department of Health recommends](https://doh.wa.gov/sites/default/files/legacy/Documents/Pubs//333-171.pdf) testing your private well every year for coliform bacteria and nitrate, and testing again when you replace any part of your well system or notice a change in taste, color, or smell, both of which can follow a rehabilitation. The [EPA advises keeping a record](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/protect-your-homes-water) of well maintenance work like disinfection or sediment removal, since it's the kind of thing you'll want on hand if a problem comes back or you sell the house.

Once the well is back in service, it's worth running a yield check, comparing the flow rate now to what it was before the decline, rather than just trusting that it "feels" better. ## Signs the Problem Is the Well, Not the Pump

Before you schedule anything, it helps to know which symptoms point toward the well and which point toward the pump or tank. A few patterns worth watching for:

1. **Yield drops gradually over months or years**, rather than stopping suddenly. A sudden loss of water is more often a pump, wire, or switch failure; a slow decline points toward encrustation or biofouling.
2. **Sand or fine sediment starts showing up** in toilet tanks and faucet aerators when it never did before. That can mean [a failing screen letting sediment through](/blog/sand-in-well-water/), not just a dirty filter.
3. **Water looks cloudy or rust colored right when the pump starts, then clears** as it keeps running. That pattern is more consistent with material loosening off an enlarged or corroded screen than with a pump problem.
4. **Pressure and amp draw at the pump still read normal**, but the flow you're actually getting at the tap is down. If the pump itself checks out on an [amp draw test](/blog/well-pump-amp-draw-testing/), the bottleneck is more likely between the screen and the pump intake.
5. **Neighbors on wells in the same area are seeing the same slow decline.** That points toward the aquifer or formation rather than something specific to your well.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does well cleaning cost?

There's no single number, and we don't want to invent one. Mechanical and chemical well rehabilitation is specialized rig work typically priced by a licensed well driller based on well depth, how bad the clogging is, and how much chemical contact time it needs. What we can price is the diagnostic side: a service call and diagnosis to figure out whether the problem is really the well runs $150 to $250, applied toward the repair if you hire us for pump-side work. If the pump comes out and needs replacing instead of going back in, submersible pump replacement runs $1,800 to $4,500 depending on depth and horsepower.

### How do I know if I need well cleaning or a new pump?

Start with the pattern, not the fix. A gradual decline in flow over months, sediment that wasn't there before, or cloudy water that clears as the pump runs all point toward the well itself. A pump that's suddenly weak, noisy, or short-cycling more often points toward the pump, switch, or tank. The only way to be certain is to test: check the amp draw and pressure at the pump, then measure actual flow rate. That diagnostic step is where we can help before you commit to a well contractor's rehabilitation quote or a full pump replacement.

### Can I clean my own well?

Not the well screen itself. Mechanical rehabilitation needs rig-mounted brushes, high-pressure jetting equipment, and a way to safely remove loosened material from a deep borehole. Chemical treatment involves strong acids and chlorine at many times the strength of a routine disinfection, left in the well for long contact periods, and mixing the wrong chemicals in the wrong order in a confined well is a real hazard. This is a job for a licensed well contractor. What a homeowner can reasonably check first is the simpler stuff: the pressure gauge, the breaker, and whether the symptom really points to the well at all.

### How long does a cleaned well stay clean?

There's no fixed number of years, and anyone who gives you one is guessing. Penn State Extension's guidance is to track well performance over time rather than clean on a calendar: once output drops around 25 percent from baseline, it's time to act, and acting sooner rather than later keeps the job cheaper and more likely to succeed. Wells with heavy iron bacteria or high mineral content in the water tend to reclog faster than wells with a cleaner chemistry to begin with, which is one more reason annual water testing matters even after a rehabilitation job is done.
