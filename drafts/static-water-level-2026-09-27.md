# Static Water Level: What It Actually Tells You About Your Well

If you've ever pulled up your well report or heard a driller mention a number in feet, there's a good chance you were looking at your static water level. It's one of the most useful numbers on that whole document, and one of the least explained. Here's what it actually measures, how it's different from the number your pump sees while it's running, how it's found without guesswork, and what it can tell you if it starts moving in the wrong direction.

## What Static Water Level Means

Static water level is the depth from a reference point, usually the top of the well casing or the ground surface, down to the water surface inside the well when nothing has been pumping for long enough that the water has settled back to its resting position. "Static" here means still: no draw, no recovery in progress, just the well sitting quiet.

That's different from two other numbers people often mix it up with. Well depth is how far down the well was drilled, from the surface to the bottom of the casing or the open hole. Static water level is almost always a much smaller number than well depth, because it's measuring where the water starts, not where the hole ends. The distance between the two, from the water surface down to the bottom of the well, is the column of water actually sitting in the casing, sometimes called the water column or the well's storage.

Depending on where you are in north central Washington, groundwater may sit in fractured basalt, in sedimentary rock, or in alluvial gravels along the valleys, and its resting depth reflects the local water table, the same concept the U.S. Geological Survey describes in its overview of how aquifers hold and move water ([USGS Water Science School, "Aquifers and Groundwater"](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/aquifers-and-groundwater)). Static level isn't fixed forever. It moves with the season, with drought, and with how much groundwater is being pulled out of the same aquifer nearby, which is the next thing worth understanding.

## Static Level vs. Pumping Level: The Difference That Matters

The moment a pump turns on, the number changes. As water is pulled out of the casing faster than the surrounding aquifer can feed it back in, the water surface inside the well drops. That lower level, measured while the pump is running and has stabilized, is the pumping level. The difference between static level and pumping level is called drawdown, and it's a direct measure of how hard your well has to work to keep up with your pump.

The USGS describes the mechanism behind this as a "cone of depression" that forms near a pumping well, a localized dip in the water level that makes surrounding groundwater flow toward the well while it's running (USGS, "Aquifers and Groundwater"). A well with a lot of available water and good recharge shows a small, stable drawdown. A well that's being pushed hard relative to what the aquifer can deliver shows a bigger drawdown, and in a low-yield well, the pumping level can fall close to the pump intake itself.

This is exactly why static level matters for how a well gets equipped in the first place. A submersible pump has to be set below the expected pumping level, with margin, or it starts drawing air and can run dry even though the well technically still has water sitting in it above the pump. We cover how that setting depth gets chosen when a pump actually goes in the well in [how to replace a well pump](/blog/how-to-replace-a-well-pump/). Static and pumping level together are also the two numbers behind a proper [well yield test](/blog/well-yield-test/), which measures how many gallons per minute your well can sustain, not just how deep the water starts.

## How Static Water Level Is Actually Measured

The accurate way to measure static water level uses an electric water level meter: a probe on the end of a marked tape, lowered down the well until the probe touches water and completes a circuit, which triggers a light or tone at the surface. The tape is marked in feet, so the reading at the moment of contact is the depth to water. Instrument makers sell meters built to measure both a resting static level and, with the pump running, drawdown, using one tool for both jobs ([Solinst, "101D Water Level DrawDown Meter"](https://www.solinst.com/instruments/level-measurement-devices/101d-water-level-drawdown-meter/)).

The probe usually goes in through a threaded access plug in the well cap, or a separate sounding tube run down alongside the drop pipe, so it can't tangle with the wiring and drop pipe. Whatever opening is used has to be closed up tight afterward, because the cap is what keeps surface water, insects and debris out of the well.

What doesn't work well is dropping something down the casing to listen for a splash. It's a rough guess at best, it tells you nothing about pumping level or drawdown, and there's a real risk of the object glancing off the drop pipe, jamming against the pump's wiring, or lodging somewhere that requires pulling the whole string to retrieve it. That's an expensive way to find out a number an electric sounder gets right the first time.

| Method | How it works | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Electric sounder/tape | Marked cable with a probe; a circuit closes on contact with water | Precise static or pumping level, in feet, at the moment measured |
| Well report record | Depth logged by the driller when the well was built | A one-time historical reading, not today's level |
| Continuous data logger | Submerged pressure sensor logging readings over time | A trend over weeks, months, or seasons |
| Dropping an object down the casing | Guessing from sound or feel | Unreliable, and risks damaging the pump or wiring |

## Finding It Without Measuring Anything: Your Well Report

Wells drilled in Washington have a well report filed with the Department of Ecology when they're built (very old wells may have none on file), and that report typically records the static water level the driller measured right after the well was completed, along with the well's depth, casing details, and yield at the time. If you don't already have a copy, Ecology's own well report search tool lets you look up records by address, parcel, or well tag number ([Washington Department of Ecology, State Well Report Viewer](https://appswr.ecology.wa.gov/WellConstruction/WellReportSearchTools/)).

The catch is that the number on your well report is a snapshot from whenever the well was drilled, which might be decades ago. It's a genuinely useful starting point, especially for figuring out roughly how much water column your well has, but it isn't a substitute for a current measurement if you're troubleshooting a problem today or deciding how deep to set a replacement pump. Aquifers move, wells age, and nearby groundwater use changes over the years between drilling and now.

## Why Static Water Level Changes Through the Year

In orchard country, static water level rarely sits still. Winter and spring recharge, from rain and snowmelt soaking into the ground, tends to bring levels up. Through the hot, dry summer months, when irrigation demand across the valley is highest and rainfall is scarce, levels in many wells drift down, sometimes noticeably, before recovering over the following winter. Wells drilled into fractured basalt can respond differently than wells in shallow alluvial gravel, since water moves through a limited network of fractures rather than draining and refilling evenly like a sponge.

None of that means every dip is a problem. A well that drops a predictable amount every August and comes back every winter is behaving normally. ## Well Water Level Monitors: What They Do and Whether You Need One

A well water level monitor, usually a small pressure sensor lowered into the well and left in place, logs the water level automatically at set intervals instead of requiring someone to show up with a hand sounder each time. Over weeks or months it builds a record you can actually compare: this July against last July, this dry spell against the one two years ago. That kind of trend is hard to see from a handful of one-off readings, and it's the same basic sensing approach used in the continuous data loggers built for exactly this job.

For most homes on a well with no history of trouble, a monitor is a nice-to-have, not a necessity. It earns its keep on a well that's already showing a slow decline, on a low-yield well where you want early warning before a dry summer turns into no water at all, or on a property where several wells share the same stretch of aquifer and everyone wants to know if a neighbor's irrigation well is pulling the shared water table down. ## What a Falling Static Level Might Be Telling You

A static level that keeps dropping and isn't recovering points at a change in the aquifer itself: a run of dry years with less recharge, or heavy pumping nearby, such as orchard irrigation on your own property or a neighbor's, pulling the local water table down. Other problems can look similar at the tap without moving the static level at all. A screen gradually choking with fine sediment, or a well that was always marginal for the demand on it, shows up as a deeper pumping level and bigger drawdown instead. Telling those apart takes a measurement, not a guess from the pressure gauge.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's the difference between static water level and pumping level?

Static water level is the depth to water when the well has been sitting quiet, with no pump running, for long enough to settle back to its resting position. Pumping level is the depth to water once the pump has been running long enough to stabilize, and it's always deeper than static level because pumping pulls the local water surface down. The difference between the two is called drawdown. Static level tells you where your well's water rests; pumping level and drawdown tell you how hard the well works to keep up with the pump.

### How do I find my well's static water level without hiring anyone?

The easiest route is your well report. Washington keeps a public database of well construction reports, searchable by address or parcel, through the Department of Ecology's State Well Report Viewer, and most reports list the static water level the driller measured when the well was built. That number is a historical snapshot, not necessarily today's level, but it's a real, documented figure rather than a guess. A current measurement requires an electric water level sounder lowered through the well's sounding port, which most homeowners don't own and don't need to buy for a one-time check.

### Why does my well's static water level change with the seasons?

Groundwater recharges from rain and snowmelt, mostly in the wetter months, and gets drawn down through the summer by irrigation, evaporation, and everyday household use across the valley. A well that drops several feet every August and comes back every winter is following a normal seasonal pattern rather than showing a problem. Wells in fractured basalt can lag or swing differently than wells in shallow alluvial gravel, since water moves through basalt fractures more unevenly than it does through loose sand and gravel.

### What does a falling static water level mean for my pump?

If static level keeps dropping without recovering, the pumping level drops with it, and a pump set based on an old static level reading can end up running with too little water above the intake, especially during peak summer demand. That risks pulling air, running dry, and burning out the motor well before it should fail. It doesn't automatically mean the well is failing. It might mean the aquifer is having a dry year it will recover from, heavier pumping nearby is pulling the water table down, or the pump simply needs to be reset deeper, and each of those has a different fix.
