# PVC Well Casing vs Steel: What Each Material Means for Your Well

If you've ever pulled the cap off a wellhead and wondered whether that pipe going into the ground is steel or plastic, you're not alone. Most homeowners never think about casing until something goes wrong: a grinding sound from below, sand in the toilet tank, or a driller telling you the casing itself is the problem. Casing is the one part of a well system almost nobody sees, and it's the part everything else depends on.

Wells across Chelan, Douglas and Grant counties were drilled across a long span of decades, through basalt in much of the Columbia Plateau and through looser alluvium along the valleys. Steel was the standard casing for most of the last century, and PVC has gained acceptance over the past few decades. Neither one is automatically the "better" choice. Each does a specific job, and each fails in a different way over time.

## What Well Casing Actually Does

Casing is the pipe that lines a well from the surface down toward the water-bearing zone. According to the [USGS Water Science School's explanation of groundwater wells](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/groundwater-wells), casing is "the tube-shaped structure placed in the well to maintain the well opening from the target ground water to the surface," and together with the grout seal around it, casing "keeps dirt and excess water out of the well," helping keep shallow or lower-quality groundwater from mixing with what you're pumping. In plain terms: casing holds the borehole open, keeps the sides of the hole from caving in, and gives the pump and drop pipe a clean, straight column to run through.

That last point matters more than people expect. A pump doesn't just need water. It needs a stable, unobstructed shaft to hang in. Casing that's collapsing, corroding, or full of scale changes how a pump performs long before the well runs dry.

## Steel Casing: Where It's Used and Why It Was the Standard

For most of the last century, steel was the default casing material, and in a lot of drilling situations it still is. Steel is strong enough to be driven through gravel and cobbles, tolerant of the pounding a well takes during drilling, and able to handle the collapse pressures of a deep or difficult borehole. In basalt country, where a driller may need to case through fractured rock or unstable zones before hitting a stable formation, steel's strength is a real advantage.

Washington's well construction rules require that any steel casing or liner meet or exceed the ASTM A-53 specification for steel pipe, with minimum wall thicknesses set by casing diameter (more on that below). That standard exists because a casing that's too thin for its depth and diameter can deform or collapse under the pressure of the surrounding formation.

Steel's weak point is corrosion. Metal casing sitting in contact with groundwater for decades is exposed to chemical and electrolytic attack that can thin the pipe wall from the outside in. [Penn State Extension's guide to well maintenance and rehabilitation](https://extension.psu.edu/water-well-maintenance-and-rehabilitation) notes that "corrosion of metal casing and other well components can seriously reduce the useful life of a well," and that in advanced cases, a casing that has "undergone significant corrosive deterioration may collapse altogether." How fast that happens depends on the local water chemistry, the soil the casing sits in, and how the well was built. It isn't a fixed timeline, and there's no way to predict it without pulling the casing or inspecting what's accessible above grade.

## PVC Casing: Where It's Used Today and Why It's Common Now

Plastic casing, almost always PVC, became a widely accepted alternative once manufacturing standards caught up to what wells actually demand. It doesn't rust, it's non-conductive, and it's lighter and generally less expensive to install than steel of the same diameter. Washington's standard requires plastic casing to be new material marked by the manufacturer with its size, class, SDR rating and ASTM designation, and to carry an NSF seal of approval for potable water use, with a minimum SDR 21 rating.

PVC isn't invincible. [University of Kentucky's extension program on plastic well casing](https://water.mgcafe.uky.edu/plasticcasing) points out that plastic casing can be "susceptible to heat related deformations, which can occur during well installation," and that problems in plastic-cased wells more often trace back to installation mistakes than to the material itself: cracked casing, poor jointing, or a section that wasn't seated correctly where it meets bedrock. In other words, PVC's failure mode tends to be an installation defect rather than the slow, chemical wearing-down that steel experiences. That's a meaningfully different kind of problem to diagnose.

PVC casing is a different part from PVC drop pipe, the pipe that carries water up from the pump inside the casing. They sit one inside the other and fail for different reasons; see our guide on [drop pipe material choices](/blog/drop-pipe-galvanized-vs-poly/) if that's the piece you're actually asking about.

## Steel vs PVC Well Casing: A Side-by-Side Look

| Factor | Steel casing | PVC casing |
|---|---|---|
| Main failure mode | Corrosion and thinning over years, potential collapse | Physical damage: cracking, poor joints, heat deformation during install |
| Strength in unstable ground | Handles driving through gravel, cobbles and fractured rock well | Washington requires it to be set in an oversized hole, never driven; depends on proper seating |
| Conductivity | Conductive | Non-conductive |
| Washington material standard | ASTM A-53 steel pipe specification | NSF-listed plastic, SDR 21 minimum, ASTM-marked |
| What to watch for over time | Rust staining, thinning walls, sand or grit increasing | Cracks, movement at joints, damage from ground shift or a hard knock |
| Typical era | The long-time standard, and still widely used | Gained acceptance over the past few decades |

## Washington's Minimum Casing Standards

Washington doesn't leave casing material to guesswork. Under [WAC 173-160-201](https://apps.leg.wa.gov/wac/default.aspx?cite=173-160-201), well casing and liner pipe must be made of either steel or plastic unless the department approves something else, and both have to meet a named standard: steel to the ASTM A-53 specification, plastic to NSF and ASTM standards with a minimum SDR 21 rating. The rule also sets a minimum wall thickness for each casing diameter, and it states plainly that casing "shall be resistant to the corrosive effects of the surrounding formations, earth, and water and shall be impervious to any contaminants encountered."

That last clause is the one that matters most for an older well. The rule is a construction standard: it sets what casing must be when it goes into the ground, built around exactly the corrosion risk steel casing faces over time. How an existing casing is holding up decades later is something only inspection tells you.

## Living With an Older Steel-Cased Well

Plenty of older homes in the valley and the surrounding foothills sit on steel-cased wells. That's not a defect. It reflects the age of the house and the drilling practices of the time. The question worth asking isn't "should I replace my steel casing with PVC," which almost never makes sense on its own, but "does my casing show signs that it's giving up."

[EPA's guidance for private well owners](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/protect-your-homes-water) recommends periodically inspecting the exposed parts of a well for problems, including "cracked, corroded or damaged well casing," a broken or missing well cap, and settling or cracking of the surface seal. You can do the visible part of that check yourself.

**Signs worth a second look on an older steel-cased well:**

1. Rust streaks or flaking on the casing where it's visible above grade.
2. A well cap that's rusted through, loose, or no longer seated tightly.
3. Sand or fine grit showing up in a sediment filter more than it used to.
4. Water that runs discolored right when the pump kicks on, then clears.
5. A sudden change in water clarity after heavy rain or spring runoff, which can point to a compromised seal near the casing rather than the casing itself.
6. Any visible lean, gap, or separation where the casing meets the ground surface.

None of these on their own means the well is failing. Say your filter is clogging faster than it used to and you've ruled out the obvious culprits, that's worth a look before it becomes a bigger job. If you're seeing more than one of these at once, or the casing itself looks compromised rather than just the cap or seal, that's a call for a professional look rather than a guess. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I replace steel well casing with PVC?

In some cases, yes, but it's a driller's decision, not a homeowner preference. It depends on the well's depth, the surrounding geology, whether the existing steel casing is structurally sound enough to leave in place with a plastic liner inside it, and what Washington's construction standards require for that specific well. A licensed driller evaluates the borehole condition before recommending a material change. It's not something to plan around in advance without that evaluation.

### How do I know if my well casing is steel or PVC?

Look at the visible section above grade, right where it comes out of the ground near your well cap. Steel casing looks and feels like metal pipe, often painted or showing some surface rust even when sound underneath. PVC casing looks like the plastic pipe you'd recognize from plumbing, usually white, gray, or a similar solid color, and it won't rust. If the visible section has been cut back or capped in a way that hides the material, a well contractor can confirm it from your well log, which records the casing type used when the well was drilled.

### Does a corroded steel casing mean I need a new well?

Not necessarily. Surface rust on the exposed section above grade is common and doesn't by itself mean structural failure below ground. What matters more is what's happening deeper down: sand or grit increasing in your water, a well cap that no longer seats properly, or any sign the casing has shifted. Those point to deeper deterioration and warrant a professional look. A full well replacement is usually the last option considered, after repair or sleeving options have been ruled out.

### Is PVC well casing safe for drinking water?

Yes, when it meets the standard Washington requires. Plastic casing has to carry an NSF seal of approval for use in potable water supplies, along with manufacturer markings for size, SDR rating and ASTM designation. That certification process specifically evaluates whether the material leaches anything into drinking water. Problems tied to plastic casing generally trace back to installation, cracked pipe, poor joints, or a section that wasn't seated correctly, rather than the material itself when it's properly rated and installed.
