## Start With the Free Stuff: 10-Minute Well Pump Troubleshooting

Before you touch a wrench, figure out what's actually wrong. Most well systems have only a handful of parts: the pump down the well, a pressure tank, a pressure switch, and the wiring that ties them together. When water stops, it's almost always one of those four.

Run these checks in order. They cost nothing and they'll tell you which way this is headed.

### Check the breaker first

Go to your electrical panel and find the breaker labeled "well" or "pump." If it's tripped, flip it fully off, then back on. One reset is fine. Storms, brownouts, and irrigation startup surges trip well breakers all the time in orchard country.

Here's the rule that matters: one reset, ever, per problem. If it trips again, something downstream is pulling too much current. That's a shorted wire, a seized pump, or a failing motor. Repeated resets cook the motor windings. Stop there and read our post on a [well pump tripping the breaker](/blog/well-pump-tripping-breaker/) before you do anything else.

### Read the pressure gauge

Find the small gauge near your pressure tank. Most systems run between 40 and 60 psi. If the gauge reads zero and the pump isn't running, the system isn't getting power or the pump is done. If the gauge is stuck mid-range and you still have no flow, the gauge itself may be dead, which is a $15 part.

### Watch and listen at the pressure switch

The gray box next to the tank is the pressure switch. With water running somewhere in the house, you should hear a soft click as it tells the pump to start. Rapid clicking every few seconds means short cycling, and that points at the tank, not the pump. No click at all with low pressure points at the switch or power supply.

Look, but don't open it. The switch carries live 240-volt wiring. We cover what goes wrong inside in our guide to [pressure switch problems](/blog/pressure-switch-problems/).

### Test the pressure tank's air charge

This one finds more problems than all the others combined. Kill the breaker, open a faucet until pressure drops to zero, then put a tire gauge on the air valve under the plastic cap on top of the tank. You should read about 2 psi below your pump's cut-in pressure, so 38 psi on a 40/60 system.

If water comes out of the air valve, your tank's bladder has failed and the tank is waterlogged. That's the number one pump killer in our service area. Here's why that matters so much.

## DIY Well Pump Repair Jobs You Can Actually Handle

Pass the checks above and you'll usually know what's wrong. Here's what's reasonable to fix yourself, assuming you're comfortable with basic hand tools and you always, always kill the breaker first.

### Recharge a tank with a slow air leak

If your tank's air charge is low but no water came out of the valve, the bladder is intact and you can add air. With the breaker off and the system drained to zero pressure, use a bicycle pump or small compressor to bring the charge to 2 psi below cut-in. Recheck it in a month. If it keeps dropping, the tank is on its way out, and a [pressure tank that's waterlogged](/blog/pressure-tank-waterlogged/) or leaking air needs replacement, not more band-aids.

### Replace a clogged sediment filter

Plenty of homes from East Wenatchee to Moses Lake run whole-house sediment filters because our wells pull fine sand and silt. A packed filter chokes flow and mimics a dying pump. Shut the valves, swap the cartridge, done. Cost: $10 to $25. If you're going through cartridges fast, the real issue might be [sand in your well water](/blog/sand-in-well-water/), which is worth diagnosing.

### Thaw frozen pipes the slow way

When it hits 5 degrees in Leavenworth, exposed pipe in pump houses freezes. A space heater, heat tape, or a hair dryer will thaw it safely over an hour or two. What won't end well is open flame.

Rob up the Icicle River road learned that one. Frozen pump house line in January, propane torch, melted a PVC fitting, flooded the pump house, and shorted his control box. His "free" fix ran $2,800 once everything was repaired. Patience is cheaper. Our guide to [frozen well pipes](/blog/frozen-well-pipes/) covers the safe sequence.

### Clean or replace the gauge and hose connections

Pressure gauges, drain valves, and short sections of accessible above-ground pipe are fair game for a handy homeowner. They're cheap parts, they're not under load when the system is depressurized, and a mistake just means a drip you'll see immediately.

## When to Stop: The Red Lines in DIY Well Pump Repair

Every expensive disaster we've cleaned up started with someone crossing one of these lines. They're not suggestions.

**Stop at anything electrical past the breaker.** Pressure switch wiring, control boxes, splices in the pump house, the wire down the well. It's all 240 volts, often in damp spaces with concrete floors. Electricians get hurt on well systems. The EPA's [private well resources](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells) are great for understanding your system, but no agency anywhere recommends homeowner wiring work on a pump circuit.

**Stop before pulling the pump.** A submersible pump on 200 feet of pipe and wire can weigh well over 200 pounds, and wells around Wenatchee commonly run 150 to 400 feet deep. Drop it, and you've turned a $1,500 repair into a $15,000 problem, because a pump wedged sideways in the casing can mean drilling a new well. We've fished exactly two homeowner-dropped pumps out successfully. The other ones stayed down there.

**Stop at repeated breaker trips, burning smells, or humming without pumping.** Those are motor and wiring failures. More resets equal more damage.

**Stop at anything inside the well casing.** Dropped tools, dropped hardware, and broken well seals contaminate your drinking water and damage the casing. The Washington State Department of Health's [private well guidance](https://doh.wa.gov/community-and-environment/drinking-water) is blunt about keeping wells sealed and protected for a reason.

**Stop if you have no water at all and no obvious cause.** Total loss of water can also mean a dropping water table, which is a real thing in parts of Douglas and Grant counties during irrigation season. That needs flow testing, not parts swapping. Our post on [losing water suddenly](/blog/no-water-suddenly/) walks through how we tell the difference.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I replace a well pressure switch myself?

We don't recommend it. The part costs about $25 and the swap looks simple on video, but the switch carries live 240-volt connections, and miswiring it can burn out your pump motor or worse. With pros charging $175 to $350 installed, the savings aren't worth the risk to you or the pump.

### How do I tell if the problem is the pump or the pressure tank?

Use the tire gauge test. Kill power, drain pressure to zero, and check the air valve on top of the tank. Water at the valve or a charge that won't hold means a tank problem. If the tank checks out, the gauge sits at zero, and the breaker is fine, suspicion shifts to the pump, switch, or wiring.

### Is it safe to pull my own submersible well pump?

For shallow wells under about 50 feet with lightweight poly pipe, some homeowners manage it with two strong helpers. Around Wenatchee, most wells run 150 to 400 feet, which means hundreds of pounds of pipe, wire, and water. A dropped pump can permanently block the well casing, so for any deep well, hire it out.
