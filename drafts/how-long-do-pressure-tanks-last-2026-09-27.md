# How Long Do Pressure Tanks Last? A Straight Answer for Well Owners

You're standing at the tank with a flashlight because something feels off. The pump is cycling more than it used to, and you want to know if you're looking at a quick fix or a tank on its way out. Fair question, and it deserves a real number instead of a shrug.

The short answer: the Washington State Department of Health puts the average life of a bladder pressure tank at 5 to 7 years, and that figure comes from the agency's own drinking water tech tips, not a sales pitch. Some tanks go longer. Some don't make it that far. What decides which side of that range your tank lands on is mostly how it's been treated, not luck. Below is how the tank works, what shortens its life, how to tell it's failing, and what replacing it actually costs on a well in Chelan, Douglas, or Grant County.

## How Long a Pressure Tank Actually Lasts

[Washington DOH's troubleshooting guide for bladder pressure tanks](https://doh.wa.gov/sites/default/files/legacy/Documents/Pubs/331-342.pdf) states plainly that "on average, a bladder pressure tank lasts 5 to 7 years." That's a useful anchor number because it comes from a state health agency, not a tank seller, and it applies to the same basic bladder tank design most homes on private wells in this valley are running.

It's not the only data point worth knowing. [Amtrol, one of the major manufacturers of residential well tanks](https://www.amtrol.com/product/well-x-trol-well-tanks/), backs its Well-X-Trol line with what it calls an industry leading seven year limited warranty. A warranty isn't a promise the tank dies the day it expires, but it tells you what the manufacturer stands behind under normal use. Read together, the state's average and the manufacturer's warranty point at the same rough window: mid single digits of years from a tank that's sized right and maintained, with some running well past that and some failing sooner.

What pushes a tank toward the shorter end or the longer end is usually the same handful of things: how often it cycles, whether the precharge air pressure has been checked, and what the water and the site are doing to it day to day. We'll walk through each of those.

## How a Pressure Tank Works, Briefly

A bladder pressure tank holds a fixed volume of air, factory pre-charged, separated from your water by a rubber diaphragm or bladder. As the pump pushes water in, the air compresses and the pressure climbs. When you open a faucet, that compressed air pushes water back out through the pipes, no pump needed, until the pressure drops enough that the pressure switch calls the pump on again.

[Penn State Extension's fact sheet on low-yielding wells](https://extension.psu.edu/using-low-yielding-wells) lays out the typical numbers: the switch usually stops the pump around 40 psi and starts it again around 20 psi, and only about 20 percent of the tank's total volume is water actually delivered between those two points (what plumbers call drawdown). A 42-gallon tank delivers roughly 8 gallons before the pump kicks back on: a small, repeating dose, not a reserve supply you can count on in a power outage.

DOH's tech tip describes the same cycle with a slightly different pressure pair, 30 psi cut-in and 50 psi cut-out, just as common in the field. [EPA's own preventive maintenance guidance for small groundwater systems](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2018-03/documents/o_and_m_guidebook.pdf) flags a waterlogged tank the same way: something that throws off pressure for the whole system, not just at the tank. Either way, the tank's whole job is to let that swing happen without the pump turning on and off every few seconds. That's the piece that fails first, and it's why "how long does a pressure tank last" is really a question about how well that pressure swing has been protected.

## What Shortens a Pressure Tank's Life

A tank that dies at 4 years and one that's still fine at 12 can be running the same design. The difference often comes down to these:

| Factor | What it does to the tank |
|---|---|
| Short cycling | The pump starts and stops rapidly instead of running a full cycle. Every extra start stresses the bladder and the pump's start components. Say the tank is cycling every 20 to 30 seconds instead of every few minutes: that's the tank telling you something is already wrong. |
| Wrong precharge | If the air side loses pressure (it can seep out slowly, the same way a bike tire does), the bladder over-expands on every cycle and wears out early. DOH's guidance is to keep the precharge about 2 psi below the pump's start pressure and check it periodically. |
| Sediment | Fine sand or grit from the well working its way into the tank's fittings and valve can interfere with the air charge and the switch, on top of whatever it's doing to your fixtures. |
| Oversized demand, undersized tank | A tank sized for a small household straining to keep up with irrigation or multiple bathrooms will cycle far more often than one that's sized correctly for the load. |
| Corrosion and water chemistry | Hard, mineral-heavy groundwater is common in this part of Washington, and scale can build up in the tank tee, fittings and gauge port. A steel shell sitting in a damp pump house or crawl space can also rust from the outside, even when the bladder itself is fine. |

None of these guarantee early failure alone. Often it's two or three stacking up: a slow precharge leak nobody caught, followed by months of short cycling before anyone noticed the sound had changed.

Short cycling is common enough, and consequential enough, that it's worth understanding on its own. See [why a well pump short cycles and what each cause costs to fix](/blog/well-pump-short-cycling/) for the full breakdown.

## Signs Your Pressure Tank Is Failing

The clearest sign is waterlogging: the bladder has failed, water has filled the space that should hold compressed air, and the tank can no longer smooth out the pressure swing. DOH's troubleshooting guide lists the tells:

1. The pump cycles on and off rapidly, sometimes every few seconds, instead of running a normal cycle.
2. Tapping on the outside of the tank produces a different sound than a healthy tank (a dull, mostly-water sound rather than the sound of air over water).
3. Gently tipping the tank feels heavier than it should for its size.
4. Pressing the pin on the air charging valve on top of the tank releases water instead of air.
5. Water at the tap looks stale or rust-tinged from corrosion happening inside a tank that's mostly full of water instead of a proper air-water balance.

Any one of these is worth a closer look. Two or more together, especially rapid cycling plus water out of the air valve, means the tank is done and should be replaced, not nursed along: once the bladder is holed, recharging the air side just buys a little time. ## Two Checks You Can Do Yourself

Before you assume the tank is dead, there are two low-risk checks a handy homeowner can do.

**The tap and tip test.** Tap first, with the system at normal pressure right after the pump shuts off. Knock on the outside near the top and near the bottom with a knuckle. A healthy tank sounds hollow up top and denser lower down. A waterlogged tank sounds the same, dense, top to bottom. Then drain it (shut the breaker to the pump, then open a faucet until it stops running) and gently rock the tank. A drained healthy tank is mostly air and feels light for its size; a waterlogged one stays unexpectedly heavy.

**The air valve test.** With the system still depressurized and drained, find the air charging valve on top of the tank (it's a tire valve, functionally). Press the center pin briefly with something small and blunt. Air hissing out is normal. Water spitting out means the bladder has failed. Don't skip draining it first: with the system under pressure, the reading means nothing and water can spray out of the valve harder than you'd expect.

If either test points to a waterlogged tank, that's a job for a professional at that point, not because the diagnosis is hard, but because pulling and replacing a tank means working with the plumbing, the pressure switch wiring, and sometimes the well pump wiring right next to it. For the wiring side specifically, keep it simple: kill the breaker and verify it's dead with a meter before anyone touches anything electrical near the tank tee. See [how to adjust and read your well pump pressure switch](/blog/well-pump-pressure-switch-adjustment/) if the switch itself, not the tank, turns out to be the culprit.

## Getting More Years Out of a New Tank

You can't control everything that shortens a tank's life, but a few habits push a new tank toward the long end of the range:

- Check the precharge once a year, ideally alongside any other annual well maintenance. It takes a tire gauge and a few minutes with the system depressurized.
- Don't ignore a change in cycling. If the pump used to run every few minutes and now it's every 30 seconds, that's the earliest, cheapest warning sign there is.
- Match the tank to the household. If you've added a bathroom, irrigation, or a second household on the same well since the tank went in, it may now be under-sized for the load.
- Keep the pump house or tank closet from freezing in winter. A frozen tank stresses fittings and seals even if it survives.

None of this is complicated, and none of it requires a service contract. It's mostly noticing when something sounds different than it used to.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long do pressure tanks last on average?

Washington's Department of Health puts the average bladder pressure tank life at 5 to 7 years, based on its own drinking water tech tips for troubleshooting these tanks. At least one major manufacturer backs its residential well tanks with a 7-year limited warranty, which lines up with that range. Tanks that are sized correctly, kept at the right precharge, and not subjected to constant short cycling often run longer than the average. Tanks that lose their precharge unnoticed or run in a system that's short cycling tend to fail earlier, sometimes well before the 5-year mark.

### What shortens a pressure tank's life the most?

Short cycling and a wrong precharge are the two biggest factors, and they're often connected: a slow air leak in the tank causes the precharge to drop, which causes the pump to cycle more often, which stresses the bladder further. Sediment from the well, an undersized tank for the household's actual demand, and scale or rust on the fittings and shell all add to the wear over time. Catching a precharge problem early, before it turns into constant short cycling, is the single biggest thing you can do to extend a tank's life.

### How do I know if my pressure tank is waterlogged and not just old?

Waterlogging has specific tells: the pump cycles on and off rapidly instead of running normal cycles, the tank sounds the same (dense) whether you tap it near the top or the bottom, it feels heavier than expected when you gently tip it, and water rather than air comes out when you press the pin on the air charging valve with the system depressurized. Any single sign is worth checking further. Two or more together, especially rapid cycling plus water from the air valve, means the bladder has failed and the tank needs replacing rather than recharging.

### Is it worth repairing a pressure tank or should I just replace it?

Once a bladder has failed and the tank is genuinely waterlogged, Washington DOH's own guidance is that it's often most cost-efficient to replace the tank rather than try to repair it, and on many residential tanks the bladder or diaphragm isn't a replaceable part anyway. If the problem turns out to be the precharge (air leaked out but the bladder itself is intact) or the pressure switch, that's a real repair, not a replacement, and it costs a fraction of a new tank. That's why a diagnosis before assuming the worst matters: it's the difference between a $150 - $250 service call and a $600 - $1,800 tank swap.
