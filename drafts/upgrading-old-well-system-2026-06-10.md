# Upgrading an Old Well System: What to Replace First

A lot of the wells serving homes around Wenatchee, Cashmere, and Chelan were drilled in the 70s and 80s. The pumps and tanks bolted to them? Many haven't been touched since the Reagan administration. If that sounds like your place, you're not running a well system. You're running an experiment.

Here's the thing, though. You almost never need to replace everything at once. That's the mistake we see most often with an old well system upgrade: homeowners either ignore the whole thing until it dies on a Friday night, or they panic and pay for a full teardown they didn't need.

There's a smarter middle path. Some parts of an aging well system fail early and take other parts down with them. Other parts can soldier on for decades. In this guide, we'll walk through the order that actually makes sense, what each piece costs here in North Central Washington, and which upgrades are worth adding while the system's already open.

## How to Tell Your Well System Is Past Its Prime

You don't need a gauge to know something's off. Old systems talk. You just have to know what they're saying.

Watch for these signs:

- **The pump kicks on every time you wash your hands.** That's short cycling, and it usually means the pressure tank has failed, not the pump. Our post on [well pump short cycling](/blog/well-pump-short-cycling/) breaks down why this kills pumps fast.
- **Pressure that surges and sags.** Strong stream, weak stream, strong stream. Classic tired-tank or worn-switch behavior.
- **Rust streaks, sweating tanks, corroded fittings.** Galvanized tanks from the 80s rust from the inside out. By the time you see it outside, the inside is worse.
- **Rising power bills with no other explanation.** A worn pump or leaking drop pipe runs longer to do the same job. Some homeowners around here are paying $40 to $80 a month extra without knowing it.
- **Sputtering faucets, sediment, or metallic-tasting water.** Could be the pump breaking down, could be the well itself.

Age matters too. A submersible pump typically lasts 10 to 15 years. Pressure tanks run 8 to 15. Pressure switches, sometimes only 5. If you don't know how old yours are, check our guide on [how long well pumps last](/blog/how-long-do-well-pumps-last/) and dig up your well log.

## The Smart Order for an Old Well System Upgrade

When budget is tight, and it usually is, replace in the order that protects the most expensive component: the pump down the well. Everything below is ranked by bang for the buck.

### First: the pressure tank

This surprises people. The tank seems like the boring part. But a failed pressure tank is the number one pump killer we see in Chelan and Douglas counties.

Here's why. The tank's air bladder stores pressurized water so your pump doesn't have to start for every glass of water. When the bladder fails, the tank waterlogs and the pump starts cycling constantly. Every start is a surge of heat and torque on the motor. A pump that should start 40 times a day starts 300 times. It cooks itself.

Dale in Cashmere learned this the expensive way. His pump was clicking on every 40 seconds, and he figured he'd deal with it after harvest. Two weeks later the pump motor burned out 180 feet down. Instead of a $1,400 [pressure tank replacement](/pressure-tank-replacement-wenatchee/), he paid $3,800 to pull and replace the pump, plus the tank he still needed anyway.

If your tank is original to the house, replace it first. Full stop.

### Second: the pressure switch, gauge, and controls

Cheap parts, big consequences. A pressure switch costs $150 to $350 installed, and a sticking switch can either leave you with no water or weld itself closed and run the pump until it burns up. Gauges on old systems are often so corroded they read wrong, which hides every other problem.

If your switch has pitted contacts, buzzes, or the cover is rusted shut, swap it when you do the tank. The labor overlaps, so bundling saves money.

### Third: the pump itself

If the pump is over 12 to 15 years old, plan its replacement on your schedule instead of the pump's. A planned [well pump replacement](/well-pump-replacement-wenatchee/) costs the same as an emergency one, except you're not paying for it at 9 p.m. in January with no water and a house full of guests.

Modern pumps are also better. Today's stainless submersibles are more efficient, handle sediment better, and pair with variable-speed controllers that old motors can't.

One honest caveat: if your pump is under 10 years old and testing strong, leave it alone. An old well system upgrade doesn't mean replacing healthy equipment.

### Fourth: drop pipe and wiring

Whenever the pump comes up, inspect what it hangs from. Galvanized drop pipe from the 70s and 80s corrodes and develops pinhole leaks, which means the pump runs constantly trying to maintain pressure it's leaking back down the well.

Marie, on a 1987 system outside Leavenworth, called us about an electric bill that had crept up $90 a month. Her pump was fine. The galvanized drop pipe had three pinhole leaks, so the pump was running 14 hours a day to keep up. New poly drop pipe and wire cost her about $1,600 and paid for itself in 18 months of power savings.

Old submerged wiring with cracked insulation is also a common cause of nuisance breaker trips. Replace pipe and wire while the pump's out of the hole. Pulling everything twice doubles the labor.

### Fifth: the well cap, seal, and casing top

This one's about water safety more than convenience. Old vented caps with rotted gaskets let insects, mice, and surface runoff into your well. That's a direct line for bacteria. The EPA recommends private well owners test annually for coliform bacteria and nitrates, and a bad cap is the most common reason a test comes back dirty. You can read their guidance for well owners at the [EPA's private wells page](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells).

A modern sanitary well cap runs $200 to $500 installed. It's the cheapest insurance on this whole list.

## Upgrades Worth Adding While the System's Open

Once you're replacing components anyway, a few add-ons cost far less bundled than installed separately.

### Constant pressure systems

If your house was built around a 30/50 pressure switch, you've lived with pressure swings your whole life. A [constant pressure system](/blog/constant-pressure-well-systems/) uses a variable-speed controller to hold pressure steady within about 1 PSI. Showers stay strong while the sprinklers run. For orchard properties in the Wenatchee and Quincy areas running irrigation off the same well, it's a genuine quality-of-life jump.

### A generator transfer setup

Power outages here come with windstorms in fall and ice in winter. No power means no pump means no water, including for livestock and frost protection. Wiring a transfer switch while your pump electrical is already being worked on is the cheap time to do it.

### Pump house and pipe insulation

Single-digit January nights in the valley split pipes and crack pressure switches every year. If your pump house insulation is original, upgrade it during the project, not after the first freeze warning.

The Kowalskis bought a place near Quincy in 2024 with an untouched 1979 well system. We staged their upgrade: tank and switch that fall for $1,900, then pump, poly drop pipe, and a constant pressure controller the next spring for $6,200. Total was about $8,100 over 18 months, no emergencies, no dry days, and their summer pressure went from 38 PSI swings to a steady 58.

## Where to Stop: What's Not a DIY Job

Plenty of this is homeowner-friendly. Checking your tank's air charge with a tire gauge, reading the pressure gauge, resetting a tripped breaker once. All fair game.

Draw the line here:

- **Anything inside the pressure switch or control box while powered.** That's 240 volts. Kill the breaker, and if the problem isn't obvious with power off, stop.
- **Pulling the pump.** A pump on 200 feet of pipe full of water can weigh several hundred pounds. Dropped pumps wedge in the casing, and retrieval can cost more than the pump.
- **Anything involving the well seal or casing.** Done wrong, you've created a contamination pathway into your aquifer. The Washington State Department of Health publishes well owner guidance worth reading at [doh.wa.gov](https://doh.wa.gov/community-and-environment/drinking-water).
- **Wiring a generator to the pump circuit.** Backfeed kills line workers. Transfer switches exist for a reason.

If you've lost water entirely and the breaker reset didn't fix it, don't keep flipping it. Repeated resets into a fault can finish off a motor that was salvageable. One more thing worth scheduling during any upgrade: a water test. Old systems often hide water quality problems behind old plumbing, and new components can stir up sediment that masks or reveals them. At minimum, test for coliform bacteria and nitrates once a year, and any time the water changes taste, smell, or color.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I replace my well pump and pressure tank at the same time?

If both are over 10 years old, yes, bundling saves on labor and you reset the clock on the whole system. If one component is clearly newer and testing healthy, replace only what's failing. The key rule: never put a new pump on a waterlogged tank, because the tank will destroy it.

### How do I find out how old my well system is?

Start with your well log, which Washington keeps on file with the Department of Ecology and is searchable online by address or parcel. The pump and tank may be newer than the well itself, so check for date stickers on the tank and control box. If there's no paper trail, a technician can usually estimate age from the model numbers and condition.

### Is it worth upgrading an old well system before selling a house?

Usually, yes. Rural buyers around Wenatchee almost always order a well inspection and flow test, and a failed system becomes a negotiating chip that costs you more than the repair would have. A documented recent upgrade with receipts is a genuine selling point on well-and-septic properties.

### Can an old well system upgrade fix low water pressure?

Often, but it depends on the cause. A new tank, switch, or constant pressure system fixes pressure problems that start at the equipment. If the well itself has a falling water level or clogged screen, no amount of new hardware will fix it, which is why we test well recovery before recommending equipment. A proper diagnosis first keeps you from buying parts you don't need.
