## What PFAS Are and Why "Forever Chemicals" Is the Right Nickname

PFAS stands for per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, a family of thousands of manufactured chemicals built around an extremely stable carbon-fluorine bond. That bond is what makes them useful in nonstick cookware, water-repellent jackets, food packaging, stain-resistant carpet, and firefighting foam. It's also what makes them almost impossible to break down in the environment or in the human body.

Unlike arsenic or iron, which occur naturally in rock and groundwater, PFAS only get into your well because of human activity somewhere upstream, sometimes decades ago. Once they're in an aquifer, they tend to stay put and move slowly with the groundwater instead of degrading. That's the "forever" part. It's also why a well can test clean for years and then pick up PFAS if the plume from a source finally reaches it.

The health concerns tied to long-term PFAS exposure include effects on cholesterol, the immune system, thyroid function, certain cancers, and developmental effects in infants and children. As with most groundwater contaminants, the risk is about steady exposure over years, not a single glass of water.

## Where PFAS Comes From in Washington Well Water

PFAS contamination isn't random. It tends to cluster around specific kinds of sites, and knowing the pattern helps you judge your own risk.

**Firefighting foam.** Aqueous film-forming foam, used for decades at airports, fire training facilities, and some industrial sites, is one of the most concentrated sources of PFAS in groundwater nationwide. If your property or well is near a current or former airport, fire training area, or military installation, that's worth factoring in.

**Industrial and manufacturing sites.** Facilities that made or used PFAS in coatings, textiles, or metal plating are known sources in various parts of the state.

**Landfills and biosolids.** Consumer products containing PFAS eventually end up in landfill leachate or in treated biosolids applied to farmland, which can be a slower, more diffuse source over time.

We're not going to point to specific contaminated parcels here, because the honest answer is that it varies well by well and changes as more testing gets done. The Washington State Department of Health keeps updated guidance and testing information for private well owners, and the Washington Department of Ecology tracks known and suspected PFAS sites statewide. Checking those resources for your area is a smarter first step than guessing based on a neighbor's story.

Rural, orchard-heavy areas like the ground around Cashmere and Chelan don't have an obvious PFAS source the way an airport does, but shallow wells anywhere can be affected by nearby land use you can't see from the surface. That's exactly why testing matters more than assumptions.

## What the Health Guidelines Say

PFAS regulation has moved fast, and it's worth understanding where things stand.

In 2024, the EPA finalized the first national drinking water standard for PFAS, setting individual limits around 4 parts per trillion for PFOA and PFOS, two of the most studied PFAS compounds, along with limits for several others and a combined hazard index for mixtures. Parts per trillion is an extraordinarily small unit, which tells you how seriously health agencies are treating even low-level, long-term exposure.

That federal rule applies to public water systems. Private wells aren't regulated by the EPA or by Washington State, so nobody is required to test your well or hold it to that standard. The Washington State Department of Health has published its own PFAS guidance and action levels for regulated water systems over the past several years, and those numbers are a reasonable benchmark for private well owners too, even though they aren't mandatory for you.

A few practical takeaways:

- **Parts per trillion is the scale that matters here.** Don't expect PFAS results to look like arsenic or nitrate numbers, which are usually reported in parts per million or billion. A "small" PFAS number can still be significant.
- **Multiple PFAS compounds can add up.** Labs often test for a panel of 18 to 40+ individual PFAS chemicals. A handful of low results can combine into a meaningful total exposure.
- **Guidance keeps evolving.** Because this is a newer and fast-moving area of drinking water science, it's worth rechecking DOH and EPA guidance every year or two rather than relying on old numbers.

For current numbers and a plain-language explanation, the [Washington State Department of Health's PFAS page](https://doh.wa.gov/community-and-environment/drinking-water/contaminants/pfas) and the [EPA's PFAS drinking water resources](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/and-polyfluoroalkyl-substances-pfas) are both kept current.

## How to Test Your Well Water for PFAS

PFAS testing is more specialized than a standard well panel, and a few details actually matter.

1. **Use a lab accredited specifically for PFAS analysis.** Not every certified drinking water lab runs PFAS panels in-house. Confirm before you order, since some labs ship samples to a partner facility.
2. **Expect to pay $300 to $600.** That's higher than a basic bacteria or nitrate test because the lab methods for detecting parts-per-trillion concentrations are more involved.
3. **Follow strict sampling protocols.** Many labs ask you to avoid wearing sunscreen, insect repellent, or new synthetic rain gear while sampling, and to avoid touching the sample container with gloves that have any water-repellent treatment. PFAS is sensitive enough that cross-contamination from the sampler's own clothing is a real risk.
4. **Ask for a full panel, not a single-compound test.** A panel covering PFOA, PFOS, and the broader PFAS family gives you a much more complete picture than testing for just one or two legacy compounds.

If you've never tested your well for anything, it's worth pairing a PFAS panel with a broader private well test for bacteria, nitrates, and metals. ## Treatment Options That Actually Remove PFAS

If a test comes back positive, the fix is usually more manageable than people expect. A few technologies are proven for PFAS. Most common household filters are not.

### What works

**Granular activated carbon (GAC).** The most common and cost-effective PFAS treatment for homes. Water passes through a bed of activated carbon that adsorbs PFAS molecules. Works as a whole-house system or a point-of-use unit under the sink. Performance depends on which PFAS compounds are present and how much carbon the system uses, so sizing matters.

**Reverse osmosis (RO).** An under-sink RO system removes PFAS along with nitrates, arsenic, and most other dissolved contaminants, typically above 90 percent removal. Because it treats only the water you drink and cook with, it's often the most affordable path to protecting the water that matters most.

**Ion exchange resin.** A whole-house option that swaps PFAS ions for chloride, similar in concept to a water softener but using PFAS-specific resin. Effective for a broad range of PFAS compounds, though the resin needs periodic replacement rather than regeneration like a standard softener.

### What doesn't work

- **Boiling.** Does nothing to PFAS and can even concentrate it slightly as water evaporates.
- **Standard sediment or carbon pitcher filters.** Built for taste, chlorine, and particles, not for parts-per-trillion PFAS removal.
- **UV treatment.** Excellent for bacteria and viruses, as we cover in our [UV treatment guide](/blog/uv-treatment-well-water/), but it doesn't touch dissolved PFAS.
- **Water softeners alone.** They address hardness minerals, not PFAS compounds.

## What to Do This Week

Keep it simple and don't overspend before you know your numbers:

1. Order a PFAS panel test from an accredited lab. Budget $300 to $600.
2. Sample carefully, avoiding sunscreen, repellent, and water-repellent gear near the sample.
3. If results come back below current EPA and DOH guidance, retest every 2 to 3 years or sooner if nearby land use changes.
4. If results come back elevated, start with an under-sink RO or GAC system rather than jumping straight to a whole-house build.
5. Retest treated water annually to confirm the system is still performing.

One thing not to do: don't assume a clean neighbor test means your well is fine, and don't assume an old contamination story about a specific property applies to yours. PFAS moves with groundwater in ways that are hard to predict from the surface. Testing your own well is the only real answer.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is PFAS in well water dangerous at low levels?

Health agencies are most concerned about long-term, steady exposure rather than a single low reading. Because PFAS builds up in the body over time and doesn't break down, agencies like the EPA and Washington DOH have set very low guidance levels, in the parts-per-trillion range, as a precaution. A low detection isn't an emergency, but it's worth tracking with regular retesting rather than ignoring.

### How is PFAS testing different from a regular well water test?

A standard well panel checks for bacteria, nitrates, arsenic, and common metals using methods that detect parts per million or billion. PFAS testing measures parts per trillion, requires a specialized accredited lab, and involves strict sampling precautions to avoid cross-contamination from everyday products like sunscreen or rain gear. It also costs more, typically $300 to $600 versus $25 to $200 for a basic panel.

### Can I remove PFAS myself with a filter from the hardware store?

Most off-the-shelf pitcher and faucet filters aren't rated for PFAS removal. You need a system specifically certified for PFAS reduction, such as reverse osmosis, granular activated carbon sized correctly for your water, or PFAS-specific ion exchange resin. Check for NSF/ANSI 53 or 58 certification for PFAS reduction before buying anything.

### Does boiling my well water get rid of PFAS?

No. Boiling doesn't break the carbon-fluorine bond that makes PFAS so persistent, and because boiling evaporates water, it can actually concentrate PFAS slightly in what's left behind. Boiling is useful for bacteria, not for PFAS or most other dissolved chemical contaminants.
