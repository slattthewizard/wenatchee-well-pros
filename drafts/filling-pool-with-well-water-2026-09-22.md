# Filling a Pool With Well Water: Will Your Well Keep Up?

A 16 by 32 foot in-ground pool holds about 17,200 gallons. Figure a family of three at roughly 80 gallons a person a day, the ballpark the [USGS uses for home water use](https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/water-qa-how-much-water-do-i-use-home-each-day), and that pool is about two months of household water, pulled through one garden hose. Filling a pool with well water usually works. The real question isn't whether your pump can push that much water. It's whether your well can make it fast enough to keep up.

The time to think about it is before the pool company shows up, because nobody on their quote is going to ask where the water comes from. Do the math before you uncoil the hose, watch the well while you fill, and know when to stop. Get that right and a pool fill is a non-event. Skip it and a spring weekend can end with a burned-out pump.

## How Many Gallons Does It Take to Fill a Pool With Well Water

Pool volume comes down to length times width times average depth, converted from cubic feet to gallons. One cubic foot holds 7.48 gallons, so the formula is length x width x average depth x 7.48.

A 16 by 32 foot rectangular in-ground pool at 4.5 feet of average depth works out to 16 x 32 x 4.5 x 7.48, or about 17,200 gallons. A 24 foot round above-ground pool holding 4 feet of water is roughly 3.14 x 12 x 12 x 4 x 7.48, or about 13,500 gallons. A hot tub or spa is a different animal: a few hundred gallons, usually 300 to 500, which is a small ask for a well that already supplies a whole house every day.

| Pool | Approx. Gallons | Hours at 3 GPM | Hours at 5 GPM | Hours at 8 GPM |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hot tub or spa | 300 - 500 gal | 1.5 - 3 hrs | 1 - 2 hrs | about 1 hr |
| 24-foot round above-ground, 4 ft of water | ~13,500 gal | ~75 hrs | ~45 hrs | ~28 hrs |
| 16 x 32 ft in-ground, 4.5 ft average depth | ~17,200 gal | ~96 hrs | ~57 hrs | ~36 hrs |

Those hour totals aren't a continuous run you'd actually sit through. They're there so you can see how much the number moves depending on what your well and pump can really deliver, which is the next thing to find out.

If your pool isn't one of these standard shapes, the math still works. Measure the surface footprint and the average depth (shallow end plus deep end, divided by two), then multiply by 7.48. A free-form pool or one with a big tanning ledge runs a bit higher than a simple rectangle of the same footprint, so round up.

## Measure Your Well's Real Flow Rate Before You Start

Forget the number on the pump's spec plate. What matters is what actually comes out of the hose bib closest to your pressure tank, after friction loss, elevation change and everything else between the pump and the nozzle has taken its cut.

Grab a 5-gallon bucket and a stopwatch, or your phone, and open the spigot all the way. Time how long it takes to fill the bucket, do it twice, and average the two times. Divide 300 by the number of seconds it took to get your flow rate in gallons per minute. A bucket that fills in 60 seconds is 5 gpm, one that takes 100 seconds is 3 gpm.

Once you have that number, hours to fill is gallons divided by gpm times 60. A 17,200-gallon pool at 5 gpm comes out to roughly 57 hours of hose time, which you'll spread over several days rather than run straight through. More on that below.

Use an outside hose bib close to where the water line comes into the house, not a kitchen or bathroom tap. Those indoor lines are usually smaller and share pressure with fixtures you're not thinking about, so the reading runs low and fools you into thinking your well is weaker than it is. If the pool company plans to fill through a different connection, such as a hose splitter or a longer run than you tested, ask them what diameter hose they're using, since a narrow hose can choke a good flow rate before it ever reaches the pool.

## Your Well's Yield Is the Limit, Not the Pump

A pump rated for 10 gallons a minute doesn't help you if the well only makes 4. The pump can move water faster than the aquifer refills the casing, and when that happens you're pulling the water level down, not filling a pool with a steady stream.

This is the part people skip, and it's the part that actually decides whether a pool fill goes fine or goes badly. If you've never had your well's yield tested, now is a reasonable time to find out what it really produces instead of guessing from the pump's rating. Our guide on [testing a well's yield](/blog/well-yield-test/) covers how that's done, and if you've dealt before with a [well that can't keep up with demand](/blog/well-running-dry/), a pool fill is exactly the kind of load that exposes it again.

A well making 6 or 8 gpm all day handles a pool fill without much drama, just time. A well making 2 or 3 gpm can still do it. It just needs longer, more rest, and more patience from you.

## Signs to Shut the Hose Off Right Now

Watch the water while you fill, not just the pool climbing up the wall. Four signs mean stop immediately, not in ten minutes.

Sputtering or coughing at the hose. Air showing up in a stream that was running clean a minute ago, or water that turns cloudy or starts carrying [sand](/blog/sand-in-well-water/). Pressure that drops and doesn't climb back even after you ease off. Any one of those means the well is being pulled down faster than it can recharge.

Shut the hose off and give the well an hour or more to recover before you try again. Keep pushing through a drawdown and you risk running the pump dry, which overheats the motor and can end it early. A submersible replacement runs $1,800 - $4,500, real money to spend because a pool got filled in one stretch instead of three.

Keep a rough log of how long each block runs clean before the first sputter. Don't read much into how fast pressure comes back at the tap, because that's the pump refilling the tank, not the well refilling itself. The run time is the useful number. If it holds steady day to day, the well is recovering overnight. If it gets shorter each day, the well isn't keeping up, and the answer is shorter blocks and longer rests, or a truck.

If the pump doesn't come back to normal pressure after a rest, or it starts short cycling partway through the fill, stop and [request a free estimate](/#contact). That pattern is worth a look before you touch the hose again, and our [well pump repair](/well-pump-repair-wenatchee/) and [emergency well service](/emergency-well-service-wenatchee/) pages cover what a call like that involves.

## Fill In Stages So the Well Can Recover

Don't plan on one long run. Fill in blocks, six to eight hours during the day is a sensible chunk, then shut the hose off overnight and let the well come back to its resting water level. Resume the next morning. For the bigger pools in the table above, that's three to five days of hose time spread across a week or more, not a single weekend.

Timing matters too. Spring and early summer are the better windows, right after snowmelt and spring rain have recharged the aquifer. A dry August, when [drought conditions stretch wells thin here](/blog/drought-restrictions-wells-chelan-douglas/) already, is the worst time to ask a well for an extra 17,000 gallons on top of irrigation and everyone else's household use. If the pool can wait until June, let it.

## Filling a Pool With Well Water in Washington: What the Rules Say

Washington's groundwater permit exemption covers domestic use of up to 5,000 gallons a day without a water right. It comes from state law, [RCW 90.44.050](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=90.44.050), and the Department of Ecology administers it.

Do the arithmetic. A 17,200-gallon pool filled in a single day blows well past 5,000 gallons. Spread over four days, it's about 4,300 gallons a day, and even with the house's normal use on top, that stays under the figure. So filling in stages helps twice: it's easier on the well, and it keeps your daily use under the exemption's number. Read the exemption itself on [Ecology's site](https://ecology.wa.gov/water-shorelines/water-supply/water-rights/groundwater-permit-exemption), and see our explainer on [permit-exempt wells in Washington](/blog/permit-exempt-wells-washington/) for the fuller picture.

None of this is a legal opinion, and it doesn't cover every local rule. Some basins carry their own added restrictions on top of the state exemption, so check with your county before you commit to a fill date. And if you're on a [shared well with neighbors](/blog/shared-wells-washington/), a 17,000-gallon pool is a conversation to have with them first, not a surprise they discover when their pressure drops.

## Test the Water Before You Fill the Pool

Well water that looks clear at the kitchen tap can still cause trouble in a pool. If your well carries iron or manganese, both oxidize the moment you shock the pool with chlorine, and they leave rust-colored or brownish black stains on plaster and liner alike. Hard water scales, leaving a chalky rim at the waterline and clouding the water until it's balanced.

Test your water before you fill, not after you're scrubbing stains off the steps. If iron, manganese or hardness show up, ask your pool supplier about a sequestering agent or a hose-end pre-filter rated for those minerals, and run the water through it before it ever reaches the pool. That's a pool chemistry fix, not a well fix, so we won't price it here. But skip the test and a fresh fill can turn tea-colored the first time it's shocked.

A home test strip will give you a rough read on iron and hardness in a couple of minutes, which is enough to decide whether you need to filter the fill water at all. If the strip shows anything meaningful, or if you've never had the well tested, a lab result is worth the wait before you commit thousands of gallons to a pool you'll be looking at all summer. We're a well and pump company, not a lab, so we can't run that test for you, but a county health district or a private water testing lab can point you to the right one.

## When Trucking In Water Beats the Well

Some wells just aren't a good match for a pool this size. A marginal yield, a drought year, a shared well with neighbors who won't love a week of low pressure: any of those is a reason to look at bulk water delivery instead.

A tanker truck brings potable water and fills the pool directly, with no strain on your well at all. A hybrid works too: truck in the bulk of the pool, then top it off gradually with the well once the big volume is already there.

If your bucket test came back under 3 or 4 gpm, or your well has a history of running low in late summer, price out delivery before you commit to weeks of hose time. Call two or three local haulers, compare their per-gallon price and minimum delivery, and decide before the pool company shows up expecting a full pool by the weekend.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Will filling my pool damage my well pump?

Not from a careful fill. The risk isn't the volume, it's running the pump against a well that's drawn down or dry for an extended stretch, which overheats the motor. Watch for sputtering, air or cloudy water, stop when you see it, and give the well time to recover between runs.

### How long does it take to fill a pool with well water?

It depends on the pool's size and your well's real yield, not the pump's rated output. At 4 to 5 gallons a minute, a mid-size pool takes somewhere around 45 to 70 hours of hose time, which works out to several days of filling in blocks. Measure your own flow with a bucket and a stopwatch before you plan anything.

### Can I fill a hot tub with well water without worrying about any of this?

Usually, yes. A hot tub is only a few hundred gallons, a small ask for a well that already supplies a household every day. If your well's yield is marginal or it's had trouble keeping up before, treat it as a small version of the same math and watch the water while it fills.

### Is there a legal limit on how much well water I can use to fill a pool in Washington?

Washington's groundwater permit exemption covers domestic use up to 5,000 gallons a day without a water right. A pool fill spread over several days, plus the house's normal use, generally stays under that figure. This is general guidance, not a legal opinion, and some basins carry their own added rules, so check locally before you plan a fill date.
