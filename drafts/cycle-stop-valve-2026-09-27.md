# Cycle Stop Valve: How It Works, and Where It Falls Short

If your well pump kicks on and off every time someone runs the kitchen faucet, or your pressure swings hard between a shower and a hose bib, you've probably run into the term cycle stop valve. It's a real fix for a real problem, and it's also one of those parts that gets oversold online as a cure for everything. Here's what it actually does, how it's built, what it costs you in exchange, and how it stacks up against a variable frequency drive (VFD) constant pressure system.

## What a Cycle Stop Valve Is, and the Problem It Solves

A standard well pump is built to run at one speed and push one flow rate. When you open a faucet, the pump has already been pushing that same volume of water since the pressure switch turned it on. If you only need 2 gallons per minute (GPM) to fill a glass or run a sprinkler zone, and the pump wants to push 12, the extra water has nowhere to go but back into the pressure tank. The tank fills fast, hits cut-out pressure, and the pump shuts off again in well under a minute. Close the tap, and the pump has already stopped and started two or three times.

That's short cycling: the pump switching on and off far more often than the tank and switch were designed for. It's hard on the pressure switch contacts, hard on the motor's starting components, and it's the single biggest thing a cycle stop valve is built to stop. We've covered the broader causes and fixes in [why a well pump runs constantly or cycles too often](/blog/well-pump-short-cycling/), so this article stays focused on the valve itself.

## How a Cycle Stop Valve Works

A cycle stop valve (CSV) is a mechanical, spring-loaded valve installed in the pipe between the pump and the pressure tank tee, ahead of the pressure switch. It has no motor, no electronics, and nothing to program. Inside, a piston or diaphragm senses the pressure downstream and moves closer to or farther from its seat depending on how much water is flowing past it.

When you're only drawing a small amount of water, the valve closes down and adds resistance, which holds the downstream pressure at a set point, usually near the middle of the pressure switch range (50 psi on a 40/60 switch, for example), according to the manufacturer's own description of the mechanism ([Cycle Stop Valves, Inc., "Basic Operation of a Cycle Stop Valve"](https://cyclestopvalves.com/pages/basic-operation-of-a-cycle-stop-valve)). The pump keeps running the whole time, just pushing less flow against more resistance. Open more taps, and the valve opens further to let more flow through at the same pressure. In effect, it turns a fixed-speed pump into something that behaves like a variable-flow, constant-pressure source, without changing the pump's speed at all.

When every fixture in the house is closed, the valve doesn't slam shut completely. It's built to bypass a small flow, which the manufacturer gives as 1 to 5 GPM, so the pump keeps moving enough water to stay cool while the tank tops off the rest of the way and the switch finally cuts the pump off (same Cycle Stop Valves, Inc. page).

## What It Fixes: Short Cycling and Tank Sizing

Short cycling generally traces back to one of two things: a pressure switch or tank problem, or a mismatch between how little water a fixture draws and how much the pump wants to deliver. A cycle stop valve addresses the second one directly. By letting the pump throttle down to match small draws, it stretches the time between starts even with a small pressure tank, and it keeps pressure steadier between the switch's cut-in and cutout points instead of the usual sawtooth swing.

That's also why a CSV shows up in discussions about undersized tanks. If you've got a small tank and can't fit a bigger one in the pump house or the pit, a cycle stop valve can make that small tank behave better than it otherwise would, because the pump isn't slamming it full on every draw. It is not, on its own, a fix for a truly failing pressure switch, a waterlogged tank with a ruptured bladder, or a check valve that's letting water drain back down the drop pipe. Those still need to be diagnosed on their own terms, whether the answer turns out to be [well pump repair](/well-pump-repair-wenatchee/) or a [pressure tank replacement](/pressure-tank-replacement-wenatchee/).

## The Trade-off: Reduced Flow, Motor Cooling, and Energy Use

Nothing here is free. A cycle stop valve holds pressure steady by adding resistance, and resistance has to come from somewhere. The pump motor is still spinning at full speed while the valve throttles the output down to a couple of GPM. Its power draw falls somewhat as flow drops, but not nearly in step with the flow, and the difference is lost as pressure drop across the valve. The U.S. Department of Energy's guidance on control valves in pumping systems makes the same point: pressure drop across a throttled valve adds to the energy a pumping system uses, and adjustable speed drives (the family a VFD belongs to) are often recommended where flow needs vary ([U.S. Department of Energy, Pumping Systems Tip Sheet on control valves](https://www.energy.gov/sites/prod/files/2014/05/f16/control_valves_pumping_ts10.pdf)). A cycle stop valve trades some electrical efficiency for pump longevity and steadier pressure. For most private well owners that trade is worth it, but it's not a free upgrade.

The other real limit is minimum flow past the motor. Submersible motors rely on the water flowing past their outer shell for cooling, and manufacturers publish minimum flow requirements based on motor size and well casing diameter; Franklin Electric's own installation manual lists the required flow in feet per second and gives a table of minimum GPM by motor type and casing size, noting that a well too wide to reach that flow speed, or a pump set below the casing in open rock, needs a flow sleeve to meet it (small 4-inch motors of 2 HP and under only need flow above zero) ([Franklin Electric, Submersible Motors Application, Installation, Maintenance Manual](https://fele.widen.net/content/mrbmponfcz/pdf/M1311_60Hz_AIM_Manual.pdf)). A cycle stop valve has to be sized so its minimum bypass flow stays above that number for your specific pump and well. Get that wrong on a larger motor in a wide casing, or one hanging below the casing in open basalt, and you can starve the motor of cooling flow at the exact moments the valve is doing its job best, which is the opposite of what you installed it to do.

## Cycle Stop Valve vs. Variable Frequency Drive

The other way to get steady pressure and stop short cycling is to change the pump's speed instead of throttling its output. That's what a VFD-based constant pressure system does: it slows the motor down when demand is low and speeds it up when demand is high, so the pump only ever moves as much water as the house is asking for. We wrote a full breakdown of how these systems work and who they make sense for in [are constant pressure well systems worth it](/blog/constant-pressure-well-systems/), and a look at the pumps built for it in [variable-speed well pumps](/blog/variable-speed-well-pump/).

The short version: a VFD system is more efficient because it slows the motor down instead of fighting it with resistance, and it can hold pressure flatter across a wider flow range. It's also more expensive up front, has electronics that can fail, and often needs a motor the drive is built to work with, not just any submersible you already have in the ground. A cycle stop valve is the simpler, cheaper, all-mechanical option that works with the pump you already own. Neither one is automatically the right answer; it depends on your pump, your well, and your budget.

## Is a Cycle Stop Valve a Good Fit for Your Well?

| Your situation | How a cycle stop valve fits |
|---|---|
| Frequent small draws (a faucet, an icemaker, a single bathroom) causing rapid pump cycling | Good candidate; this is the exact problem it's built for |
| Wide range of demand, from a single tap to full orchard irrigation | Works, but sizing the minimum bypass flow correctly matters more here |
| Larger submersible motor in a wide casing, or set below the casing, already near its minimum cooling flow | Needs careful sizing; check the motor's minimum flow requirement before adding one |

## Installation and What to Check First

A cycle stop valve installs in the pipe between the pump's discharge and the pressure tank tee, upstream of the pressure switch, and it needs a functioning check valve below it to hold pressure between cycles. Before adding one, it's worth confirming three things: the pump's actual output at your system's operating pressure, the motor's minimum cooling flow for your casing size, and whether your current short cycling is really a flow-matching problem versus a failing switch or a waterlogged tank. Say the pump cycles the same way no matter which fixture you open, even a single toilet fill valve: that pattern points at the switch or tank, not at flow matching. If your pressure switch is chattering, sticking, or reading zero, that's worth ruling out first; see our guide to [common well pressure switch problems](/blog/pressure-switch-problems/) before assuming the fix is a new valve.

Sizing has to account for your well's actual yield and your pump's curve, not just the pipe size. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does a cycle stop valve replace my pressure switch or pressure tank?

No. A cycle stop valve works alongside both, not instead of either. You still need a pressure switch to turn the pump off completely when nobody's using water, and you still need a pressure tank to hold a working volume of water and absorb the small bypass flow the valve lets through when everything's closed. A cycle stop valve changes how the pump behaves while it's running; it doesn't remove the need for the switch or tank that control when it runs at all.

### Will a cycle stop valve let me install a smaller pressure tank?

Often, yes, within limits. Because the valve prevents the pump from dumping full flow into the tank on every small draw, you don't need as much stored drawdown volume to avoid rapid cycling as you would without one. That's a real benefit in a tight pump house or pit where a full-size tank won't fit. It's not a reason to go undersized on purpose, though: the tank still needs enough volume to handle the valve's minimum bypass flow and give the switch a reasonable run time. Size it against your specific pump and valve, not a guess.

### Can I add a cycle stop valve to any well pump?

Most constant-speed submersible and jet pumps can accept one, but the valve has to be sized correctly for your pump's flow rate, your system pressure, and, most importantly, your motor's minimum cooling flow requirement for your well's casing diameter. A motor in a wide casing, set below the casing, or already running close to its minimum flow spec is a case where sizing matters more, not less. Pumps built specifically for variable-speed operation with a VFD are a different category and aren't typically paired with a cycle stop valve.

### Does a cycle stop valve save electricity?

Not usually, and sometimes it costs a little more. The valve controls pressure by adding resistance while the motor keeps spinning at full speed, so the energy that would have gone into moving water gets absorbed as pressure drop across the valve instead. A variable frequency drive system saves energy because it slows the motor down; a cycle stop valve does not. What you're paying for with a CSV is steadier pressure and fewer pump starts, which extends component life, not a lower electric bill.
