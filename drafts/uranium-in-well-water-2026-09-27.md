# Uranium in Well Water: What It Means, How to Test, and What Removes It

If you already have a handle on the basics of whether your [well water is safe to drink](/blog/is-well-water-safe-to-drink/), uranium is one of the quieter items on that checklist. It does not show up in a glass of cloudy water or a whiff of rotten eggs. It shows up in a lab report, and only if you asked for the test.

## What Uranium Is Doing in Your Well Water

Uranium is not something someone dumped in the ground. It is a natural part of certain rock, and it has been there since long before anyone drilled a well. As groundwater moves through rock that contains uranium, tiny amounts dissolve into the water, the same way minerals that make water hard dissolve into it. The rock type matters more than almost anything else. The U.S. Geological Survey has found that [uranium shows up most often in groundwater drawn from granitic and metasedimentary rock](https://www.usgs.gov/centers/washington-water-science-center/science/uranium-groundwater), along with some younger sedimentary deposits. Basalt, the volcanic rock that underlies much of the Columbia Plateau in Douglas and Grant counties, is a different rock type, and it is not on that list.

That does not mean every well in orchard country is automatically clear. Bedrock changes as you move from the valley floor into the foothills, and nobody can tell you what is under your casing without a water sample. Guessing from a map is not a substitute for a test.

## The EPA Limit: 30 Micrograms Per Liter, and Why It Exists

Public water systems have to keep uranium under 30 micrograms per liter, also written as 30 parts per billion. [The EPA's radionuclides rule](https://www.epa.gov/dwreginfo/radionuclides-rule) sets that number as the maximum contaminant level. That federal rule covers public water systems, not private wells, so nobody enforces it on your well. The 30 µg/L limit is still the useful yardstick. You are just the one who has to apply it.

A result above 30 is not a household emergency, and a result just under it is not a clean bill of health either. It is a long-term exposure figure, and it deserves a level-headed response either way.

## What Uranium Exposure Actually Does to Your Body

The [Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSp/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=439&toxid=77) is specific about where uranium does its damage: the kidneys. Kidney damage has been seen in people and animals after ingesting uranium compounds. The agency is also clear that these effects come from uranium's chemistry, not its mild radioactivity. That distinction matters for how you think about the risk. This is not the same category of hazard as spent fuel or a contamination event. It behaves, chemically, more like a heavy metal your kidneys have to filter out, day after day, for years.

That slow timeline is the hard part with uranium. The concern is steady drinking over a long period, not one glass. And unlike sulfur, iron, or sediment, uranium gives you nothing to notice along the way. No taste, no smell, no color change. A lab test is the only way to know.

## What We Actually Know About Uranium in Chelan, Douglas, and Grant County

Be honest about what is actually documented here. The USGS work linked above covers northeastern Washington, in country with granitic and metasedimentary bedrock, where uranium in groundwater has been measured at nearly 40 times the health standard. That is a different part of the state from our service area, and much of it east of the Columbia sits on the basalt plateau.

We have not seen a USGS or Washington Department of Health study naming Chelan, Douglas, or Grant county wells as a documented uranium hot spot, and we are not going to pretend one exists. What we can say is that bedrock is not uniform across the region. It changes as you climb from the valley floor into the foothills around [Leavenworth](/well-service-leavenworth/) or Chelan, and rock type, not town name, drives uranium risk. If you own a well up in that terrain and you have never tested for it, that curiosity is reasonable. The answer either way is the same: test it, do not guess it.

## How to Test Your Well for Uranium

There is no home strip or color-change kit for uranium the way there is for chlorine or pH. It needs a water sample sent to an accredited lab, and the result comes back as a number in µg/L or ppb that you compare directly to the 30 µg/L standard. A basic checklist for doing it right:

1. Call a drinking water lab that is accredited for uranium specifically (certification is granted contaminant by contaminant) and ask for a uranium test, sometimes bundled into a "radionuclides" or "metals" panel.
2. Follow the lab's sample instructions exactly. A contaminated bottle or the wrong collection point can throw off the result.
3. Ask whether the same sample can be tested for arsenic at the same time. Both are naturally occurring and both can come from bedrock. Our [arsenic in well water guide](/blog/arsenic-in-well-water-washington/) covers what that test looks for.
4. If you have never checked for [radon in your well water](/blog/radon-in-well-water/) either, ask about that too. It is a separate test with its own sampling instructions, so ask the lab for both kits at once.
5. Keep the results with your other well paperwork. If you sell the property later, a buyer may ask what water testing has been done.

## What Actually Removes Uranium From Water

If a result comes back over 30 µg/L, or you would rather not have it there at all, the technology to take it out already exists. [University of Nebraska Extension](https://extensionpubs.unl.edu/publication/g1569/2014/html/view) lays out the methods that actually work, and it is a short list.

| Method | How it works | Where it usually fits |
|---|---|---|
| Anion exchange | Water passes through resin that swaps uranium for chloride | Whole house, moderate cost, needs periodic backwash and recharge |
| Reverse osmosis | Pressure forces water through a membrane fine enough to reject uranium | A kitchen tap unit, or a larger whole-house system |
| Distillation | Water is boiled and the vapor is condensed, leaving uranium behind | Single tap, slow, adds an energy cost |
| Adsorbent media (titanium dioxide and similar) | Uranium binds to the media surface as water passes through | Whole house or single tap, media needs periodic replacement |

Two things worth knowing before you shop for a fix. First, boiling your water does not remove uranium, and the Nebraska guide says pour-through, faucet-mount and activated carbon filters are not effective either, so do not count on the filter pitcher in the fridge. Second, a standard water softener is not the same thing as an anion exchange uranium system, even though both use resin and a brine tank. A softener swaps calcium and magnesium for sodium to fix hardness. That is a different job with different resin. [Ohio State University's watershed program](https://ohiowatersheds.osu.edu/node/1544) describes anion exchange as resin that trades uranium for chloride, which is not what a softener does. Reverse osmosis is the other option worth knowing about if you would rather solve it at the [whole-house level](/blog/reverse-osmosis-system-well-water/) instead of one tap.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does uranium in well water have a taste, smell, or color?
No. Uranium does not change how water tastes, smells, or looks, even at levels well above the EPA health-based limit. That is what makes it different from problems like sulfur or iron, which usually announce themselves right away. The only reliable way to know whether your well has uranium, and how much, is a laboratory water test. If your water looks or smells off, that is almost always a separate issue such as sulfur, iron, or sediment, not uranium.

### Will a water softener remove uranium from my well water?
Not reliably. A standard softener uses resin to swap calcium and magnesium for sodium, which solves hardness, not uranium. Removing uranium takes a resin system built and sized specifically for that job, known as anion exchange, or another dedicated method such as reverse osmosis or specialized adsorbent media. If a softener is the only treatment on your well, do not assume it has lowered your uranium; test the water at the tap to know.

### Is my well automatically at risk because I live in the foothills near Leavenworth or Chelan?
No, not automatically. Uranium risk tracks bedrock type, mainly granitic and metasedimentary rock, more than it tracks elevation or town name. The documented Washington cases are in the northeastern part of the state, not our service area, and we have not seen a study naming local wells as a hot spot. Foothill bedrock does differ from the basalt plateau, though, so if you are curious, a lab test settles it far better than assuming either way.
