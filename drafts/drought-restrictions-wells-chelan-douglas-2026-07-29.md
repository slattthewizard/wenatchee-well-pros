# Drought Restrictions and Your Well: What Actually Applies in Washington

Washington declares another drought year, the evening news shows a cracked reservoir bed, and your neighbor on city water starts talking about odd-even watering days. You're on a private well outside Wenatchee. Does any of that apply to you, or does it pass right over your kitchen tap?

Drought restrictions well Washington questions land on our phones every time the Department of Ecology makes a drought announcement, usually from people expecting the worst. The honest answer is more specific than most folks assume. Curtailment orders are aimed at a particular kind of water right, and a permit-exempt domestic well usually isn't it. That doesn't mean a drought year has zero effect on your water. It just means the effect isn't the one making the headlines.

This guide walks through what a Washington drought declaration actually triggers, who really gets curtailed and why, where your well fits into that system, and what a dry year does to your water table no matter what any letter from the state says. By the end you'll know the difference between a legal restriction and a physical one, and why you should still care about both.

## What a Washington Drought Declaration Actually Triggers

Ecology can declare a drought emergency for a basin, a region, or the whole state when the forecasted water supply drops below 75 percent of normal, measured against a rolling 30-year average, and the shortfall is likely to cause undue hardship for water users or the environment. North Central Washington has seen that threshold crossed more than once in recent years, usually tied to a thin Cascade snowpack the winter before.

A declaration by itself doesn't shut off anyone's water. It's a switch that turns on a set of tools. Ecology can issue emergency drought permits that let cities, irrigation districts, and farms move water around faster than the normal permitting process allows. The state legislature can free up emergency grant money for things like temporary pumping and drought-related infrastructure. And in basins where the shortage gets serious, Ecology or a court-appointed watermaster can start actively regulating water rights by priority date, which is the piece most people mean when they say "curtailment."

That last tool is the one that worries well owners, and it deserves its own explanation before you decide whether it touches your property. Ecology's [drought response page](https://ecology.wa.gov/water-shorelines/water-supply/water-availability/statewide-conditions/drought-response) tracks current declarations and basin conditions if you want to check where Chelan and Douglas counties stand in a given year.

## Curtailment 101: Junior Water Rights Go First, Not Domestic Wells

Washington runs on the prior appropriation doctrine for both surface water and groundwater, and it comes down to one phrase: first in time, first in right. Whoever put water to beneficial use first holds a senior right with an earlier priority date. Everyone who came later holds a junior right. When there isn't enough water to go around, seniors get their full share before juniors get anything at all.

Curtailment is how that rule gets enforced during a shortage. Ecology or a watermaster notifies junior right holders, sometimes by letter, sometimes through a daily hotline, telling them to reduce or stop withdrawals on specific days or for a full season. The rights that end up on the receiving end of those notices are almost always large, quantifiable permitted rights: irrigation districts, farms, and municipal systems drawing enough water to actually matter on a stream gauge or a shared aquifer.

The Columbia Basin Project districts around Quincy and Moses Lake, and the instream-flow-protected Wenatchee basin near Cashmere and Leavenworth, are the kinds of places where curtailment conversations happen most in our service area. ## Does Drought Curtailment Apply to Your Private Well?

Here's the nuance that trips people up. A permit-exempt domestic well, the kind covering most homes in Chelan and Douglas counties, is still a real water right with a real priority date, even though it skipped the formal permit application. That means it lives inside the same prior appropriation system as everything else, at least on paper.

In practice, individual domestic wells are almost never the target of a drought curtailment action. There are a few reasons for that. Domestic use under the exemption is already capped low, generally 5,000 gallons a day for a household, which is a fraction of what a permitted irrigation right can pull. Ecology's regulation tools are built around identifiable, metered, large-volume rights, not thousands of individual rural taps that are hard to track and harder to police day to day. And basic household water has historically been treated as close to a protected priority in how the state actually enforces shortages, even when the legal theory doesn't guarantee it.

That said, "almost never" isn't "never." A domestic exempt well established very recently in a basin with an active instream flow closure, or one shown to be directly impairing a senior right or a protected stream, could in theory become part of a broader regulation action. The 2016 Hirst decision made clear that courts take "an exempt well is still a water right" seriously, not just a formality. We walk through what the exemption does and doesn't cover, including Hirst, in our [permit-exempt wells guide](/blog/permit-exempt-wells-washington/).

The bottom line: the legal risk to your house tap during a drought is low, and it depends heavily on which watershed (WRIA) you sit in and when your well was established. It is not automatically zero just because the well is exempt.

| Water Right Type | Priority Basis | Curtailment Risk in a Drought | What a Dry Year Still Does |
|---|---|---|---|
| Domestic permit-exempt well | First beneficial use, no formal permit | Legally possible if impairing a senior right; rarely targeted in practice | Water table can still drop under the pump |
| Junior permitted irrigation right | Permit approval date | High; usually first to receive curtailment notices in a shortage | Full or partial shutoff by Ecology or watermaster order |
| Senior permitted water right | Early permit or pre-code claim | Low; protected until junior rights are exhausted | Rarely restricted directly |
| Stock watering (exempt) | Tied to the well's established use | Very low; not typically part of curtailment actions | Aquifer decline can still reduce well yield |

## What Drought Really Does to a Well, Rules or No Rules

None of the legal distinctions above change one basic fact: the aquifer under your property doesn't read Ecology's website. It responds to snowpack, rainfall, and how much everyone nearby is pumping, curtailment order or not.

A thin winter means less snowmelt recharging the groundwater through spring. Add a hot, dry summer with every orchard and hayfield in the valley pulling hard from the same shared aquifer, and water tables drop, sometimes several feet, sometimes more in a bad year. Your well doesn't have to run dry to feel it. It just has to drop below your pump intake, and most submersible pumps sit 20 to 50 feet above the bottom of the well.

The symptoms look the same whether or not your basin is under an active curtailment order: sputtering faucets, water pressure that fades by afternoon, a pump that runs longer than it used to. We wrote a full breakdown of those warning signs, and the look-alike problems that get mistaken for them, in our [well running dry guide](/blog/well-running-dry/). It's worth reading before a dry August catches you off guard.

The stakes get real when a pump actually starts sucking air. Running a submersible pump dry, even briefly, can burn out the motor in hours. That turns a routine service call into a full pump replacement, typically $1,800 to $4,500 depending on depth and horsepower. A dry-run protection device, usually a couple hundred dollars installed alongside other common part repairs, is cheap insurance against that outcome in a drought year.

## Protecting Your Well Through a Drought Year

A few habits make a real difference when the aquifer is running thin, whether or not your basin is under a formal drought declaration.

Watch your well's behavior in spring and again in late summer, and keep rough notes year to year. A well that drops predictably every August and recovers by fall is behaving normally. One that drops a little further every single year is telling you something worth acting on before it becomes an emergency.

Spread out heavy water use instead of stacking it. Running irrigation, laundry, and a full household shower rotation back to back stresses a marginal well far more than the same total gallons spread across the day.

Fix leaks fast. A running toilet or a dripping outdoor spigot can waste over a hundred gallons a day, which barely registers on a strong well and matters a great deal on a weak one.

Consider a dry-run protection device if you don't already have one. It shuts the pump off before it can run dry and burn out, and it's one of the cheapest insurance policies in the well business.

If your well has a history of low yield, or you're buying property that's sat vacant through a dry spell, get a service call scheduled before you assume anything. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can Washington actually shut off my private well during a drought?

Legally, it's possible if your well's water right is junior to a protected right and shown to cause impairment. In practice, curtailment orders during a drought almost always target larger permitted irrigation and agricultural rights, not individual domestic permit-exempt wells, since policing thousands of small home wells one at a time isn't how Ecology's regulation actions typically work. Your well's water table can still drop in a dry year, which can feel similar but is a completely different problem with a different fix.

### What's the difference between a curtailment order and my well just running low from drought?

A curtailment order is a legal notice telling a specific water right holder to reduce or stop withdrawals because of their priority date. If that ever applied to you, Ecology or a watermaster would contact you directly about your specific right. A well running low from drought has nothing to do with paperwork. It's the aquifer sitting lower because of thin snowpack and reduced recharge, and it shows up as sputtering faucets, fading pressure, or a pump running longer than it should.

### Do stock watering and irrigation exemptions on my property face the same drought rules as my house well?

Stock watering is exempt and unlimited under state law, and it's rarely targeted in drought regulation the way a permitted irrigation right is. If you also hold a separate permitted irrigation right on the same property, that right has its own priority date and can be curtailed independently of your domestic well or stock use, so it helps to know exactly which type of right covers each use.

### What should I do if I get a letter from Ecology or a watermaster during a drought year?

Read it carefully and note which specific water right or use it references, since it may only apply to an irrigation or commercial right and not your household tap. If you're unsure what it means for your property, Ecology's regional office can explain the specific action, and a water rights attorney is worth the call when real money is on the line. None of that replaces getting your well and pump physically checked if you're also seeing signs of low water at the same time.
