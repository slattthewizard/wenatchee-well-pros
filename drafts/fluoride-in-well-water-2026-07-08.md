## Why Fluoride Shows Up in Wenatchee-Area Well Water

Fluoride is a naturally occurring mineral. It leaches out of certain rock formations as groundwater moves through them over years or decades. The Columbia River Basalt Group, the layered volcanic rock that underlies much of North Central Washington, is exactly the kind of formation known to release fluoride into the aquifers below it.

That's different from most other well water contaminants we write about. Nitrates and bacteria usually trace back to something on the surface: septic systems, fertilizer, livestock. Fluoride comes from underneath, from the rock itself. You can't fix the source. You can only test for it and treat the water if the number is too high.

Wells drilled deeper into basalt formations tend to show more fluoride than shallow wells pulling from sand and gravel aquifers. That doesn't mean deep wells are automatically bad. Plenty of deep basalt wells in the Wenatchee area test completely normal. It just means depth and geology matter more here than they do in a lot of the country, and it's part of why we always ask about well construction when someone calls with a water quality question.

## Fluoride in Well Water vs. City Water: What's the Difference

City water systems that fluoridate control the dose. Municipal treatment plants dial fluoride to the level public health agencies recommend for cavity prevention, currently 0.7 milligrams per liter (mg/L), and they monitor it constantly. Learn more from the [CDC's overview of community water fluoridation](https://www.cdc.gov/fluoridation/basics/index.html).

Well water has no such control. Whatever concentration is naturally in your aquifer is what comes out of your tap, whether that's zero or well above what's considered safe. Nobody's adjusting it, and nobody's watching it change over time unless you test it yourself.

That cuts both ways. Plenty of wells in our service area have low or undetectable fluoride, which means kids on those wells might benefit from a fluoride supplement their dentist recommends. Other wells run high enough that the household needs to actively reduce fluoride intake. You genuinely can't guess which side of that line your well falls on without a test.

## How Much Fluoride Is Too Much? Health Effects Explained

The EPA sets two separate numbers for fluoride, and the difference matters.

The enforceable limit, called the maximum contaminant level, is 4.0 mg/L. Drinking water above that level over a lifetime is linked to skeletal fluorosis, a bone and joint condition that develops slowly from years of high exposure. It's rare in the U.S., but it's the reason the limit exists.

The secondary standard, which is a guideline rather than a hard rule, is 2.0 mg/L. Above that level, kids under 8 whose teeth are still forming are at risk for dental fluorosis, which shows up as white spots, streaks, or in severe cases brown staining and pitting on permanent teeth. It's cosmetic, not dangerous to overall health, but it's permanent once the teeth come in, and most parents would rather avoid it entirely. You can read the full standard in the [EPA's National Primary Drinking Water Regulations](https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water/national-primary-drinking-water-regulations).

For adults, low to moderate fluoride exposure isn't something to lose sleep over. The people who need to pay closest attention are households with young kids, especially if formula is being mixed with well water, since infants get a concentrated, repeated dose during exactly the window when their teeth are developing.

## How to Test Your Well for Fluoride

Fluoride testing is simple and affordable. A standalone fluoride test through a state-certified lab typically runs $25 to $50, and most labs turn results around in a few business days. It's often bundled into broader well water panels that also check arsenic, nitrates, and bacteria, which makes sense given how often these contaminants share the same geologic sources in our region.

Our general recommendation for wells across Chelan, Douglas, and Grant counties:

- **Test fluoride once**, if you've never tested your well before, since geology-driven fluoride levels don't swing much year to year the way surface contaminants can.
- **Retest after any major change** in well depth, a new well being drilled nearby, or if you're buying a property and don't have prior results.
- **Test again if a child in the house develops new tooth staining**, especially if it appears symmetrically on multiple teeth, which is a classic sign of fluorosis rather than a cavity or injury.

## How to Remove Fluoride From Well Water

This is where a lot of homeowners get tripped up, because the most common home water treatment devices don't touch fluoride at all. A standard carbon filter, including the pitcher filters everyone keeps in the fridge, will not remove it. Neither will a conventional water softener, since softeners are built to swap out calcium and magnesium, not fluoride ions.

For most families with a moderately elevated result, an under-sink reverse osmosis unit is the practical starting point. Since fluoride exposure risk is almost entirely about what you drink and cook with, not what you shower in, treating the kitchen tap covers the meaningful risk without the cost of a whole-house system. Budget $100 to $150 a year for filter and membrane replacement.

Activated alumina is the fluoride specialist of the group and tends to hold up well over long service life if it's sized correctly for your flow rate and fluoride concentration. Whole-house reverse osmosis makes the most sense when fluoride is high and you want every fixture covered, or when other contaminants are in the mix too, the way arsenic sometimes shows up alongside fluoride in basalt aquifers.

Whatever you install, get the treated water retested after setup to confirm it's actually working, and then annually after that. Treatment media does wear out.

## Protecting Your Family and Your Well Going Forward

Since fluoride comes from geology and not from anything happening on your property, there's no prevention step that changes the source the way there is for nitrates or bacteria. Your job is knowing your number and treating accordingly.

A few practical habits make a real difference:

**Test before you assume, especially with kids in the house.** If you've got a baby on formula, young kids with developing teeth, or a pregnancy in the family, get a fluoride test done rather than guessing. It's a small cost against a permanent outcome.

**Keep your results on file and share them with your dentist.** Dentists base fluoride supplement recommendations for kids partly on water source. A well owner who shows up with an actual lab result gets better guidance than one who just says "we're on a well."

**Check in on treatment equipment during other well work.** If you're already having your pump serviced or a pressure tank swapped out, it's a good time to have a tech look at your treatment system too. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a safe level of fluoride in well water?

The EPA's enforceable limit is 4.0 mg/L, above which skeletal fluorosis risk rises with long-term exposure. The secondary guideline of 2.0 mg/L is where dental fluorosis risk starts for kids under 8 whose teeth are still forming. Many public health agencies point to 0.7 mg/L as the optimal level for cavity protection, so results below that may mean your family gets no natural cavity-fighting benefit from the water.

### Can I taste or see fluoride in my well water?

No. Fluoride has no taste, smell, or color at the levels found in well water, even well above safe limits. The only reliable way to know your level is a lab test. Most families who discover a fluoride problem find out through a child's dental exam rather than anything noticeable at the tap.

### Does boiling or filtering with a Brita remove fluoride?

Boiling does nothing to fluoride and can slightly concentrate it as water evaporates off. Standard carbon pitcher filters, including Brita and similar brands, are not designed to remove fluoride either. You need reverse osmosis, activated alumina, or distillation, which are the only home treatment methods proven to reduce fluoride levels.

### Is fluoride in well water linked to arsenic too?

Both fluoride and arsenic can leach from the same basalt rock formations common under North Central Washington, so wells with elevated fluoride sometimes test high for arsenic as well. They're not guaranteed to travel together, but it's common enough that we recommend testing for both whenever you're checking one. A combined panel from a certified lab typically costs less than testing separately.
