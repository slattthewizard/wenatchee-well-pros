# Pressure Tank Tee: Parts, Layout, and Common Leaks

If you have ever pulled the door off your pump house and found a cluster of pipe, gauges, and a wired switch crowded around the top of your pressure tank, that cluster is the tank tee. It is a small piece of plumbing that carries a lot of the system's information and controls: the pressure switch that starts and stops your pump, the gauge you read, the relief valve that protects the tank, and usually a drain. Most of it is threaded fittings, and threaded fittings are where systems leak.

Knowing what belongs at the tee, and what each part is supposed to do, makes it a lot easier to tell a nuisance drip from something that needs attention now.

## What a Pressure Tank Tee Actually Is

The "tee" is really shorthand for a small assembly, not one single fitting. On most residential systems it is a brass or galvanized tee (sometimes a cross, if there are four ports instead of three) threaded into the top of the pressure tank or into the pipe run right next to it. From that tee, short nipples and additional fittings branch out to hold the pressure switch, the gauge, the relief valve, and a shutoff or union that lets a tech isolate the tank for service.

Tank tee kits are also sold as a package, a single tee body with the fittings for the switch, gauge, relief valve and drain supplied together for field assembly. For the tank side of the picture, [Amtrol's own installation manual](https://fwwebbimage.fwwebb.com/ProductInfo/9016868_MC10185_09_16_WXT_IO.pdf) for its Well-X-Trol tanks shows where the relief valve and pressure switch sit relative to the tank in its factory diagrams, which beats guessing from what is in front of you.

## The Parts Gathered at the Tee

Here is what you should expect to find, and what trouble looks like for each one.

| Part | What it does | What trouble looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Pressure gauge | Shows current system pressure | Needle stuck, reads far off from what the switch settings suggest, or leaks at its base |
| Pressure switch | Starts the pump at cut-in, stops it at cut-out | Pump short cycles, will not start, or will not shut off |
| Pressure relief valve | Vents water if pressure runs away | Weeping or dripping, especially right after the pump shuts off |
| Boiler drain / drain valve | Lets you drain the tank and lines for service | Won't seat, drips constantly, or is missing entirely on an older setup |
| Union or shutoff | Lets a tech isolate the tank without draining the whole house line | Corroded stuck, or absent, which turns every small job into draining the whole system |
| Check valve (on some systems) | Keeps water from draining back down the well when the pump stops | Pump "waterhammers" on start, or the tank loses pressure fast between cycles |

Not every tee has all six. Older installs, especially ones that have been added onto over the years, are often missing the union or the drain, which is part of why service on them takes longer than it should.

If you want the deeper walkthrough on reading and setting the gauge and switch together, our [guide to checking pressure tank air pressure](/blog/pressure-tank-air-pressure/) covers that step by step; this article stays focused on the tee itself.

## Why a Tidy Tee Matters

A tee that was assembled with some care, with a shutoff or union in the right place, matched thread types, and enough clearance to get a wrench in, pays for itself the first time something needs service. A tech can isolate the tank, swap a switch, or clear a clogged gauge port without draining every line in the house or shutting off water to the whole property.

A tee that was thrown together with mismatched fittings, no isolation valve, and everything crammed too close together turns a fifteen-minute switch swap into an hour of fighting corroded galvanized pipe. If your tee looks like that and you are already having repeat problems with the switch or gauge, it is often worth rebuilding the whole cluster at once rather than patching it fitting by fitting. A [pressure tank replacement](/pressure-tank-replacement-wenatchee/) is a natural point to rebuild the tee properly at the same time, since the tank is already disconnected.

## Where the Check Valve Belongs

This is one of the more common points of confusion, because the answer depends on the kind of pump you have.

On a jet pump system, the job of holding water in the suction line usually falls to a foot valve at the bottom of the suction pipe in the well, or an in-line check valve on the suction side, not to a valve at the tank tee.

On a submersible pump system, the primary check valve almost always lives down in the well, at or just above the pump itself, not at the tee. Some submersible setups also carry a second check valve up near the tank as a backup, but the one doing the main work of holding pressure in the drop pipe between pump cycles is downhole, out of sight. If you are chasing water-hammer noise or a tank that loses pressure unusually fast between pump runs, our guide to [well pump check valve types](/blog/well-pump-check-valve-types/) walks through where each style sits and how they fail; it is not something to diagnose from the tee alone.

## Common Leaks at the Tee, and What Each One Means

Most tee leaks show up in one of a handful of predictable spots. Work through them in this order before you assume the worst:

1. **Weeping at a threaded joint.** Old pipe dope or tape dries out, or a fitting was never tightened enough. Usually a straightforward fix: shut the water off, drain that section through the boiler drain, and redo the joint.
2. **A steady drip from the relief valve.** Often the valve is doing its job because pressure somewhere in the system is running higher than it should. See the section below before you touch it.
3. **Water pooling under the pressure switch.** Stop here. Do not touch it live. Shut off the breaker to the pump circuit and confirm it is dead with a meter before you go near the switch housing. A wet switch is both a shock hazard and usually a sign the diaphragm or a nearby fitting has failed.
4. **A leak right at the union or shutoff.** Often just a loose packing nut or a worn gasket, but on an older brass union it can mean the fitting itself is worn out and needs replacing, not just tightening.
5. **Rust-colored water or crumbling metal at an old galvanized fitting.** This is corrosion, not a simple leak, and it usually means the whole fitting (sometimes the whole tee) is past the point of patching.

If you find active leaking you cannot isolate with a nearby shutoff, or the switch housing itself is wet, that is a good time to call **[(509) 300-5151](tel:+15093005151)** rather than keep working on it.

## The Relief Valve: The One Fitting You Never Cap

The pressure relief valve exists to vent water if system pressure climbs past a safe limit, and it is designed to open under specific conditions, not to be silent all the time. A drip from the relief valve after the pump shuts off is a symptom. Two causes to rule out first:

- **A pressure switch that is not cutting out where it should.** If the switch is stuck or misadjusted and the pump keeps running past its normal cut-out point, pressure climbs until the relief valve has to vent it. Our [pressure switch troubleshooting guide](/blog/pressure-switch-problems/) covers how to check this safely.
- **A waterlogged tank.** When a bladder or diaphragm tank loses its air charge, the tank can no longer cushion pressure swings the way it is supposed to, and pressure spikes hit the relief valve more often. [What a waterlogged pressure tank looks like](/blog/pressure-tank-waterlogged/) covers the symptoms and the fix.

If the switch cuts out where it should and the tank's air charge checks out, the valve itself may be worn or have grit on its seat, and the fix is a new relief valve of the right rating, not a plug. Whatever you do, never plug, cap, or tie down a dripping relief valve. Blocking it turns a small drip into a real overpressure risk. Amtrol's manual, for example, calls for a relief valve at the tank's connection to the system piping, set to open at excessive pressure and able to discharge the pump's full capacity, which is not something to improvise with a cap or a plug.

## Rebuilding a Tank Tee: What the Job Involves and What It Costs

A full tee rebuild usually means shutting the water off, draining the tank and lines, removing the old fittings, and reassembling with new nipples, unions, gauge, switch, and relief valve, then repressurizing and checking for leaks at every joint before closing things back up. It is straightforward work for someone who has done it, and a good time to also check the tank's own air charge while everything is drained down.

Costs vary with what actually needs replacing. A basic service call and diagnosis to figure out where a tee leak is coming from runs $150 - $250, which is applied toward the repair if you hire us to do it. ## Local Notes: Hard Water, Freeze, and Old Fittings

A few things specific to wells in Chelan, Douglas, and Grant counties are worth knowing about your tee. Groundwater across much of the Columbia Plateau's basalt geology tends to run hard and mineral-heavy, which crusts up gauge ports and switch nipples over time. Old galvanized fittings also corrode first at the threads, where the wall is thinnest, which is part of why a tee full of old galvanized pipe tends to develop leaks sooner than one built with brass fittings.

Winters here bring hard freezes, and a pump house or wellhead enclosure that is not insulated can let a tee freeze and split, especially at the drain valve, which sits lowest and holds standing water. If your pump house runs cold in January, it is worth checking that the tee and any exposed drain are protected before the next freeze, not after a fitting has already cracked.

On older properties, a tee can end up added onto piecemeal over decades, with mismatched thread types and no isolation valve at all. If that describes your setup and you are dealing with more than one small leak, treat it as a sign the whole cluster is due for a rebuild rather than another round of pipe dope.

For general guidance on keeping a private well system in working order year to year, [WSU Extension's well maintenance tips](https://s3.wp.wsu.edu/uploads/sites/2079/2014/02/wellMaintenance.pdf) and the [CDC's guidance on private well upkeep](https://www.cdc.gov/drinking-water/safety/) both cover the broader annual-check habits that catch small problems before they become bigger ones.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a pressure tank tee?

It is the cluster of fittings, usually built around one tee or cross fitting, threaded into the plumbing right at or near the pressure tank. It holds the pressure switch that runs the pump, the pressure gauge, the pressure relief valve, and typically a drain valve and a union or shutoff for service. Not every tee has all of these; older or piecemeal installs are often missing the union or the drain, which makes future service slower.

### Why is my pressure relief valve dripping?

A relief valve usually drips because it is venting pressure that has climbed higher than it should. The two causes to check first are a pressure switch that is not cutting out at the right point, so the pump keeps running past its normal stop, or a waterlogged pressure tank that has lost its air charge and can no longer smooth out pressure swings. Never cap or plug a dripping relief valve. Check the switch and the tank's air charge first; if both are fine, the valve itself may be worn and due for replacement.

### Can I replace a leaking tank tee fitting myself?

A weeping threaded joint on the water side, with the system drained and depressurized, is within reach for a handy homeowner comfortable with pipe fittings. Anything involving the pressure switch is different: shut off the breaker and confirm it is dead with a meter before you open the switch housing, since it is a live electrical component. If the leak is at a fitting you cannot isolate, or you are not confident working around a wet electrical component, call a professional rather than guess.

### How much does it cost to fix a leaking tank tee?

It depends on what actually needs replacing. A service call and diagnosis to locate the leak runs $150 - $250, applied toward the repair if you hire the work. If the pressure switch needs replacing, that typically runs $150 - $350. If the tank itself has failed and the tee is being rebuilt as part of a tank swap, a pressure tank replacement runs $600 - $1,800 depending on size and what the existing plumbing needs redone.
