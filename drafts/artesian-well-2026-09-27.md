# What Is an Artesian Well? A Plain-English Guide for Well Owners in Central Washington

If someone told you your well is "artesian" and you nodded along without really knowing what that meant, you are not alone. The word gets used loosely, on bottled water labels and in casual conversation about wells, and most of that usage is a little off. Here is what artesian actually means, why a flowing well is a different animal from a well that just happens to be deep, and what it means for you if you own one in Chelan, Douglas, or Grant County.

## What "Artesian" Actually Means

Most household wells tap an unconfined aquifer: a layer of saturated sand, gravel, or fractured rock with nothing solid capping it. The water table in that kind of aquifer sits open to the surface above it, more or less, and it rises and falls with rainfall and pumping nearby.

An artesian well is different. It taps a confined aquifer, one sandwiched between layers of much less permeable material, such as clay or dense basalt, above and below it. Groundwater in a confined aquifer is under pressure from the weight and geometry of that overlying layer. When a well punches through the confining layer into the aquifer, that pressure pushes the water up the casing well above the point where it first entered. The level the water would settle at if nothing stopped it is called the potentiometric surface, sometimes called the piezometric level, and it can sit anywhere from a few feet below the ground to well above it, according to the [USGS Water Science School's explanation of artesian water](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/artesian-water-and-artesian-wells).

The name itself comes from Artois, an old region of France, where wells with this behavior were being drilled as early as the Middle Ages. It has nothing to do with depth. A well can be 600 feet deep and not artesian at all, and a shallower well can be a textbook artesian well, if it happens to sit over the right kind of confined aquifer. Depth alone does not make a well artesian; the geology above the aquifer does.

## Flowing vs. Non-Flowing: Two Kinds of Artesian Well

This is the distinction that actually matters for a homeowner, and it is where a lot of casual explanations get muddy.

If the potentiometric surface sits below ground level, the water rises in the casing under its own pressure but stops short of the surface. You still need a pump to lift it the rest of the way, same as any other well, but the pump may not have to work as hard because it is starting from a higher point than a well in an unconfined aquifer at the same depth. This is a non-flowing artesian well.

If the potentiometric surface sits above ground level, water pushes all the way to the surface and keeps coming, on its own, with no pump at all. This is a flowing artesian well, and it is the case people usually mean when they picture "artesian water." Washington's own well construction rule defines the term to cover both cases: under [WAC 173-160-111](https://apps.leg.wa.gov/wac/default.aspx?cite=173-160-111), an artesian well is one tapping an aquifer bounded above and below by confining layers, and the definition adds: "This term includes both flowing and nonflowing wells." Legally, artesian is about the aquifer, not about whether water is running out of the top of your casing.

| Aquifer type | Unconfined, open water table | Confined, under pressure | Confined, under pressure |
| Water level before pumping | At the water table | Above the aquifer, below ground | Above ground, water discharges on its own |

## How to Tell If Your Well Is Artesian

Guessing from symptoms is unreliable. The way to know for certain is to pull the well report, called a water well report in Washington, filed with the Department of Ecology when the well was constructed. It will state the aquifer conditions the driller encountered, including whether artesian pressure was noted. If you have not already tracked down your well's paperwork, our guide to [finding the well on your property](/blog/how-to-find-a-well-on-your-property/) walks through where those records live and how to request them.

A few loose clues can point you toward asking the question, though none of them prove anything on their own: water rising quickly in the casing right after drilling, a static water level well above where the water-bearing zone was hit, or a driller's comment about "artesian conditions" during the original job. If your property has a visibly flowing wellhead, that part is not subtle, but plenty of non-flowing artesian wells look completely ordinary from the surface. When in doubt, the well log is the source, not a guess based on how the pump sounds.

## Why Washington Requires a Control Valve on a Flowing Well

Washington does not leave flowing wells to run however they run. Under [WAC 173-160-251](https://apps.leg.wa.gov/wac/default.aspx?cite=173-160-251), when a well flows at land surface, "it must be equipped with a control valve so that flow can be completely stopped." The same rule requires the driller to seal the casing into the confining layer so water cannot leak around it, and it requires a written sealing plan, with a contingency for what happens if the flow cannot be controlled, whenever flowing artesian conditions are known or suspected before drilling even starts.

The reasoning is straightforward. A flowing well is discharging groundwater continuously, whether or not anyone in the house is using a drop of it. Left unchecked, that is water leaving the aquifer around the clock, erosion and standing water at the wellhead, and in this part of the state, a real freeze risk where that discharge sits and pools through a Wenatchee Valley or foothills winter. A functioning control valve is what turns an uncontrolled leak into a well you can actually manage.

## Living With a Flowing Well: A Homeowner Checklist

If your property has, or might have, a flowing artesian well, work through this list:

1. **Find the control valve and test it.** Confirm it can shut the flow off completely, not just reduce it.
2. **Check the wellhead area after storms and in winter.** Standing water, erosion, or ice buildup around the casing are signs the discharge is not being managed the way the rule intends.
3. **Pull the water well report.** Confirm the aquifer and casing details match what you were told, and note whether a control valve was documented.
4. **Never cap or plug the discharge without a working valve in place.** Shutting in artesian pressure with makeshift fittings can strain the casing and its seal and push water out around the outside of the casing, the kind of leakage the state's sealing rule exists to prevent.
5. **If you are buying a property with a flowing well, get the well report before closing.** Ask directly whether the valve has ever been tested.

## Artesian Wells and Your Home's Water System

Here is a common misunderstanding worth clearing up: a flowing well at the surface is not the same thing as pressurized water at your kitchen faucet. Flow at the wellhead just means the aquifer is pushing water up and out on its own. Getting that water through a pressure tank and plumbing at a steady, usable pressure at every fixture in the house still takes a pump and tank system in almost every case, the same as a conventional well.

For a non-flowing artesian well, nothing about the pump side changes at all. It works like any other submersible or jet pump installation; the only real difference is where the static water level sits before the pump kicks on. For a flowing well, some setups do use the natural flow to feed a storage tank or cistern that a booster pump then draws from, but that is a specific design choice, not something you get automatically just because the well is artesian.

## Artesian Wells, Water Rights, and Permit-Exempt Use in Washington

A flowing well raises a fair question: if water is coming out on its own, does it count as "using" water? Physically, water running out of a flowing well is leaving the aquifer whether it is captured in the house or simply runs onto the ground, which is one practical reason a valve that can stop the flow completely matters. How water law treats that flow is a question for Ecology or a water rights professional, not something to assume.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is artesian well water safe to drink?

Artesian water is not inherently safer or less safe than water from any other well. It is the same groundwater, just under natural pressure from a confined aquifer, according to the [USGS Water Science School](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/artesian-water-and-artesian-wells). "Artesian" describes how the water reaches the surface, not its quality. Private well owners are responsible for their own water safety since [private wells are not regulated the way public water systems are](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells). Test an artesian well the same way you would test any private well, through an accredited lab, regardless of how the water arrives at the surface.

### How can I tell if my well is artesian?

The reliable way is the water well report filed with the Department of Ecology when the well was drilled. It records the aquifer conditions the driller encountered, including whether artesian pressure was present. Loose signs, like water rising fast right after drilling or a well that flows on its own at the surface, can point you toward the question, but only the well log confirms it. If you cannot locate your well's paperwork, our guide to finding the well on your property explains where those records are kept and how to request a copy.

### Does an artesian well mean I have an unlimited water supply?

No. Artesian describes pressure, not quantity. A confined aquifer can be a strong producer or a weak one, exactly like an unconfined aquifer, and pressure at the wellhead tells you nothing about how many gallons per minute the aquifer can sustain over time. A well can flow steadily at the surface and still be a low-yield well if you draw on it harder than the aquifer recharges. Yield and pressure are separate questions, and a flowing well does not exempt you from either drought concerns or normal water-use planning.

### What should I do if my well starts flowing at the surface on its own?

Do not plug or cap the discharge without a working control valve in place; makeshift fittings can strain the casing seal. Check whether a valve exists at the wellhead and confirm it fully stops the flow, then pull the water well report to see whether artesian conditions were documented at construction. If there is no valve, or the one you have does not fully shut off, that is casing work for a licensed well driller.
