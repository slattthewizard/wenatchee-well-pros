# High pH Well Water: What It Means and What to Do About It

Your water tastes a little like baking soda, feels slippery between your fingers even after you rinse the soap off, and there's a chalky white crust building up around the tub spout and the pressure tank fittings. None of that means your water is unsafe. It usually means the pH is running high, and it's one of the more straightforward water chemistry questions to sort out once you know what you're looking at.

## What pH Actually Measures

pH measures how acidic or basic water is on a scale of 0 to 14. Seven is neutral. Anything below 7 is acidic, anything above 7 is basic, or alkaline. The scale is logarithmic, so a jump from pH 7 to pH 8 isn't a small step, it's a tenfold change in the concentration of hydrogen ions.
pH by itself doesn't tell you whether water is contaminated. It tells you how the water behaves: how it reacts with pipe and fixture materials, how well soap rinses off, how effectively chlorine disinfects it. That's why it's handled as an aesthetic and operational issue rather than a health rule.

## Where the EPA Draws the Line

The EPA's [Secondary Drinking Water Standards](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/secondary-drinking-water-standards-guidance-nuisance-chemicals) set a recommended pH range of 6.5 to 8.5. Secondary standards aren't enforceable health limits the way the ones for arsenic or nitrate are. They're aesthetic guidelines aimed at taste, appearance, and how water treats your plumbing. Water outside that range isn't automatically unsafe to drink, but the EPA notes real effects at both ends: below 6.5, a bitter or metallic taste and corrosion; above 8.5, a slippery feel, a soda-like taste, and mineral deposits.

One thing worth knowing: hardness itself isn't on that secondary standards list. It only gets a mention as an effect of high total dissolved solids, not as its own regulated number. pH and hardness often show up together on the same well because they come from the same rock chemistry, but they're measured and corrected differently. ## Why Groundwater Here Tends Toward Alkaline

Much of Douglas and Grant counties sits on the Columbia Plateau basalt aquifer system, a stack of ancient lava flows and interbedded sediment underlying much of central and eastern Washington. A [2019 USGS assessment of the Columbia Plateau basaltic-rock aquifers](https://pubs.usgs.gov/fs/2019/3058/fs20193058.pdf) sampled public-supply wells across the plateau in Washington, Oregon, and Idaho, and found that groundwater pH exceeded the EPA's 6.5 to 8.5 range in some areas. Specifically, pH came in above 8.5, which the report calls alkaline, in about 7 percent of the study area.

That's not a claim that every well in the region runs alkaline. Across most of the study area, pH wasn't above that range. But it's a real, measured pattern in the same regional basalt aquifer that many local wells draw from, including around [Quincy](/well-service-quincy/) and [Moses Lake](/well-service-moses-lake/). It's part of why a homeowner out here is more likely to run into a high-pH well than someone pulling water from a sand-and-gravel aquifer with no basalt in the mix.

## What High pH Looks and Feels Like at the Tap

Because pH is a secondary issue, most people notice the symptoms long before they ever think to test the actual number.

| pH range | What you are likely to notice |
|---|---|
| Below 6.5 (acidic) | Bitter or metallic taste, blue-green staining on fixtures, pinhole leaks in copper pipe |
| 6.5 to 8.5 (EPA's target range) | No pH-related taste, staining, or scale complaints |
| Above 8.5 (alkaline) | Slippery or soapy feel, a soda-like or bitter aftertaste, white or gray scale on faucets and glassware |

Say your gauge and glass both look fine, but a neighbor on the next well over mentions the same slippery feel. That's a decent sign it's a regional groundwater trait rather than something specific to your property, though the only way to know your own number is to actually test it.

## What High pH Does to Your Well Equipment

This is where it stops being a taste question and starts costing money. Alkaline water tends to leave mineral scale wherever it sits or heats up: inside the pressure tank, on the diaphragm of the pressure switch, around the seat of a check valve, inside a water heater tank. Mineral buildup in a pressure switch's small inlet or on its diaphragm can make it stick or respond late, which shows up as pressure swings or erratic cycling. Deposits in fittings, valves, and the tank connection add wear on parts that would otherwise last longer.

None of that happens overnight. It's a slow tax on parts that would otherwise last years longer. ## High pH and Disinfection: Why It Matters at Shock Chlorination Time

pH also changes how well chlorine works, which matters if your well ever needs shock chlorination for a bacteria hit. Chlorine's disinfecting power comes mostly from hypochlorous acid, and the [CDC's guideline on chemical disinfectants](https://www.cdc.gov/infection-control/hcp/disinfection-sterilization/chemical-disinfectants.html) explains that as pH climbs, more of it converts into hypochlorite ion, a less effective germicide, so chlorine's disinfecting efficacy drops as pH rises. Maine's drinking water program makes the same point specifically for wells in its [shock chlorination guidance](https://www.maine.gov/dhhs/mecdc/healthy-living/health-and-safety/drinking-water-safety/drinking-water-professionals/resources-shock-chlorination-of-water-systems-using-bedrock-wells): chlorine's effectiveness decreases dramatically in high pH water, and adding chlorine itself pushes the pH up further. In practical terms, the more alkaline the water, the less a given dose of chlorine actually does.

That's one more reason to know your pH before you shock a well rather than after. It doesn't change the basic shock chlorination process, which our [step-by-step shock chlorination guide](/blog/well-chlorination-shock/) walks through, but Maine's guidance recommends getting help from a water professional to shock a high pH well effectively, and a retest afterward is the only way to confirm the bacteria are actually gone.

## Testing, and What Actually Corrects High pH

A cheap test strip is fine for a first look, but strips can be hard to read precisely in the range that matters most here, somewhere between 7.5 and 9. If the number is going to drive a decision about buying and installing a neutralizer, get it from an accredited lab along with the rest of a standard well water panel. Our guide to [well water testing in Wenatchee](/blog/well-water-testing-wenatchee/) covers what a standard panel checks and how to pull a proper sample.

If you suspect high pH, here's a reasonable way to work through it:

1. Note the actual symptoms: slippery feel, soda taste, visible scale, or maybe none of the above and you're just curious about the number.
2. Pull a sample and send it to an accredited lab, or use a calibrated meter rather than strips if the reading needs to be precise.
3. Compare the result to the EPA's 6.5 to 8.5 range, and look at the rest of the panel too. Hardness, iron, and manganese often ride along with pH on basalt-aquifer wells and can explain part of what you're seeing.
4. If pH alone is the driver, get a quote from a water treatment company. Watch the equipment names here: calcite neutralizing filters and soda ash feeders are for acidic water and raise pH, so they're the wrong tool. For pH above 8.5, the [Water Systems Council's pH fact sheet](https://www.watersystemscouncil.org/download/wellcare_information_sheets/potential_groundwater_contaminant_information_sheets/pH.pdf) lists a special ion exchange unit designed to reduce alkalinity, or a chemical feed pump that injects a weak acid solution. That's not something we install, since it's treatment equipment rather than well or pump work.
5. If the symptoms are really pump, tank, or switch wear from years of scale, that's our lane, and you can [get a free estimate](/#contact) on that side of the job. Call the well side in first, since a treatment system bolted onto a tired pressure tank just delays the tank replacement instead of avoiding it.

Testing and treatment sit in the same conversation, but they're two different jobs done by two different trades. Knowing which one you actually need before you spend money is most of the battle.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is high pH well water safe to drink?

In most cases, yes. pH is regulated as a secondary, aesthetic standard rather than a health limit, so a reading above 8.5 isn't the same kind of concern as bacteria, nitrate, or arsenic. It mainly affects taste, feel, and how the water treats plumbing and fixtures. That said, high pH is worth testing for, since it often travels with other minerals worth knowing about, and since it's easy enough to confirm with a proper lab test instead of guessing from symptoms alone.

### Why does my well water feel slippery and taste like soda?

Both are classic signs of pH above the EPA's 8.5 upper limit. Alkaline water changes how soap rinses off skin, which reads as a slippery or soapy feel, and it can carry a flat, soda-like or slightly bitter aftertaste. Neither symptom on its own proves the number, so a test is still the right next step, but if you're noticing both together, high pH is a reasonable first guess before you troubleshoot anything else.

### Will a water softener fix high pH?

Not directly. A softener is built to remove calcium and magnesium hardness through ion exchange, and it isn't designed to correct pH. Lowering high pH generally calls for either a special ion exchange unit designed to reduce alkalinity or a chemical feed pump that injects a weak acid, sized to your water's actual chemistry and flow rate. Calcite and soda ash systems do the opposite: they raise pH. A water treatment company can size and install the right equipment. If your water has both hardness and high pH, which often happens together since the same dissolved minerals drive both, you may end up needing a softener and a neutralizer.

### How do I know if scale is from high pH or hard water?

You often can't tell from the scale alone, since hard, alkaline groundwater tends to produce both symptoms at once. A full lab test that reports pH and hardness (usually as grains per gallon or mg per liter) separates the two. As a rough rule, white or gray crust on fixtures and glassware points toward hardness and alkalinity together. If you're also seeing pressure swings or a pump that short cycles, have the pressure switch and tank checked, which is a well-side repair rather than a water treatment fix.
