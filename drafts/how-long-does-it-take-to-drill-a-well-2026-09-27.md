# How Long Does It Take to Drill a Well in Washington?

The drilling itself is the fast part. A crew can put a hole in the ground for a typical residential well in a day or two once the rig is on site. What actually stretches a new well project out to a few weeks, sometimes longer, is everything around the drilling: the required notice, getting a driller scheduled, casing and grouting, letting the seal cure, developing the well so it runs clear, and testing the water before anyone drinks it. If you're planning a new well or trying to figure out why yours is taking longer than you expected, here's the sequence, in order, with the parts that actually eat calendar days.

## Step 1: The Notice of Intent, and the Water Right Question Behind It

Before a licensed driller can turn a bit into the ground in Washington, the property owner, the owner's agent, or the well operator has to file a Notice of Intent (NOI) with the Department of Ecology. State rule sets that at a minimum of 72 hours before work starts, and the notice has to include the well owner's name, the property location, the proposed use of the water, the planned start and completion dates, and the operator's license number ([WAC 173-160-151](https://app.leg.wa.gov/wac/default.aspx?cite=173-160-151)). If the planned use needs a water right, the permit or certificate has to be attached to the notice. There's a filing fee attached, and it depends on the casing diameter. That 72-hour minimum is not a permit backlog; it's a short, fixed notice window, not a review-and-approval queue. Nothing about the notice itself should be the thing slowing your project down.

What can slow things down before you ever get to the NOI is the water right question. Most homes on a private well fall under the state's permit exemption. Ecology's own guidance is that if you plan to use more than 5,000 gallons a day or irrigate more than half an acre, you need to apply for a water right before you drill, and it also asks you to check whether your watershed is open to new withdrawals ([Washington Department of Ecology, wells overview](https://ecology.wa.gov/water-shorelines/water-supply/wells)). If your planned use is bigger than that, a large lawn or an orchard block, you'll need to secure a water right first. That's a separate process from well construction, and it can take far longer than the drilling itself. We cover the exemption in more detail in [permit-exempt wells in Washington](/blog/permit-exempt-wells-washington/); the short version for a timeline is: know which side of that line you're on before you call a driller, because it changes everything that follows.

## Step 2: Getting a Driller Scheduled

This is often the longest single wait in the whole project, and it has nothing to do with the state. Washington requires anyone who constructs or decommissions a water well to hold a state well operator license, with narrow exceptions ([Washington Department of Ecology, information for drillers](https://ecology.wa.gov/water-shorelines/water-supply/wells/information-for-drillers)), which means your choices are limited to whoever is actually licensed and working in the area. Licensed drillers in Chelan, Douglas, and Grant counties run their own calendars, and a rig that's tied up on another job, or waiting out bad weather or frozen ground, pushes your start date the same way a contractor backlog pushes any other trade. Basalt is common underfoot through much of the Columbia Plateau, and a driller working through hard rock at depth moves slower than one working sandy alluvium, which also affects how far out they're willing to commit to a start date. Ask early, ask for a realistic window rather than a single date, and expect that window to be measured in weeks, not days.

## Step 3: Drilling the Borehole

Once the rig is set up, actual drilling for a residential well is often the shortest step in the whole sequence, frequently a single day or two for a well of ordinary depth in this area. Rotary rigs and cable-tool rigs both advance a hole steadily under normal conditions; hard basalt, cobbles, or lost circulation zones slow the bit down and can stretch drilling to several days even on a routine job. Depth matters too. We've written separately about typical depths across the valley and foothills in [how deep are wells in the Wenatchee area](/blog/how-deep-are-wells-wenatchee/), and a well drilled several hundred feet deeper simply takes more rig time than a shallow one, independent of anything else on this list.

## Step 4: Casing, Grouting, and the Cure Time Nobody Talks About

As the hole goes in, casing goes in behind it, and the annular space between the casing and the borehole wall gets sealed with grout, typically cement or bentonite, to keep surface water and shallow groundwater from running down the outside of the casing into the aquifer. That grout has to set before work continues. When the driller seals with neat cement and still has drilling to do below it, state rule has the cement placed at least 72 hours before drilling resumes, unless additives are mixed in that make it set faster ([WAC 173-160-221](https://app.leg.wa.gov/wac/default.aspx?cite=173-160-221)). It's a real cure time, not a formality, and it's one of the parts of the schedule that a homeowner watching from the driveway can mistake for the crew standing around doing nothing. How long your site sits quiet depends on the sealing material and method your driller uses, so ask.

## Step 5: Development, Disinfection, and the Water Test

Development is the process of clearing fine sediment out of the formation around the screen or open borehole so the well produces clean water at its real yield, usually by surging, pumping, or airlifting until the water runs clear. Once the well is developed and the permanent pump is set and running water through the system, not before, the well and plumbing get shock chlorinated to knock down bacteria introduced during construction. After the chlorine has had time to work and the system has been flushed, a water sample goes to an accredited lab for the standard checks: total coliform bacteria and nitrate, at minimum, before anyone should treat the water as safe to drink. If you want the full rundown on that testing step, we cover it in [well water testing in Wenatchee](/blog/well-water-testing-wenatchee/). This step alone, from setting the pump to getting lab results back, commonly adds several more days to a week. Separately, the driller is required to file a completed well report with Ecology within 30 days of finishing the well ([WAC 173-160-141](https://app.leg.wa.gov/wac/default.aspx?cite=173-160-141)); that paperwork runs on its own timeline and doesn't hold up your water test.

Somewhere in this stage the driller also runs a yield test, pumping at a controlled rate and watching how the water level responds, to document how many gallons per minute the well can sustain. That number matters for sizing everything downstream: the pump, the pressure tank, and whether the well can support the household's actual demand. We go into how that test works in [well yield testing](/blog/well-yield-test/).

## What Actually Adds Days or Weeks to the Schedule

A handful of things reliably turn a two week project into a five or six week one:

1. **Driller scheduling.** A backed-up driller can easily be the longest wait, and it's worth asking about calendar availability before you commit to anything else.
2. **Depth and geology.** Deeper wells and hard basalt formations both add rig time.
3. **A water right requirement.** If your planned use exceeds the exempt-well threshold, the water right process runs on its own, much longer timeline, separate from construction.
4. **Grout cure time.** Non-negotiable, and if a neat cement seal goes in with more drilling still to do, it can mean a 72-hour pause.
5. **Lab turnaround.** Coliform and nitrate results from an accredited lab typically take a few business days once the sample is submitted.
6. **Access and weather.** A site that needs road or clearing work before a rig can reach it, or a stretch of frozen ground, adds time before drilling can even begin.

## A Realistic Timeline, Start to Running Water

Every property is different, but a straightforward residential well in Chelan, Douglas, or Grant County usually moves through these stages in roughly this order:

| Stage | What happens | Typical time added |
|---|---|---|
| Scheduling a driller | Getting on a licensed driller's calendar | Days to several weeks, driller-dependent |
| Notice of Intent | Filed with Ecology before work starts | At least 72 hours by rule |
| Drilling | Boring the hole to depth | Often 1 to 3 days for a routine well |
| Casing and grouting | Setting casing, sealing the annulus, curing | Depends on the seal; up to 72 hours with neat cement if drilling continues |
| Development | Clearing sediment, yield testing | A day or more |
| Pump, tank, and wiring | Sizing and installing the working system | Scheduled once the yield is known |
| Disinfection and lab test | Shock chlorination through the pump and plumbing, flush, sample, lab turnaround | Several days to a week |

Add it up and most straightforward projects land somewhere between two and six weeks from the day you call a driller to the day water is running reliably at the tap, with driller availability and any water right question doing the most to push that window longer. A hard-rock, deep-foothill well, or one that needs a water right first, can run well past that.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does it actually take to drill the hole itself?

For a typical residential well in this area, once the rig is on site, drilling usually takes one to a few days depending on depth and the ground it's going through. Basalt and other hard rock slow the bit down compared to sand or gravel formations. That's genuinely the fastest part of the whole project. The delays that stretch a well job into weeks almost always happen before drilling starts, waiting for a driller's schedule to open up, or after it, during casing cure, development, and lab testing of the water sample.

### Do I have to wait a long time for the Notice of Intent to clear before drilling can start?

No. Washington's rule sets the Notice of Intent at a minimum of 72 hours before work begins, and it's a notice requirement, not an approval you sit in line for ([WAC 173-160-151](https://app.leg.wa.gov/wac/default.aspx?cite=173-160-151)). The driller typically handles filing it as part of scheduling your job. The thing that actually takes real time is getting on a licensed driller's calendar in the first place, and, if your planned use is large enough to need a separate water right, that process runs on its own much longer timeline.

### Why would my well take a lot longer than a neighbor's?

Depth, geology, and driller availability all vary property to property. A well drilled deep into basalt in the foothills takes longer to bore than a shallower well in looser ground down in the valley. A driller juggling several jobs at once may not be able to start yours for weeks even after everything else is ready. Site access, like needing a road cut or clearing before a rig can reach the pad, adds time too. None of that reflects a problem with your specific well; it's just what that particular ground and schedule required.

### When can I actually turn on the tap and use the water?

Not until the well has been developed, the pump is set and running water through the system, and a water sample has come back from an accredited lab clear of total coliform bacteria, at minimum, along with a nitrate result you're comfortable with. Shock chlorination happens with the pump running, not beforehand, and the system needs to be flushed afterward before sampling. Treat the water as unsafe to drink until you have those lab results in hand, even if it looks clear and runs fine at the tap.
