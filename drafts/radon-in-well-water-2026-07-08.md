# Radon in Well Water: Should Wenatchee Homeowners Test?

Every time you run the shower, wash dishes, or start the dishwasher, a well drawing from the wrong rock can release a radioactive gas into your air. You won't smell it. You won't see it. ## What Radon in Well Water Actually Is

Radon is a naturally occurring radioactive gas that forms when uranium in rock and soil breaks down over time. It's invisible, odorless, and tasteless, which puts it in the same "you'd never know" category as arsenic and nitrates. The difference is where it comes from and how it reaches you.

Uranium shows up in trace amounts in nearly all rock, but granite holds more of it than basalt. That matters here because North Central Washington sits at the boundary between two very different geologies. The Cascade foothills around Leavenworth, Plain, and parts of the upper valley are built on granite and granodiorite, while the Columbia Plateau stretching toward Quincy and Moses Lake is mostly basalt, which tends to produce less radon.

Wells drilled into fractured granite, or into gravel and sand aquifers fed by groundwater that has moved through granite, are the ones most likely to pick up dissolved radon gas. That's a general pattern, not a rule that applies to every well in Leavenworth and skips every well in Quincy. The only way to know where your specific well falls is to test it.

## Two Ways Radon Reaches You: Breathing It vs. Drinking It

This is the part that surprises most people. Radon in well water isn't mainly a drinking problem. It's mainly a breathing problem.

When radon-laced water comes out of a faucet, showerhead, or washing machine, the gas releases into the air of your home. Hot water and agitation, like a hot shower, release it fastest. The [EPA's radon program](https://www.epa.gov/radon) notes that inhaling radon released from water is a far bigger lifetime cancer risk than swallowing it in a glass of water, since radon is linked to lung cancer, not stomach or bladder issues the way arsenic is.

Radon is the second leading cause of lung cancer in the United States after smoking, and most of that risk nationally comes from radon seeping into homes through soil and foundations, not from well water. Water is usually a secondary contributor. In a home with a strong well-water source and a lot of showering and laundry, it can still meaningfully raise indoor air levels.

That's also why radon in well water and radon in your home's air are two separate tests. A soil-gas radon problem coming through your foundation has nothing to do with your well, and a high water reading doesn't always mean your indoor air is elevated too. Careful homeowners test both.

## Is There a Safe Radon in Well Water Level?

Here's where radon gets messier than arsenic or nitrates. The EPA has never finalized a nationwide legal limit for [radon in drinking water](https://www.epa.gov/radon/radon-drinking-water), though it has proposed standards over the years, generally in the range of 300 picocuries per liter (pCi/L), with a higher allowance of 4,000 pCi/L for water systems that also run an active program to address radon in indoor air.

Washington doesn't regulate radon in private well water either, though the [Washington State Department of Health](https://doh.wa.gov/community-and-environment/drinking-water/contaminants/radon) publishes general guidance for well owners who want to test. That leaves you working from guidance rather than a hard legal cutoff:

- **Under 300 pCi/L:** Generally considered lower risk from the water itself, though it's still worth checking your home's indoor air separately.
- **300 to 4,000 pCi/L:** A gray zone. Many water treatment professionals recommend addressing it, especially if your indoor air radon is also elevated.
- **Above 4,000 pCi/L:** Most labs and health agencies recommend treatment regardless of your indoor air results.

For comparison, indoor air radon is measured in picocuries per liter of air, and the EPA's action level there is 4 pCi/L, a completely different scale than the water numbers above. Don't mix up the two units when you're reading a lab report.

## How to Test for Radon in Well Water

Testing radon in well water takes a specific kind of lab test, not a strip you dip in a glass.

1. **Order a certified water radon test.** A Washington-accredited lab can supply a sample kit for $50 to $150 as a standalone test, or bundled into a broader private well panel for $150 to $400.
2. **Sample from an untreated cold tap**, the same rule as every other well test. You want to know what's coming out of the ground, not what a softener or filter is already catching.
3. **Handle the sample fast.** Radon has a short half-life, about 3.8 days, so labs want the sample shipped and tested quickly. Follow the kit's timing instructions exactly or your result can read artificially low.
4. **Test your indoor air separately.** A short-term charcoal canister test for indoor air runs $15 to $30 and takes 2 to 7 days. It's a different test from the water sample and measures a different exposure.

## Treatment Options for Radon in Well Water

If your well tests high, you have two proven ways to treat it, and one popular option that's a poor fit for most homes.

**Aeration systems** are the gold standard for radon in well water. These whole-house units spray incoming water into a tank and vent the released gas safely outside through a fan and pipe, essentially doing on purpose, and before the water reaches your tap, what happens naturally when you shower. Aeration removes 95 to 99.9 percent of radon and doesn't build up radioactivity in a filter media, which is why most water treatment professionals recommend it first.

**Granular activated carbon (GAC) tanks** can also remove radon and sometimes cost less to install. The catch is that radon decays into other radioactive particles that accumulate on the carbon media over time. On a high-radon well, that can turn the tank itself into a low-level radioactive source, usually requiring extra shielding, installation away from where people spend time, and periodic monitoring. Most pros steer higher-radon households toward aeration instead.

**What doesn't work:** standard sediment filters, water softeners, and UV disinfection. None of them touch dissolved gas, so don't expect equipment bought for iron, hardness, or bacteria to do anything for radon.

Aeration systems involve electrical fan wiring, venting that has to be routed correctly so radon doesn't get pulled back into the house, and plumbing tied into your main water line. That combination of electrical work and pressurized plumbing is not a weekend DIY project. If a job touches your wellhead, casing, downhole equipment, or main electrical panel, that's a call for a licensed professional every time.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is radon in well water actually dangerous?

Yes, particularly through inhalation rather than drinking. When radon-laced water is used for showers, laundry, and dishwashing, the gas releases into your indoor air, and breathing it over years raises lung cancer risk. The risk from swallowing radon in drinking water is much smaller than the risk from breathing it once it's released into your home's air.

### How is radon in well water different from radon in my home's air?

They're related but separate problems with separate causes and separate tests. Air radon usually comes from soil gas seeping through your foundation, while water radon comes from dissolved gas in groundwater that releases when you run the tap. A home can have one problem without the other, so testing both gives you the full picture.

### Which parts of the Wenatchee area are most likely to have radon in well water?

Wells drilled into granite or granodiorite bedrock, common in the Cascade foothills around Leavenworth, Plain, and parts of the upper valley, tend to run higher than wells on the basalt-dominated Columbia Plateau toward Quincy and Moses Lake. That's a general pattern, not a guarantee, since radon can vary well to well even on similar ground. Testing your specific well is the only way to know for sure.

### Can I remove radon with a standard whole-house filter or softener?

No. Standard sediment filters and water softeners are built to catch particles and swap hardness minerals, and neither one touches dissolved gas. Removing radon from well water requires an aeration system, or in some cases a granular activated carbon tank, both of which are purpose-built for the job and should be installed by a licensed water treatment professional.
