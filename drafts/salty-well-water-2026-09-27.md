# Well Water Tastes Salty? What TDS, Chloride, and Sulfate Are Telling You

Say you fill a glass at the kitchen tap and it tastes faintly salty, almost like it needs a rinse of plain water to clear. On a private well, that taste almost never means the ocean found your aquifer. It means one or more dissolved minerals, most often total dissolved solids, chloride, or sulfate, have crept up past the point where your tongue notices them.

None of those three are contamination in the sense of making you sick right away. They are what water chemists call aesthetic problems: they change taste, not necessarily safety, at the levels most homes see. But "usually fine" is not the same as "ignore it," and the fix depends entirely on which mineral is doing the work and where it is coming from. Guessing wastes money. Testing tells you.

## What "Salty" Water Usually Means

Homeowners describe a few different sensations as "salty," and they point to different culprits.

A flat, mineral, slightly bitter taste usually tracks with total dissolved solids (TDS), the catch-all measure of everything dissolved in the water: calcium, magnesium, sodium, chloride, sulfate, and more. A taste closer to actual table salt points more specifically at sodium and chloride together, since sodium chloride is table salt. A taste some people describe as bitter or medicinal, especially if it comes with looser stools after a big glass, points at sulfate.

These three overlap so often that separating them by taste alone is a guess. A lab test is not.

## TDS: The Number Behind the Taste

TDS is not one chemical. It is the sum of whatever minerals are dissolved in your water, reported as milligrams per liter (mg/L), also written as parts per million. The Environmental Protection Agency sets a secondary standard for TDS at 500 mg/L, based purely on taste and appearance rather than health risk, according to [EPA's guidance on secondary drinking water standards](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/secondary-drinking-water-standards-guidance-nuisance-chemicals). Secondary standards are not enforceable rules. They are the concentration where most people start to notice the water tastes different, looks cloudy, or leaves spots on glassware.

A well can run well above 500 mg/L and still be perfectly fine to drink. It just will not taste great, and depending on what makes up that TDS, it may scale up your water heater or fixtures faster than soft, low-mineral water would. [Penn State Extension recommends testing private well water routinely for TDS](https://extension.psu.edu/water/drinking-and-residential-water/testing-and-interpretation), alongside bacteria and pH, precisely because it is such a useful early signal.

TDS by itself will not tell you whether the culprit is chloride, sulfate, sodium, calcium, or something else. It tells you there is more dissolved mineral in the water than usual. The next test tells you what kind.

## Chloride and Sulfate: The Two Minerals Most Likely to Blame

Chloride and sulfate are the two ions most often behind a salty or bitter taste, and EPA sets a secondary standard of 250 mg/L for each, again based on taste rather than health effects at typical levels.

Sodium is the other half of the equation, and it does not have a federal secondary standard, but EPA's own drinking water advisory on sodium recommends that concentrations not exceed 30 to 60 mg/L to avoid adverse effects on taste, and not exceed 20 mg/L for people on a very low sodium diet (500 mg of sodium a day), per [EPA's Drinking Water Advisory on sodium](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2014-09/documents/support_cc1_sodium_dwreport.pdf). If someone in the house watches sodium for blood pressure or kidney reasons, a sodium test result matters more than the taste test does, since a well can carry meaningful sodium long before it tastes noticeably salty to most people.

Sulfate has its own wrinkle. People start to taste it somewhere between 250 and 350 mg/L, where it just tastes off. At higher levels, above 500 mg/L and in many cases above 1,000 mg/L, it can have a temporary laxative effect, and EPA's technical review found people tend to adjust within about a week of first exposure. The groups EPA flags as sensitive are formula-fed infants, elderly people using powdered nutritional supplements mixed with tap water, and visitors who are not used to high-sulfate water, according to [EPA's regulatory review of sulfate in drinking water](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2014-09/documents/support_cc1_sulfate_dwreport.pdf). In that review EPA decided against an enforceable federal limit: the effect is short-lived and recovery is rapid, and public water supplies rarely test above 500 mg/L.

None of that means you should ignore a persistently salty or bitter well. It means the next step is a lab report, not a guess.

## Where the Salt Is Actually Coming From

Groundwater picks up dissolved minerals from geology, climate, and human activity, often some mix of the three, according to the [U.S. Geological Survey's overview of chloride, salinity, and dissolved solids in groundwater](https://www.usgs.gov/mission-areas/water-resources/science/chloride-salinity-and-dissolved-solids). USGS lists these among the human sources, and any of them can make a well that was fine for years start tasting different:

1. **Road salt.** USGS notes that road salt application in the United States has tripled since the 1970s, and salt-laden runoff can work its way into shallow groundwater near heavily salted roads over time.
2. **Irrigation and evaporation.** When irrigation water evaporates or is taken up by plants, it leaves its dissolved salts behind in the soil, and excess irrigation water can flush salts that have built up in the soil down to the water table. Dry climates with high evaporation also leave less water to dilute what the rock contributes.
3. **Fertilizer.** Some fertilizers contain sulfate or chloride compounds, and heavy, long-term use on nearby ground can add to what ends up in shallow groundwater.
4. **Septic systems and household products.** Detergents, water softener discharge, and septic effluent all add dissolved solids to the ground near a drainfield, which matters most for wells that sit close to one.
5. **A softener that is doing exactly what it is designed to do.** More on that below, because it is an easy cause to overlook.

Geology still plays a part: USGS notes that some rocks, such as shales, carbonates, and evaporites, dissolve more readily than others, so a well's natural mineral load depends on what it is drilled into. A lab test, not an assumption about the rock, tells you which source you are dealing with.

## Your Water Softener Could Be Making It Worse

If your water has tasted more mineral or slightly salty since a softener went in, or since it started working harder, the softener itself is worth a look before anything else.

A standard softener works by ion exchange: it swaps calcium, magnesium, and often iron and manganese for sodium. That is a good trade for hardness and scale, but it means every gallon of softened water carries more sodium than it did before treatment. University of Maryland Extension cites a study finding that softened well water averaged 278 mg/L of sodium, well above the 30 to 60 mg/L taste threshold and far above the 20 mg/L guideline for very low sodium diets, per [University of Maryland Extension's fact sheet on sodium in well water](https://extension.umd.edu/sites/extension.umd.edu/files/publications/Sodium%20in%20well%20water%20FS%201084%20%281%29.pdf). A softener does not reduce chloride, sulfate, or overall TDS in any meaningful way; it just trades one set of dissolved minerals for sodium.

If a softener is the likely source, a few options exist, though sizing and installing any of them is a water treatment company's job, not ours. A softener that uses potassium chloride instead of sodium chloride avoids adding sodium, at a higher cost per bag. Softening only the hot water line and bypassing cold water reduces sodium intake from drinking and cooking water, though that is not a good option if iron or manganese removal is also needed. Point-of-use reverse osmosis at the kitchen tap treats drinking water specifically, without changing how the whole-house softener runs. ## What to Test For

A home TDS meter (the kind sold for aquariums and reverse osmosis systems) gives you a fast, rough number, but it cannot tell chloride from sulfate from sodium from ordinary hardness minerals. For a salty or bitter taste that will not go away, a state-certified lab test is worth the cost. Ask for a package that includes:

| Parameter | What it tells you | EPA guidance |
|---|---|---|
| TDS | Overall dissolved mineral load | 500 mg/L (secondary standard) |
| Chloride | Salt-driven taste, road salt and septic markers | 250 mg/L (secondary standard) |
| Sulfate | Bitter taste, laxative effect at high levels | 250 mg/L (secondary standard) |
| Sodium | Diet-relevant salt content, softener signature | No secondary standard; advisory 30 - 60 mg/L (taste), 20 mg/L (very low sodium diet) |

[Penn State Extension's guidance on testing and interpreting residential water results](https://extension.psu.edu/water/drinking-and-residential-water/testing-and-interpretation) is a useful plain-English companion once results come back, and our own [guide to well water testing in Wenatchee](/blog/well-water-testing-wenatchee/) covers where to send a sample locally. ## What Actually Fixes Salty Well Water

Once you know which mineral is elevated, the fix follows from the source.

If a softener is adding the sodium, switching to potassium chloride, softening only hot water, or adding point-of-use treatment for drinking water are all reasonable paths, and a water treatment company can size any of them correctly for your household's water use. ## When It's a Well Problem, Not a Water-Quality Problem

There is one category worth separating out: a salty taste that shows up suddenly, especially alongside cloudiness, grit, or a change after heavy rain or snowmelt, can mean surface water or road runoff is getting into the well itself rather than the aquifer slowly changing. A cracked or poorly sealed well cap, a casing that has settled or corroded, or a wellhead sitting in a low spot that collects runoff can all let water in that never should have reached the casing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is salty well water safe to drink?

In most cases, yes. TDS and chloride carry only secondary, taste-based standards, and sodium has no federal drinking water standard at all, only an EPA advisory set mainly for taste. Sulfate can cause a temporary laxative effect at higher concentrations, above 500 mg/L, but the effect is short-lived and people tend to adjust within about a week. The exceptions worth taking seriously are households on sodium-restricted diets, where sodium in water adds up, and homes with formula-fed infants, a group EPA flags as sensitive to high sulfate. A lab test tells you which situation you are actually in.

### Will a water softener fix salty-tasting well water?

No, and in some cases it makes the taste worse. A standard softener trades calcium and magnesium for sodium through ion exchange; it does not reduce chloride, sulfate, or overall dissolved solids. Softened well water has been measured averaging well above 250 mg/L of sodium in at least one published study, far past the taste threshold. If a softener runs on your whole house, it is worth testing sodium specifically rather than assuming the softener is unrelated to a salty taste that started around the same time.

### Can road salt make well water taste salty?

Yes, particularly for shallow wells near state highways, county roads, or routes that get heavy winter salt use. USGS reports that road salt application in the United States has tripled since the 1970s, and salt-laden runoff can work into shallow groundwater over years of repeated application. A chloride test is the most direct way to check, since chloride is the component of road salt most associated with taste and with tracking a road-salt source rather than a natural or softener-driven one.

### What should I ask a lab to test for salty well water?

Ask for TDS, chloride, sulfate, and sodium at minimum, since a salty or bitter taste can come from any one of them and they point toward different causes and different fixes. If your well sits near a septic drainfield, heavily fertilized ground, or a salted road, mention that to the lab, since it may suggest testing for related parameters like nitrate at the same time. A state-certified lab, rather than a home test strip, is the right choice once a taste change has stuck around rather than come and gone with the weather.
