# FHA Well Requirements: What VA and USDA Ask For Too

If you're buying or refinancing a home on a private well in Chelan, Douglas or Grant County, the loan program cares about two things: how far your well sits from anything that could pollute it, and whether the water coming out of it is safe to drink. FHA, VA and USDA loans all ask those same two questions, but they don't ask them the same way, and the paperwork that satisfies one program won't automatically satisfy another. Here's what each one actually requires, straight from the agency handbooks, and where they overlap and where they don't.

## What FHA Requires From a Well (HUD Handbook 4000.1)

FHA-insured loans follow [HUD's Single Family Housing Policy Handbook 4000.1](https://web.archive.org/web/20250426015013/https://www.hud.gov/sites/dfiles/OCHCO/documents/4000.1hsghhdbk103123.pdf). When a property has an individual water supply, the handbook requires the water quality to meet the standards of whatever health authority has jurisdiction, and if there's no local or state standard, the EPA's National Primary Drinking Water Regulations apply instead.

A water test isn't automatic on every FHA file. It's specifically required when the property is newly constructed, when the appraiser reports a deficiency with the well or the water, when the water is reported or known to be unsafe, or when the property sits close to a dump, landfill, industrial site, or farm operation where pesticide use could be a concern. Where a test is required, HUD says the sample has to be collected and transported by a disinterested third party, meaning the local health authority, a commercial testing lab, or a licensed sanitary engineer. The borrower or any other interested party is never allowed to collect or carry the sample themselves.

When a water test is required, the report can't be more than 180 days old at the disbursement date, which for most buyers means closing. FHA also sets a minimum flow rate: an existing well has to deliver a continuous flow of at least three gallons per minute, and a well on new construction has to deliver five gallons per minute over at least a four-hour period.

## Where VA Requirements Differ

VA loans run on a separate rulebook, [VA Pamphlet 26-7's chapter on minimum property requirements](https://www.knowva.ebenefits.va.gov/system/templates/selfservice/va_ssnew/help/customer/locale/en-US/portal/554400000001018/content/554400000314692/VA-Pamphlet-VAP26-7-Chapter-12-Minimum-Property-Requirement-Overview), and while the underlying idea is the same, at least one number isn't. VA also defers to the health authority with jurisdiction first. If the local authority has no specific requirements, EPA guidelines apply, and the chapter points readers to [EPA's list of private well programs by state](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/private-drinking-water-well-programs-your-state). Sampling has to be done by that same kind of disinterested third party, never the veteran or another interested party.

The difference that actually matters if you're timing a closing: a VA water test is only valid for 90 days from the date the local health authority certified it, unless the local authority says otherwise. That's half of FHA's window. On distances, VA's chapter doesn't print a table of its own: the appraiser has to know the minimum distances between wells and sources of pollution, but isn't required to sketch or note them (FHA's appraisal rules say the same). VA also asks the veteran to sign a written acknowledgment when the water comes from a source like a dug well, a cistern, a spring, a river, a sand-point or artesian well, hauled water, rainwater catchment, or a mechanical chlorinator. If the home has a water filtration system, the veteran also acknowledges in writing that it has to be maintained and its filters changed on the manufacturer's schedule for the water to stay safe. Connection to public water is only mandatory if the local authority requires it, not automatically because public water happens to run nearby.

## What USDA Wants for a Rural Development Loan

USDA's Single Family Housing Guaranteed Loan Program, covered in [Handbook HB-1-3555's chapter on property and appraisal requirements](https://web.archive.org/web/20250929212034/https://www.rd.usda.gov/media/file/download/3555-chapter12.pdf), takes a more direct route: it points straight back to HUD's numbers. A private well and septic system that already meets HUD Handbook 4000.1, or that the local or state health authority accepts, doesn't need extra inspections beyond the water quality test itself.

USDA's water analysis window matches FHA's: no more than 180 days old at loan closing, with the caveat that a new report can be required if the agency knows of a recent environmental impact, such as a chemical spill or a natural disaster, that could make the original analysis invalid. USDA also names who runs the analysis: the local health authority or a state certified laboratory. The well-to-septic separation has to be measured and has to meet HUD's distances, or be found acceptable by the local or state health authority.

## The Numbers Behind the Rules: HUD's Distance Table

FHA uses this table directly, USDA points back to it, and VA publishes no distance table of its own, so these are the federal figures to measure against. For existing construction:

| Feature | Minimum distance |
|---|---|
| Well to property line | 10 feet |
| Well to septic tank | 50 feet |
| Well to drain field | 100 feet (may drop to 75 feet if the local health authority allows it) |

If the property line borders another residential property, the local jurisdiction's own well-distance rule takes over instead of the table above. If it borders nonresidential land or a road, the 10-foot property line minimum still applies. In every case, whatever the local health authority requires prevails if it's stricter than HUD's numbers.

Here's how the paperwork compares once you line the three programs up side by side:

| | FHA | VA | USDA |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water test valid for | 180 days | 90 days, unless the local authority says otherwise | 180 days |
| Distance standard | HUD's own table (a greater local distance prevails) | No table of its own; appraiser must know the minimums | Measured, and meets HUD's table or is acceptable to the local/state authority |
| Shared well cap | 4 living units, with enough flow or storage for each home | No fixed unit cap, but a recorded easement and agreement required | 4 living units, unless the local code authority approves more |

## How This Plays Out on a Well in Chelan, Douglas or Grant County

The "health authority with jurisdiction" here is your local health district: Chelan-Douglas Health District for Chelan and Douglas counties, Grant County Health District for Grant. On distances, Washington's on-site sewage rule, [WAC 246-272A-0210](https://app.leg.wa.gov/WAC/default.aspx?cite=246-272A-0210), sets the same 50 feet from a septic tank and 100 feet from a drainfield for a private well, so on most properties the state and HUD figures line up; our post on [septic and well separation in Washington](/blog/septic-well-separation-distance-washington/) covers the local side. Orchard country adds its own wrinkle: nitrate from fertilizer use is a real testing concern here, which is one reason the [Washington State Department of Health recommends every private well owner test annually](https://doh.wa.gov/community-and-environment/drinking-water/contaminants/testing-your-water) for coliform bacteria and nitrate, not just when a loan requires it. We've written separately about [nitrates in well water](/blog/nitrates-in-well-water/) if you want the deeper explanation of where that risk comes from and what the numbers mean.

Deeper wells in the foothills around towns like [Leavenworth](/well-service-leavenworth/) don't get a pass on the distance rule just because the well is drilled far down. The 50-foot and 100-foot figures are horizontal separations, so a 400-foot well set 40 feet from a septic tank still fails the same way a 60-foot well would.

## Who Can Actually Test the Water, and Who Can't

FHA and VA draw the same line in so many words: the person collecting and carrying the water sample has to be a disinterested third party, and the borrower or any other interested party may never collect or transport it. That rules out the buyer, the seller, and anyone else with a stake in the sale. USDA gets to the same place by requiring the analysis to be done by the local health authority or a state certified lab. ## Shared Wells: Three Different Numbers

If your property doesn't have its own well and instead splits one with a neighbor, each program handles it differently. FHA caps a shared well at four living units, requires a valve on each home's service line, and requires the well to assure each connected home at least three gallons per minute over a continuous four-hour period for existing construction, or five gallons per minute for new construction. It allows pressurized storage to make up the difference if the well itself yields less. VA doesn't set a household cap but requires a permanent recorded easement and a binding well-sharing agreement covering maintenance and repair costs. USDA caps a shared well at four living units unless the local code authority specifically approves and enforces something larger, and requires a shutoff valve on each dwelling. Whichever program applies, the agreement has to be recorded, not a handshake between neighbors.

## Getting Your Well Ready Before the Appraisal

A few things are worth doing before an appraiser or inspector shows up, whichever loan you're using:

1. Confirm which program is financing the purchase, FHA, VA or USDA, and use that program's test-validity window when scheduling the sample.
2. Arrange third-party sample collection through your local health district or a certified lab rather than relying on the seller's paperwork.
3. Pull any well log or as-built record you have. It saves the appraiser time confirming depth and construction, and it's a useful record to keep regardless.
4. If a flow test comes back short, find out whether the well itself is under-producing or whether a failing pump or a waterlogged tank is the real cause before assuming you need a new well.
5. Know your test's expiration date against your expected closing date. A test that's fine in February can expire before a closing that slips into September.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does my well need to be licensed or certified to qualify for an FHA, VA or USDA loan?

No. None of the three programs require a well "license" or certification. What they look at is water quality, meaning water that meets the local health authority's standard (or EPA's, if there's no local standard), shown by an independent test when the program or the appraiser calls for one, and safe separation from the septic system. FHA and USDA hold the well to HUD's distance minimums or the local authority's rule, and USDA requires the distance to be measured. There's no pass/fail licensing step separate from those things.

### How old can a well water test be at closing?

It depends on the program. FHA and USDA both allow a water analysis report up to 180 days old at closing. VA is stricter: a VA test is only valid for 90 days from the date the local health authority certified it, unless that authority says otherwise. If your closing date slips, check the test date against the program's window before assuming the paperwork still holds.

### Can I collect my own water sample to save the cost of a lab visit?

No. FHA and VA require the sample to be collected and transported by a disinterested third party: the local health authority, a commercial testing lab, a licensed sanitary engineer, or another party the local health authority accepts. The borrower or any other interested party is never allowed to collect or carry the sample, however careful they are. USDA requires the analysis to be done by the local health authority or a state certified lab, so plan on the same independent collection there too.

### What happens if my well is closer to the septic tank than the required distance?

It depends on the program and your local health authority. HUD's minimums are 50 feet to the septic tank and 100 feet to the drain field, and the drain field distance can drop to 75 feet only if the local authority allows it. A greater local distance always prevails. USDA will also accept a separation that the local or state health authority finds acceptable. Washington's own rules use the same 50 and 100 foot figures for a private well, so talk to your local health district before assuming the loan is dead.
