# Water Witching for a New Well: What Actually Works

Water witching, also called dowsing, is the practice of walking a piece of land with a forked stick, a pair of bent rods, or a pendulum and letting the tool tell you where to drill. It goes back centuries, and it is still practiced in rural areas today, sometimes by a neighbor who says they have a knack for it. Before you pick a well site based on where the stick dipped, it helps to know what the evidence actually shows, and what a driller or a hydrogeologist, a geologist who specializes in groundwater, looks at instead.

## What Water Witching Actually Is

Dowsing takes a few forms. The classic method is a forked willow or hazel branch held loosely by both hands, walked slowly across a property until the branch dips or twists. Some dowsers use two L-shaped metal rods, one in each hand, and watch for the rods to cross. Others use a pendulum, or claim to count swings to estimate depth. In every version, the tool itself does nothing on its own. It is sensitive to small, often unconscious movements of the person holding it. There is no sensor, no reading, and no way to separate the tool's response from the dowser's own hands.

## What the Evidence Actually Shows

The [U.S. Geological Survey addresses water dowsing directly](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/water-dowsing), and its position is blunt: when dowsing is put under scientific examination, it looks very different from the success stories, and pinning down groundwater's depth, quantity and quality takes hydrologic, geologic and geophysical knowledge, not a divining tool. The reason those stories exist has less to do with the stick and more to do with geology. As the USGS puts it, in many areas underground water is so common close to the surface that it would be hard to drill a well and not find water. A dowser who says drill here is very often right that water exists somewhere below that spot. That is a different claim from having found the best spot, the right depth, or a well that will actually produce enough water for a household.

## Why It Can Seem to Work Almost Anywhere

Say a dowser walks your property, the rods cross near the old orchard rows, and the well comes in at 60 feet with decent flow. That feels like proof. But nobody drilled the comparison hole ten feet away, or on the other side of the driveway, to see what would have happened there. In areas with a shallow, continuous water table, most spots on a property would have produced a similar result. The dowsing "hit rate" people remember is really the local geology's hit rate, not the method's. It is a fair result for the wrong reason, and it tells you nothing about whether a different spot on the same lot would have given you more water, less iron, or a shorter, cheaper well.

## What Actually Predicts a Good Well Site Here

North central Washington is not one uniform layer of gravel with water sitting a few feet down. The ground under a property in this area generally falls into one of two broad categories, and the difference matters more than any stick.

Across much of the Columbia Plateau bench land, including the flatter ground around [Quincy](/well-service-quincy/) and [Moses Lake](/well-service-moses-lake/), the rock beneath the soil is Columbia River basalt, laid down in a series of ancient lava flows. Solid basalt itself does not hold or move much water. What matters is the fracture zones and thin sediment layers wedged between individual flows. Washington State University researchers studying the region's basalt aquifer, the underground formation that holds and yields water, describe it as a kind of layer cake: mostly fractured basalt, with "frosting" between the layers where [water moves more easily through the system](https://news.wsu.edu/press-release/2025/07/09/wsu-study-offers-detailed-look-at-declining-groundwater-in-regional-aquifer-system/). A well that lands in one of those zones can be a strong producer. A well drilled a short distance away, into solid rock between flows, can be a disappointment, and no stick, rod, or pendulum can tell the two apart from the surface.

On the valley floors along the Wenatchee and Columbia rivers, including around [Leavenworth](/well-service-leavenworth/) and [Cashmere](/well-service-cashmere/), wells are more likely to draw from alluvium instead, loose gravel, sand, and silt left behind by rivers and old glacial meltwater. Water moves through the spaces between those particles rather than through fractures, which generally makes shallower alluvial wells more forgiving to site than a basalt well, though depth and yield still vary block to block. Climb out of the valley bottom into the foothills and wells are more likely to be drilled into bedrock, where fractures matter again. [Well depths across the valley](/blog/how-deep-are-wells-wenatchee/) reflect that mix of geology more than they reflect luck.

None of this means a dowser will always be wrong. It means the method has no way to sense fracture zones, sediment contacts, or rock type, which are the things that actually govern where water sits and how much of it a well can produce.

## Read the Wells Already Around You First

Before you spend money guessing, there is a free source of real data sitting in a state database: the well reports for every well already drilled near you. Washington requires the person who constructs a well to [furnish a well report within thirty days](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=18.104.050) of finishing the job. That report typically records the location, the driller, the depth drilled, the depth water was struck, the static water level (how high the water sits in the well when it is not being pumped), the yield the driller measured, and a description of the rock and soil layers encountered on the way down.

You can search those reports yourself with [Washington's well report viewer](https://appswr.ecology.wa.gov/WellConstruction/WellReportSearchTools/), which covers the whole state. Pull up a handful of reports near your property and look for a pattern: are nearby wells shallow and pulling from gravel, or do most of them punch through a similar depth of basalt before hitting a productive zone? One report by itself is a single data point, and geology can shift over a short distance, especially near old stream channels or fault zones. But five or six real logs from your immediate area beat a stick's opinion, because a well log is a record of what actually came out of the ground, not a guess about what might be underneath it.

## What a Hydrogeologist or Driller Does Instead

The [USGS also lays out how hydrologists actually locate groundwater](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/how-do-hydrologists-locate-groundwater), and none of it involves a rod. The process generally starts with geologic mapping: identifying which rock and sediment types are at the surface and how they're likely arranged underground, based on outcrops, old quarries, road cuts, and prior drilling. Landscape clues matter too. Shallow groundwater is more likely under a valley than under a ridge, and in dry country, stands of water-loving plants like cottonwoods or willows can point to shallow water even without a well nearby.

From there, the well records described above get folded in, because they are the cheapest and most direct evidence available. Only when existing information isn't enough does anyone drill a test hole, which gets followed by a pump test, actually pumping the hole to measure real gallons per minute rather than estimating it. A local licensed driller who has worked your specific area for years is often doing an informal version of this: they remember what came up two lots over. A hydrogeologist gets brought in for larger properties, low-yield areas, or when a first well disappoints and the owner needs a documented, defensible answer before spending more money on a second attempt. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does water witching actually work?

There's no scientific support for it. The U.S. Geological Survey says dowsing looks very different under scientific examination than it does in anecdotes, and that locating groundwater's depth, quantity and quality takes hydrologic and geologic knowledge. Individual success stories are usually explained by geology, not the method: in many places groundwater sits close to the surface almost everywhere, so most spots on a property would produce water regardless of how the site was chosen.

### Why do so many people believe in dowsing if it doesn't work?

Because it often looks like it works. If groundwater is widespread and shallow in an area, a dowser can point almost anywhere and a well drilled there will likely find water, which gets remembered as a hit. Nobody usually drills a second, unchosen spot nearby to compare. That missing comparison is what makes anecdotes so persuasive. It also does not tell you anything about depth, yield, or water quality, which is what actually determines whether a well is a good one.

### What should I actually do before drilling a new well?

Start with the free data that already exists: search Washington's well report viewer for logs near your property and look at depth, yield, and rock type in the wells already drilled around you. Talk with a licensed driller who has worked in your specific area and ask what they've actually hit nearby. For larger properties, low-yield ground, or a disappointing first well, a hydrogeologist can bring in formal geologic mapping and testing rather than relying on local memory alone.

### Can a dowser predict how deep a well will need to be or how much water it will produce?

There's no reliable evidence that dowsing predicts depth or yield. Both are governed by the geology underground, fracture zones and rock layers in basalt country, or gravel and sediment thickness in alluvial ground, and neither shows up as a signal a rod or stick can detect from the surface. The only ways to actually confirm depth and yield are reviewing real well records from nearby, formal geologic assessment, or drilling and running a pump test on your own property.
