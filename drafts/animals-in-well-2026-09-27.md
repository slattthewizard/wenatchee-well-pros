# Snake in Your Well? What to Do When Animals Get In

You lift the well cap to check the pressure switch, or you're peering down through a gap in the casing with a flashlight, and something moves. Or worse, something doesn't move. A snake curled on top of the pitless fitting, a mouse or a squirrel that didn't make it back out, a haze of gnats around the vent screen. However it shows up, the question is the same: is the water still safe, and how do you fix it.

The short version is that a sealed well should never let animals in at all. If one got in, there's a real entry point somewhere, and until that's closed, this will happen again. Here's how to handle what's in front of you right now, and how to stop it from becoming a repeat problem.

## How Animals and Bugs Actually Get Into a Well

A well casing is supposed to be a closed system from the ground down to the pump. Water goes out through the drop pipe or pitless line, air moves in and out through a screened vent to equalize pressure, and nothing else gets in or out. When something does get in, it's usually through one of a handful of gaps:

- A well cap that's cracked, warped, or missing its gasket, so it no longer seals tight to the casing.
- A vent that lost its screen, or has one torn or rusted through.
- An electrical conduit or wire entry into the casing that was never sealed after the pump was wired or serviced.
- A casing with a crack, a rusted-through seam, or a top that sits below grade so runoff pools around it.

The [EPA's guidance on protecting a private well](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/protect-your-homes-water) is blunt about it: install a proper well cap or sanitary seal, and check it periodically for damage or a cap that's gone missing entirely. That single part does most of the work of keeping wildlife, insects, and surface water out of your casing. When it fails, or when it was never installed correctly in the first place, the casing turns into an inviting, cool, dark space for anything looking for shelter or water. For what a sound cap actually looks like and when it needs replacing, see our guide to [well cap replacement](/blog/well-cap-replacement/).

## Snakes in a Well or Wellhead: What It's Actually About

Snakes don't target wells for the water. They're after shelter: someplace cool, dark, and out of the wind, and a well casing or the pit around a wellhead fits that description well. In orchard and foothill country, where there's plenty of rock, brush, and irrigation ditches nearby, a gap around a cap or a low spot around the casing is as good as an invitation.

Say you find a snake sitting on top of the well cap, in the pump house, or coiled around the pitless adapter above the water line. That's a nuisance and a "don't reach in there" moment, but it's not automatically a contamination event, because it never reached the water. Say instead it's down the casing, near or below the static water level, or you can't tell how far down it got. Treat that as contamination until testing says otherwise. The same logic applies to any animal, whether it wandered in on its own or fell through a gap it couldn't climb back out of.

## What to Do the Moment You Find One

Before anything else, work through this in order:

1. **Stop drinking and cooking with the water.** Switch to bottled water until you know more. Don't rely on taste or smell to tell you it's fine.
2. **Don't reach into the casing.** A well casing is narrow, dark, and deeper than it looks. Never lower yourself into a well pit or lean far into an open casing alone.
3. **Note where the animal is.** On top of the cap or in the pump house is a different job than down inside the casing near the water.
4. **Kill power to anything electrical nearby before touching it.** If a rodent chewed wiring or an animal is near the pressure switch or control box, shut off the breaker and verify it's dead with a meter first. Anything inside a live panel is a job for a licensed electrician or us, not a flashlight-and-pliers fix.
5. **Get it out, then plan to disinfect.** Once the animal is removed and the entry point is found, the well needs shock chlorination before you trust the water again, even if it looks and smells fine.
6. **Test before you resume normal use.** A clean-looking glass of water doesn't tell you whether bacteria got into the casing.

## Getting the Animal Out Safely

If the animal is above the water line and reachable from the surface, most homeowners can remove it themselves with gloves and a grabber tool, then move straight to cleaning and disinfecting. If it's down in the casing at or below the water level, or you genuinely don't know how far it went, that's a different job. Pulling something out of a narrow casing without dropping it further, damaging the pitless fitting, or fouling the drop pipe usually calls for the right tools and a second pair of hands.

## Bugs and Ants: A Different Kind of Intrusion

Bugs in well water and ants around a well casing are a related but separate problem from a snake or a dead rodent. Insects get in through the same gaps: a torn vent screen, a cap that doesn't seat tight, a conduit entry that was never sealed. A few gnats around a vent screen usually means the screen itself needs replacing, not that something is wrong with your water chemistry.

Ants are a slightly different case. They're drawn to warmth and moisture, which makes a pressure switch or control box an easy target, especially where wiring passes through an unsealed knockout. Ants inside a control box are an electrical nuisance that can cause a pump to trip or fail to start, not a water-quality issue on their own. If that's what you're seeing, our guide to [well pump control box troubleshooting](/blog/well-pump-control-box-troubleshooting/) walks through the symptoms. Either way, if insects are getting into the casing itself rather than just the equipment around it, treat the well cap and vent screen as suspect and have them checked.

## Disinfecting the Well After Contamination

Once the animal is out and you've found (or at least suspect) the entry point, shock chlorination is the standard next step any time a well may have taken in contamination it wasn't built to handle. In broad terms: a chlorine solution goes into the well, and then the pump runs it through every faucet, hose bib, and fixture in the house so the whole system gets treated, not just the casing. That's an important detail people get backwards: the disinfection happens as the pump pushes chlorinated water through the plumbing, not by dumping chlorine down the well and leaving the pump off. [Penn State Extension's guidance on shock chlorination](https://extension.psu.edu/shock-chlorination-of-wells-and-springs) lists a positive coliform test, along with new well construction and work on the well or pump, as a reason to do it.

## Testing Before You Trust the Water Again

Disinfecting kills what's there at the time, but it doesn't tell you whether the fix actually held. Penn State Extension recommends retesting for total coliform roughly 10 to 14 days after shock chlorination and, if that comes back clean, testing again two to three months later. If bacteria come back in either test, Penn State says a continuous disinfection system will be necessary. That's equipment a water treatment company sizes and installs; our part is making sure the cap, seals and casing aren't still letting contamination in. Washington's own [testing recommendations for private wells](https://doh.wa.gov/community-and-environment/drinking-water/contaminants/testing-your-water) call for annual coliform bacteria and nitrate testing regardless, which is a good habit to fall into after an incident like this even once the immediate scare has passed.

If your test comes back positive, don't panic and don't assume the worst either. ## Sealing the Entry Point for Good

None of this is worth doing twice. Once the water tests clean, the last step is making sure whatever let the animal or the bugs in can't happen again.

| What let it in | What actually fixes it |
|---|---|
| Cracked, warped, or gasket-less well cap | Replace the cap, don't just re-seat it |
| Torn or missing vent screen | New screened vent, checked yearly |
| Unsealed electrical conduit into the casing | Seal the penetration where the wire enters the casing |
| Casing sitting low, with runoff pooling around it | Regrade around the wellhead so water sheds away |
| Open or damaged well pit | Ask about converting to a [pitless adapter](/blog/pitless-adapter-explained/), which keeps the connection below frost line and sealed |

A well cap that's already failed once is usually not worth patching. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it safe to drink water from a well that had a dead animal in it?
Not until you've disinfected and tested it. Decomposition introduces bacteria, and depending on how far into the casing the animal got, that contamination can reach the water your pump is drawing from even if the water still looks clear. Stop drinking and cooking with it, switch to bottled water, remove the animal, shock chlorinate the system, and get a coliform test back before resuming normal use. Skipping the test because the water looks fine is the mistake to avoid here.

### How do I get a dead animal out of my well?
If it's above the water line, reachable from the surface, gloves and a grabber tool are usually enough. If it's down in the casing at or below the water level, or you're not sure how far it went, don't reach in or lower yourself into a well pit. Call a well service provider who can retrieve it without dropping it further or damaging the pitless fitting or drop pipe, then move straight to disinfecting the system.

### Will shock chlorination get rid of the smell and bacteria from an animal in the well?
Done correctly, yes, for the bacteria. A chlorine solution is introduced into the well and then circulated through the entire household plumbing system by running the pump, which disinfects the casing, the pressure tank, and the pipes together rather than the well alone. Odor usually clears once the system is fully flushed. If a follow-up test still comes back positive, Penn State Extension says continuous disinfection will be necessary, and it's worth checking whether the entry point is still open.

### How do snakes and bugs get into a sealed well in the first place?
They shouldn't, if the cap, vent screen, and any conduit entries are intact. In practice, the usual suspects are a cap that's cracked, warped, missing its gasket, or was never seated correctly, along with a torn vent screen or an electrical entry point into the casing that was never sealed. Any of those turns a closed system into an open one. Finding an animal or insects inside the casing is usually a sign to inspect the cap and seals, not just remove what got in.
