## Why Your Well Water Smells Like Rotten Eggs

That smell is hydrogen sulfide (H2S), a gas that dissolves in water and escapes the moment the water hits open air. Your nose is incredibly good at detecting it. Most people can smell hydrogen sulfide at concentrations as low as 0.5 parts per million, which is why even a tiny amount makes the whole house reek.

Here in Chelan, Douglas, and Grant counties, hydrogen sulfide shows up for four main reasons.

### 1. Sulfur-reducing bacteria in the well

This is the most common cause we see. These bacteria live in oxygen-poor environments, like the bottom of a well, and they feed on naturally occurring sulfur in the groundwater. As they digest it, they release hydrogen sulfide gas. They're harmless to your health, but they make your water smell terrible and they often travel with iron bacteria, which leaves orange slime in toilet tanks. If you've got both the smell and the slime, read our guide on [iron in well water](/blog/iron-in-well-water/) because you're probably fighting two problems at once.

### 2. A spent anode rod in your water heater

If the smell only shows up in hot water, your well is probably fine. Water heaters contain a magnesium or aluminum rod that sacrifices itself to prevent tank corrosion. As that rod breaks down, it reacts with sulfate in the water and feeds bacteria inside the tank. The result is hot water that stinks while cold water smells clean. This is the cheapest fix on the list.

### 3. Naturally occurring sulfur in the groundwater

Some aquifers in our region just carry dissolved hydrogen sulfide, period. The basalt formations under parts of Douglas and Grant counties can produce sulfur water straight from the ground, no bacteria required. Deeper wells around Quincy and Moses Lake run into this more than wells up the Wenatchee or Entiat valleys. You can't kill what isn't alive, so this type needs treatment equipment rather than disinfection.

### 4. A failing well or contamination near the wellhead

This is the rare one, but it matters. A cracked casing, a damaged well cap, or surface water leaking into the well can introduce organic material that decays and produces sulfur smells along with real health risks. If the smell appeared suddenly after flooding, nearby construction, or an earthquake, get the water tested before you drink any more of it. The EPA's guidance on [private well water](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells) is clear on this: well owners are responsible for their own testing, and sudden changes in smell or taste are a signal to test now, not later.

## How to Narrow Down the Source in 10 Minutes

You can rule causes in or out with a simple sniff test, and it costs you nothing. Here's how we walk customers through it over the phone.

**Step 1: Run the cold tap only.** Fill a glass from a cold faucet that hasn't run in a few hours. Smell it immediately. If cold water smells like rotten eggs, the problem is in your well or plumbing, not the water heater.

**Step 2: Run the hot tap only.** If only the hot water stinks and the cold is fine, it's almost certainly your water heater's anode rod. That's a plumbing fix, not a well problem, and it usually runs $150 to $350 to swap the rod.

**Step 3: Check whether the smell fades as water runs.** If the first glass smells bad but the smell fades after the tap runs for a minute, the gas is building up in your pressure tank and plumbing while the system sits idle. That points to sulfur bacteria colonizing your tank and pipes. A waterlogged or aging pressure tank makes this worse because stagnant water sits longer, so it's worth checking our piece on [waterlogged pressure tanks](/blog/pressure-tank-waterlogged/) if your tank is more than 10 years old.

**Step 4: Smell water at an outside spigot closest to the well.** If water straight from the wellhead area smells strong and constant, you've likely got natural hydrogen sulfide in the aquifer itself, and that means treatment equipment.

## Sulfur Smell in Well Water: How to Get Rid of It

Once you know the source, the fix follows. Here's what actually works for each cause, roughly in order of cost.

### Replace the water heater anode rod

Hot-water-only smell. Swap the magnesium rod for an aluminum-zinc rod, which doesn't feed the bacteria the same way. Flush the tank with a diluted peroxide solution while you're at it. Done in an afternoon.

### Shock chlorinate the well

If sulfur bacteria are the culprit, shock chlorination kills the colony in the well, pressure tank, and plumbing. It's the same process used for coliform bacteria: a measured dose of chlorine sits in the system for 12 to 24 hours, then gets flushed out. It works, but be honest with yourself about the catch. Bacteria often come back within 6 to 18 months, so shocking is more maintenance than cure. We covered the full process, costs, and safety steps in our guide to [shock chlorinating a well](/blog/well-chlorination-shock/). Plan on $250 to $500 to have it done professionally, including post-treatment testing.

### Install continuous treatment

For natural hydrogen sulfide, or bacteria that keep coming back, you need equipment that treats every gallon. The three workhorses are:

- **Oxidizing media filters.** A tank of manganese-dioxide media (often sold as Katalox or similar) oxidizes the sulfide and filters it out. Great for sulfur plus iron together, which is a common combo here.
- **Aeration systems.** Air injection strips the gas out of the water before it reaches your taps. No chemicals, low maintenance, and effective up to fairly high sulfide levels.
- **Chlorine injection with carbon filtration.** A small pump doses chlorine to oxidize the sulfide, then a carbon tank removes the chlorine taste. The most powerful option for heavy sulfur loads, and the most maintenance.

For a deeper comparison of these and other options, our rundown of [well water filtration systems](/blog/well-water-filtration-systems/) covers what each one handles and what it ignores.

### Fix the well itself

If testing shows surface contamination, the cure is structural: a new well cap, casing repair, or sealing work around the wellhead. No filter fixes a broken well.

Marv, who farms 12 acres outside Quincy, fought sulfur smell for two years with grocery store filters on every faucet. ## When the Rotten Egg Smell Means a Bigger Problem

Most sulfur smells are a nuisance, not a danger. The Washington State Department of Health notes that hydrogen sulfide at the levels found in most private wells is an aesthetic issue rather than a health hazard. You can read their private well guidance at [doh.wa.gov](https://doh.wa.gov/community-and-environment/drinking-water). But there are situations where the smell is your well waving a red flag.

**The smell appeared suddenly.** Wells don't usually start producing sulfur overnight. A sudden change can mean a cracked casing, a failed well cap, or surface water intrusion, especially after spring runoff or a hard freeze.

**The smell comes with sputtering faucets or air bursts.** That combination can mean your pump is breaking suction because the water level is dropping, which pulls in gases from the well column. If your faucets are spitting, our guide to [air in water lines](/blog/air-in-water-lines/) walks through what's happening down the hole.

**The smell comes with cloudy water, sediment, or a metallic taste.** Multiple symptoms at once suggest the well itself is degrading, not just the water chemistry.

**Anyone in the house has gotten sick.** Sulfur bacteria themselves are harmless, but the conditions that let them thrive can also harbor coliform bacteria. Test for coliform any time water quality changes.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it safe to drink well water that smells like rotten eggs?

Usually, yes. Hydrogen sulfide at the concentrations found in most private wells is a nuisance, not a health threat, and sulfur bacteria don't cause illness. The exception is when the smell appears suddenly or comes with other changes, because that can signal contamination entering the well. When in doubt, test for coliform bacteria before continuing to drink it.

### Why does my well water only smell like sulfur in the morning?

Hydrogen sulfide gas builds up in your pressure tank and pipes overnight while water sits still. The first water you draw carries the accumulated gas, and the smell fades as fresh water moves through. This pattern almost always points to sulfur bacteria in your plumbing or pressure tank rather than heavy sulfide in the aquifer itself, and shock chlorination is usually the first move.

### Will a water softener remove the rotten egg smell?

No. Softeners remove calcium and magnesium hardness minerals, not dissolved gases. Hydrogen sulfide can actually foul a softener's resin bed and shorten its life. You need oxidation, aeration, or chlorination ahead of the softener to deal with sulfur.

### How much does it cost to fix sulfur smell in well water permanently?

It depends entirely on the source. A water heater anode rod runs $150 to $350, shock chlorination runs $250 to $500 but may need repeating, and permanent treatment systems for natural hydrogen sulfide run $1,800 to $4,500 installed in our area. Start with a $150 water test so you only pay for the fix you actually need.
