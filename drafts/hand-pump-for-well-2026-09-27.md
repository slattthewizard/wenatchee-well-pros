# Hand Pump for Well Water: How It Works Alongside Your Existing Pump

A hand pump for well water backup is a simple idea: a manual well pump that sits in your well next to your electric submersible and gives you water when the power is out. It doesn't replace your existing pump. It's a second, mechanical way to reach the same water, and it only works if your well is built to take one. Casing size, static water level, and the condition of your well seal all decide whether that's a quick add-on or a project that needs planning first.

## How a Deep-Well Hand Pump Fits Into a Well You Already Use

Most homes on a well already have an electric submersible pump doing the work, pushing water up a drop pipe and through wiring that runs down the casing to the pump itself. A deep-well hand pump doesn't touch any of that. It runs its own separate pipe or rod-and-cylinder assembly down the same casing, alongside the existing equipment, terminating in a pump handle or lever at the wellhead. When you work the handle, you're lifting water mechanically, one stroke at a time, with no motor and no breaker involved.

The two systems share a well but not a water path. Your submersible keeps doing the daily work of filling the pressure tank for the whole house. The hand pump sits there unused until you need it, which for most homeowners means a power outage, though some people also use one for a garden spigot or livestock trough to save wear on the electric pump.

This is different from a pitcher pump, the classic cast-iron style you might picture from an old farmhouse, and it's worth knowing which one you're actually looking at before you shop.

## Pitcher Pump vs. Deep-Well Hand Pump: Two Different Machines

A pitcher pump works by suction. It pulls water up from above ground, and atmospheric pressure only supports a column of water so tall, roughly 25 feet in practice. Past that depth, a suction pump simply can't reach the water anymore, no matter how hard you work the handle. That rules it out for any well where the water sits deeper than that, which takes in many drilled wells.

A deep-well hand pump solves that by putting the working parts down in the water instead of trying to suck water up from the surface. A cylinder or piston assembly sits below the static water level, connected to the surface handle by a rod or a length of pipe. Because the pump itself is submerged, it isn't limited by suction, only by how long the manufacturer's rod-and-cylinder assembly is built to run.

You'll see brand names like Simple Pump, Flojak, and Bison come up in searches for this. A Bison hand pump and a Flojak work on the same basic deep-well principle, a rod-driven cylinder set below the water line, even though the hardware differs between makers. Simple Pump's own spec page lists a [maximum static water level of 325 feet](https://www.simplepump.com/products/deep-well-pump) for its deep-well model. Flojak's own [help page](https://flojak.com/pages/help) rates its Plus model for lifting water 250 feet vertically from static water level, with testing to 300 feet. We don't sell or install any of these brands, and mentioning them here isn't an endorsement, just an example of what's actually on the market and what the manufacturers themselves publish about depth limits. Always check a manufacturer's current spec page before buying, since models and ratings change.

## Well Depth vs. Static Water Level: The Number That Actually Matters

Two numbers get confused a lot: how deep your well is drilled, and how deep the water sits inside it. The second one, your static water level, is what actually limits a hand pump. A well drilled to 300 feet with water sitting at 60 feet has a static water level of 60 feet, and that's the figure a hand pump has to work against, not the 300-foot total depth. Local wells vary a lot on this; our guide to [how deep wells run in the Wenatchee Valley](/blog/how-deep-are-wells-wenatchee/) covers why that range is so wide across the foothills and orchard flats.

Manufacturers build in margin on both ends. Flojak, for example, recommends the pump cylinder sit at least 20 feet below static water level and at least 10 feet above the bottom of the well, so there's reservoir above the intake and clearance below it. Simple Pump describes the same idea, positioning the pump with margin below the static water level rather than right at it.

Your static water level isn't fixed. It moves with the seasons, dropping through a hot, dry Wenatchee Valley summer when irrigation demand is heaviest and rising again over the winter. A pump sized with no margin for a wet-season reading can come up short by August. If you don't already know your well's static water level, the fastest way to find it without guessing is to pull your own well record from the Washington Department of Ecology's [well report search tool](https://appswr.ecology.wa.gov/wellconstruction/WellReportSearchTools), which lists depth and water level as measured when the well was drilled. If you'd rather not dig through the state database, call **[(509) 300-5151](tel:+15093005151)** and we can check it at the wellhead during a service visit.

## What Your Well Needs Before a Hand Pump Will Work

Before you order anything, a few things about your well decide whether a hand pump is even possible, let alone practical.

1. **Casing diameter.** The pump's cylinder or pipe has to fit down the casing alongside your existing wiring and drop pipe. Flojak's own spec calls for a minimum of 2 inches of clearance at the narrowest point for its Plus model; other deep-well pumps need more room. This varies by brand and model, so check the current spec page for whatever pump you're considering.
2. **Static water level within the pump's rated lift**, with the margin above and below described earlier.
3. **A well seal or cap with, or able to accept, a second port** for the hand pump's rod or pipe to pass through.
4. **An intact casing that still meets the standard height above ground.** Washington's [standards for the upper terminal of water wells](https://app.leg.wa.gov/wac/default.aspx?cite=173-160-291) require the watertight casing to extend at least six inches above the ground surface.
5. **Room around the wellhead** to actually stand and work a pump handle, which matters more than it sounds like if your pump house is tight or your wellhead sits under a deck or shed.

A simple way to check casing clearance before you buy anything: lower a length of plastic pipe matching the outside diameter of the pump you're considering down the casing on a string, alongside the existing equipment, and see if it reaches the depth the pump would sit at without binding. It's a trick straight from Flojak's own installation FAQ, and it catches a clearance problem before you've spent money on a pump that won't fit.

## Where It Connects: The Well Seal, Casing, and Your Existing Wiring

The hand pump enters your well through the same cap, or well seal, that already covers the casing, usually through a dedicated second port or a replacement seal drilled to fit both the existing wiring and the new pump. Washington's well construction rules require a drilled well's cap to be mechanically secured to the casing, not just loosely set on top, and any vent, port or line through the top to be shielded or sealed so foreign matter can't get in. If installing a hand pump means disturbing that seal, plan on having it properly resealed afterward rather than leaving a gap around the new opening. A cap that's already rusted, cracked, or loose is worth fixing on its own regardless of a hand pump; see our [well cap replacement guide](/blog/well-cap-replacement/) for what that involves.

This is a different piece of hardware from a [pitless adapter](/blog/pitless-adapter-explained/), which routes your main water line out through the casing wall below the frost line rather than up through the top seal, so don't expect the hand pump project to touch it.

In most cases, a properly sized deep-well hand pump installs without disturbing your electric submersible at all, since the two run down the casing side by side. Where the casing is genuinely too narrow for both, something has to give, and Flojak's own FAQ puts the answer plainly: store the hand pump until it's needed and pull the electric pump temporarily to make room, or accept that a hand pump isn't going to work in that particular well. ## Hand Pump vs. Generator: Two Different Kinds of Backup

A hand pump and a generator solve the same problem in very different ways, and on many properties it makes sense to plan for both rather than choosing one over the other.

A generator restores your normal water pressure. Once it's running your electric pump, your whole house works the way it always does, showers, laundry, irrigation, all of it. It needs fuel on hand and something to keep it running for as long as the outage lasts.

A hand pump needs no fuel and has almost nothing to fail, but it gives you a slow trickle by comparison, enough for drinking water, cooking, flushing a toilet by hand, or watering a few pots, not enough to run a house normally. It's there specifically for the outage that runs longer than your fuel supply, or the one that catches you with an empty generator tank. ## Keeping a Hand Pump Ready Through a North Central Washington Winter

A hand pump that sits idle for months at a time needs a little attention, or it won't work the one time you actually need it.

Cold matters most. Any part of the pump above ground, the handle, the linkage, exposed pipe, is exposed to the same hard freezes that threaten any unheated pump house or wellhead here. If it's mounted outside a heated enclosure, treat it the same way you'd treat any exposed wellhead going into winter: protect it from direct freezing and check it before the first hard cold snap, not during it.

Working the handle a few times each season, rather than leaving it completely untouched for a year, keeps seals and gaskets from drying out or sticking. It also confirms the pump still primes and lifts water on demand, which is exactly the reassurance you want a backup system to give you before you're relying on it.

And because static water level shifts with the seasons here, a hand pump that had comfortable margin in a wet spring can lose some of that margin by the end of a dry summer, especially in years with real drought pressure on the aquifer. If your well's water level has been trending down, it's worth rechecking the numbers against your pump's rated lift rather than assuming the original installation is still accurate years later. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I install a hand pump without removing my electric submersible pump?

In most wells, yes. A deep-well hand pump runs its own pipe or rod-and-cylinder assembly down the casing next to your existing wiring and drop pipe, so the two operate independently and don't interfere with each other. The limiting factor is casing diameter. If your casing is too narrow to fit both systems side by side, the practical options are storing the hand pump until it's needed and temporarily removing the electric pump, or accepting that your particular well can't fit both at once.

### How deep can a hand pump lift water from a well?

It depends on the type and model. A suction-style pitcher pump tops out around 25 feet of static water level, which rules it out for any well where the water sits deeper. A deep-well hand pump, which sets a cylinder below the water line and lifts mechanically rather than by suction, reaches much farther. Manufacturer spec pages list figures such as 325 feet for Simple Pump's deep-well model and 250 feet rated, 300 feet tested, for Flojak's Plus model. Always check the current spec page for the exact model you're considering.

### Will a hand pump work if my well casing is narrow?

It depends on the model and your casing's actual diameter. Some deep-well hand pumps need only a couple of inches of clearance at the narrowest point in the casing; others need more room, especially if they have to share that space with your existing wiring and drop pipe. Before buying anything, lower a test length of pipe matching the pump's outside diameter down your casing on a string to confirm it clears the full depth without binding on the existing equipment.

### How do you get water from a well without electricity?

For most homeowners on a drilled well, the two practical options are a generator sized to run the existing electric submersible pump, or a hand pump installed alongside it for a slower manual supply. A generator restores normal water pressure throughout the house but needs fuel on hand. A hand pump needs no fuel and has very little to fail, but delivers only enough water by hand for drinking, cooking, and basic flushing, not for showers or irrigation. Planning around both, rather than relying on just one, covers the most outage scenarios.
