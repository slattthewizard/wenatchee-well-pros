# Sediment Filter for Well Water: Spin-Down vs. Cartridge and Getting the Micron Rating Right

Every well carries some grit. The question is whether your filter is catching a normal amount or losing a fight it shouldn't be in. A sediment filter for well water is one of the simplest devices in the plumbing world: a housing, a screen or a cartridge, and two fittings. Pick the wrong type or the wrong micron rating, though, and you either clog it weekly or let fine grit straight through to your fixtures. And if it's clogging no matter what you put in it, the filter usually isn't the real problem.

## Spin-Down vs. Cartridge: What Each One Actually Does

A spin-down filter is a clear housing with a reusable mesh screen inside. Water enters at an angle, spins, and the motion throws heavier particles toward the outer wall and down into a collection bowl at the bottom. You flush that bowl by opening a valve, no parts to buy, no cartridge to swap. It's a mechanical, coarse first line of defense built for sand and grit, not fine silt.

A cartridge filter is a housing that holds a replaceable element, commonly pleated paper, spun polypropylene, or a similar wound fiber. Cartridge media traps particles by pore size, and different cartridges are rated from very fine up to fairly coarse depending on what's inside. According to a [University of Nebraska-Lincoln Extension guide on sediment filtration](https://extensionpubs.unl.edu/publication/g1492/na/html/view), many cartridge filters are only rated for particles 20 microns and larger, which can let some silt through, so check the rating before you buy; finer ratings such as 10 microns are available. Once a cartridge loads up, you replace it. There's no cleaning it out and reusing it the way you would a spin-down screen.

Plenty of well systems in orchard country run both: a spin-down first to knock out coarse sand and pipe scale, then a cartridge behind it to catch the finer silt the spin-down lets pass. Neither one, on its own, is a fix for water that's carrying more sediment than a normal well should.

| | Spin-down (mesh) | Cartridge |
|---|---|---|
| How it catches sediment | Spins water, throws coarse particles into a bowl | Traps particles as water passes through the media |
| What it's good at | Sand, grit, pipe scale | Finer silt and suspended particles |
| Maintenance | Open a valve and flush | Replace the element on a schedule |
| Ongoing cost | No element to buy for routine flushing | Recurring cartridge replacement |

## Micron Ratings: How Fine Do You Actually Need

Micron ratings only mean something next to the size of what you're trying to catch. Per the same UNL Extension guide, silt particles generally run from about 2 to 50 microns, and sand runs from about 50 microns up to 2 millimeters. That's a wide range, and it's why "just get the finest filter" is bad advice: a very fine filter will also strain out sand and silt, but it loads up faster, and NDSU notes that a 2 to 5 micron filter takes more pressure to push water through.

North Dakota State University Extension's guide to [sediment, carbon, and mixed-media filtration](https://www.ndsu.edu/agriculture/extension/publications/filtration-sediment-activated-carbon-and-mixed-media) puts a number on it: private systems on a drilled well, which is most of what's under Chelan, Douglas, and Grant counties, including the orchard-heavy ground around [Quincy](/well-service-quincy/), typically do well with a filter rated around 10 to 15 microns. Systems on a dug or bored well, where fine clay is more common, generally need the finest filter available, down around 2 microns.

The practical rule: start with the largest micron rating that solves the problem you actually have. If you're seeing coarse grit, a spin-down screen in the 50 to 100 micron range may be all you need. If the water still looks cloudy after that, step down to a finer cartridge behind it. Jumping straight to the smallest rating on the shelf just means more filter changes for no real benefit.

## Where It Goes: Placement in the Plumbing

On a private well system, a whole-house sediment filter belongs right after the pressure tank, before the line splits off to the water heater, fixtures, and any treatment equipment further downstream. That placement means everything in the house benefits, not just one tap. NDSU Extension's guide notes this same sequencing: the filter comes early, ahead of anything else in the treatment train, so it protects the equipment behind it rather than the other way around.

If you're adding a filter to a system that doesn't have one, that spot near the tank is where a water treatment company will plumb it in and size it to your flow rate. ## Reading What's Caught in the Filter

What's sitting in the housing tells you a lot before you ever call anyone:

- **Coarse tan or gray grit** that settles fast usually means sand, either from the aquifer material itself or from a well screen and gravel pack that isn't doing its job anymore. That's covered in more depth in [our guide to sand in well water](/blog/sand-in-well-water/).
- **A fine, cloudy film** that doesn't settle as quickly is more likely silt, which can show up after nearby digging, heavy runoff, or a well that was recently serviced. [Our sediment article](/blog/well-water-sediment/) walks through where that kind of sediment actually comes from.
- **Rust-orange sludge**, especially if it has a slimy or stringy texture, points toward oxidized iron or iron bacteria rather than plain grit. If it looks and smells like something is alive in there, that's [iron bacteria](/blog/iron-bacteria-well-slime/), not sediment, and it needs a different approach.

Knowing which of these you're dealing with saves you from buying the wrong filter twice.

## When a Filter That Clogs Fast Means a Well or Pump Problem

A correctly sized sediment filter should run for weeks to months between service, depending on your water use and the rating you chose. If yours is caking up every few days, that's not a filter problem. Something upstream is putting more sediment into your plumbing than a normal well should.

Two well-side causes are worth ruling out:

1. **A well screen or gravel pack that's breaking down.** Under [Washington's well construction rules](https://app.leg.wa.gov/wac/default.aspx?cite=173-160-111), a well screen is built to keep unconsolidated aquifer material out of the casing, and a gravel or filter pack placed around it does the same job from the outside in. When either one degrades, corrodes, or was built with the wrong slot or pack size for the formation, fine material starts working its way past it and straight up the pipe. Our [guide to sand in well water](/blog/sand-in-well-water/) covers the failure side of this in more detail.
2. **A pump set too close to the bottom of the well.** Sediment settles at the bottom of the casing over time. A submersible pump intake set right down in that layer, rather than well above it, will pull settled material up into the system every time it runs. It can also start after a [well pump replacement](/well-pump-replacement-wenatchee/) if the new pump was hung at a different depth than the original, and it's also worth checking with a [well yield test](/blog/well-yield-test/) if flow seems weaker overall.

A few patterns point toward one of these rather than a one-time event:

1. The problem showed up suddenly, right after a pump replacement or well work nearby, rather than building up slowly.
2. The filter fills with coarse grit every time, not a light dusting of fine silt.
3. Every tap in the house is affected at once, not just the ones that sat unused for a while.
4. Switching to a coarser filter didn't slow down how fast it loads.

If that sounds like what you're seeing, a finer filter or a second stage isn't going to solve it. ## Cleaning a Spin-Down vs. Changing a Cartridge

Spin-down filters are built for frequent, low-effort maintenance: open the flush valve at the bottom, let the collected sediment drain out, close it. No element to order, no downtime waiting for a part. How often depends entirely on how much sediment your well is producing, which is exactly why a spin-down that needs flushing daily is telling you something.

Cartridge filters work differently. You're watching for a drop in water pressure or flow at the fixtures, which means the element has loaded up enough to restrict flow. At that point it gets replaced, not cleaned. One caution from the UNL Extension guide is worth taking seriously: bacteria can build up on filter cartridges, particularly paper media, and regular replacement is what keeps that in check. Change it when it's due, not weeks after.

| What you're seeing | What it usually means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Housing has a light coating, water runs clear | Filter is working as designed | Nothing yet |
| Flow drops noticeably at the fixtures | Filter has loaded up | Flush the spin-down or replace the cartridge |
| Water clears right after you flush or swap, then fouls again within days | A supply-side problem, not the filter | Have the well and pump looked at |

That third row is the one worth paying attention to. A filter that keeps solving the symptom without the problem going away is a filter doing its job while something upstream keeps making more work for it.

## What a Sediment Filter Won't Fix

A sediment filter removes suspended particles. That's it. Per the UNL Extension guide, it does not effectively remove dissolved minerals or nitrate, it isn't the tool for microbial contamination, and it won't touch dissolved iron or manganese that hasn't oxidized into visible particles yet. If your water has a taste, odor, or health-related concern beyond visible grit, that's a different conversation, usually involving a water test and, if needed, treatment equipment sized for what the lab finds. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What micron rating sediment filter do I need for well water?

It depends on your well type and what's actually in the water. NDSU Extension guidance puts drilled wells, which cover most homes in Chelan, Douglas, and Grant counties, in the 10 to 15 micron range, while dug or bored wells with fine clay generally need filters rated closer to 2 microns. Since silt runs roughly 2 to 50 microns and sand runs 50 microns and up, a coarse spin-down screen catches the sand while a finer cartridge behind it handles the silt. Start coarser than you think you need, then step down only if the water still looks cloudy afterward.

### Should I use a spin-down filter, a cartridge filter, or both?

Many well systems run both in sequence: a spin-down first to knock out coarse sand and grit without any ongoing cost beyond flushing it, then a cartridge behind it to catch finer suspended material. If your sediment is mostly coarse and settles fast, a spin-down alone may be enough. If you're seeing persistent cloudiness after a spin-down, add a cartridge stage rather than replacing the spin-down with one. A water treatment company can size either option to your home's flow rate.

### Why does my sediment filter clog every week or two?

That pace usually means more sediment is entering your plumbing than a properly working well should produce. Two causes worth ruling out are a well screen or gravel pack that's degraded enough to let aquifer material through, or a submersible pump set too close to the bottom of the casing where settled sediment collects. A finer filter or a second filtration stage will not fix either cause; it will just need changing more often. Getting the well and pump checked is the more direct path to actually solving it.

### Will a sediment filter make my well water safe to drink?

No. A sediment filter removes visible particles, not the things that make well water unsafe, like coliform bacteria or nitrate. The [Washington State Department of Health recommends testing private well water every year for coliform bacteria and nitrate](https://doh.wa.gov/community-and-environment/drinking-water/contaminants/testing-your-water), regardless of how clear the water looks after filtration. Clear water from a sediment filter tells you the grit is gone. It doesn't tell you anything about bacteria, nitrate, or dissolved contaminants, which need their own test.
