# Well Pump Surge Protector: How Lightning Actually Reaches a Submersible Pump

Say a summer thunderstorm rolls through the valley overnight, and by morning the pump won't run at all. No clicking, no hum, no error light on the control box, just nothing. That pattern, a pump that worked fine the night before and is completely dead the next morning after a storm, is one of the few well failures that has nothing to do with age or wear. It points at the electrical system, and specifically at a surge that found its way down the wire to the motor.

A well pump surge protector, also called a lightning arrestor, is the device that's supposed to stop that. It's cheap compared to the pump it protects, and on an older system it may be missing or poorly grounded. Here's what it actually does, what it can't do, and where it needs to sit to matter.

## Why a Well Pump Is an Easy Target

A submersible pump sits at the far end of a long run of wire, sometimes several hundred feet, connecting it to a control box and breaker panel at the surface. That wire is a straight electrical path from the pump house to a motor sitting in water, inside a steel or PVC casing that goes deep into the ground. You don't need a direct hit on your property to get a damaging surge. A strike on a nearby power line, or even switching loads on the utility grid, can send a voltage spike down that same line into your pump house and out to the motor.

This is common on any home, but it's a bigger deal for a well because the pump itself is expensive and hard to reach. A blown appliance is a trip to the hardware store. A blown submersible motor means pulling the pump, which on a deep well is not a homeowner job.

## Direct Hit vs. Induced Surge: Two Different Problems

It matters which one you're protecting against, because they aren't the same thing. A direct lightning strike to the wellhead, the overhead line, or the building itself carries enormous energy, and a residential arrestor isn't built to absorb it. Washington's Department of Health says so about the lightning arrestor shown in its standard water system control diagram: it protects against voltage surges from lightning, switching loads, and power line interference, but "these do not protect against a direct strike," according to the [state's pump controls tech tip](https://doh.wa.gov/sites/default/files/legacy/Documents/Pubs//331-401.pdf).

What an arrestor is built for is the more common event: an induced surge. Franklin Electric, which makes submersible motors and the arrestors sold for them, explains in its [lightning arrestor manual](https://fele.widen.net/content/mpq9luxgy5/pdf/225594101_Lightning_Arrestor_Manual.pdf?u=bt7ctv) that overhead power lines draw lightning, and those strikes induce voltage surges in the lines that feed homes. Lightning doesn't have to touch your property to put a spike on your wiring. That induced surge is the threat a well pump surge protector is designed to catch, provided it's grounded properly (more on that below).

## What a Lightning Arrestor Actually Does

A lightning arrestor is wired between the pump's power lines and ground. Under normal voltage it does nothing. When a spike arrives, it gives the surge a path to ground before it can reach the motor windings or the control box electronics; Franklin's manual describes it conducting the surge to ground, then shutting off again without damage to itself, ready for the next one. Washington's own pump controls reference sheet lists this as a standard component of a small water system's control setup, right alongside the breaker box, pump control box, and pressure switch, described as protecting "the pump motor and controls from voltage surges caused by lightning, switching loads and power line interference" (see the [Washington DOH pump controls diagram](https://doh.wa.gov/sites/default/files/legacy/Documents/Pubs//331-401.pdf)).

A well pump motor's windings are wrapped in insulation rated for a specific voltage. A surge that exceeds that rating punches through the insulation, and once that happens, the motor is done. That's why an arrestor matched to the pump's voltage (Franklin sells separate single-phase and three-phase models) matters more than most homeowners realize: it's protecting a single point of failure that costs real money to fix, not a cheap part.

## Where Surge Protection Belongs on a Well System

A surge protector at the main panel helps the rest of the house, but it isn't what protects a submersible motor. Franklin's [AIM installation manual](https://fele.widen.net/content/mrbmponfcz/pdf/M1311_60Hz_AIM_Manual.pdf?u=bt7ctv) says every submersible motor needs its own dedicated surge arrestor, mounted as close to the motor as practical, and notes that some motors of 5 hp and smaller come with one built in (they're marked "Equipped with Lightning Arrestors"). Here's how the pieces fit:

| Protection layer | What it's for | Where it typically sits |
|---|---|---|
| Dedicated pump arrestor | Diverting induced surges on the pump circuit away from the motor | In the pump panel or [control box](/blog/well-pump-control-box-troubleshooting/), or in a separate box at the wellhead |
| Built-in motor arrestor | The same job, on motors that come with one | Inside the motor itself, on models marked for it |
| Arrestor ground | Giving the surge a better path to ground than the motor windings | Run metal to metal down to below the pumping water level, usually through the drop cable ground wire to the motor |
| Whole-house surge protector | Surges arriving from the utility line for the rest of the house | Main service panel; not a substitute for the pump arrestor |

If your system has a panel protector but no dedicated pump arrestor, the motor and control box are still exposed.

## Grounding Is the Part That Makes or Breaks It

An arrestor only works as well as the ground path behind it. Franklin's [lightning arrestor manual](https://fele.widen.net/content/mpq9luxgy5/pdf/225594101_Lightning_Arrestor_Manual.pdf?u=bt7ctv) is blunt about why: the surge takes the best path to ground it can find, and if the arrestor's ground isn't as good as the motor's, most of the surge goes to ground through the motor and damages the windings even with an arrestor installed. Franklin's instruction is that the arrestor ground lead be connected metal to metal all the way down to the water-bearing strata, and it warns that grounding the arrestor only to the supply ground or a driven ground rod gives the motor little or no surge protection. Its AIM manual notes this is usually done by tying the drop cable ground wire to the motor lead or the motor ground lug. An arrestor with a corroded, undersized, or wrongly routed ground wire is doing a fraction of its job.

If it's been years since anyone looked at the ground connections on your system, that's worth checking before the next storm season, not after a pump fails.

## Signs Your System Took a Hit

Not every dead pump after a storm is a surge, but a few signs point that way:

1. The breaker trips again the instant you reset it, or won't reset at all.
2. The control box shows a scorch mark, a melted spot, or a burnt smell.
3. The pressure switch or gauge stopped responding entirely, with no slow decline beforehand.
4. A capacitor or relay inside the control box looks swollen, cracked, or discolored.
5. The pump draws no water at all, with no gradual pressure drop leading up to it.

Say your gauge reads zero and the breaker won't stay on. That combination, sudden and total, right after a lightning-heavy storm, is the pattern worth taking seriously.

## After the Storm: What to Check Before You Reset Anything

1. Shut off the breaker to the pump circuit at the panel, and verify it's dead with a meter before touching any wiring, terminal, or connection. Never work inside a live panel.
2. Look at the panel, the control box, and the pressure switch terminals for obvious scorch marks or melted insulation.
3. Check whether the control box has an internal reset or protector that tripped. If you're not sure what you're looking at, that's a sign to stop rather than guess.
4. If everything looks physically intact, reset the breaker once and see if the pump starts normally. If it trips again immediately, or the pump hums without turning, stop there.
5. For anything involving a live panel, a control box you can't identify the parts of, or pulling a pump from a deep well, that's a job for a professional rather than trial and error.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does a well pump surge protector stop a direct lightning strike?

No. Washington's own pump controls guidance says these devices protect against voltage surges from lightning, switching loads, and power line interference, but not a direct strike, and Franklin Electric describes its arrestors as protection against surges induced by lightning hitting nearby lines. A direct strike carries far more energy than any residential arrestor is rated to absorb. What the arrestor protects against is the much more common scenario: a surge traveling in from somewhere else on the grid.

### Where should the lightning arrestor be installed on a well system?

Franklin Electric's installation manual calls for a dedicated arrestor for every submersible motor, mounted as close to the motor as practical, usually in the pump panel or control box, sometimes in a separate box at the wellhead. Some smaller motors have one built in. The ground matters as much as the arrestor: Franklin wants it run metal to metal down below the pumping water level, usually through the drop cable ground wire, not just to a ground rod. A whole-house protector at the main panel is a useful extra, not a substitute. If you're unsure what your system has, confirm it during a service visit.

### How do I know if lightning damaged my well pump instead of something else?

The pattern to watch for is sudden and total: a pump that worked normally the day before and is completely dead, with no gradual decline in pressure or output beforehand, following a storm with nearby lightning. A breaker that won't reset or trips immediately, a scorch mark or burnt smell at the control box, or a swollen capacitor all point toward a surge rather than routine wear. A pump that's been declining gradually, losing pressure over weeks, is more likely a separate mechanical issue.

### Does homeowners insurance cover a well pump damaged by lightning?

Many homeowners policies cover sudden, accidental electrical damage from a lightning-related surge, since it's treated as a covered peril rather than gradual wear. Whether it's worth filing depends on your deductible compared to the repair cost, and some policies handle well equipment differently than they handle household appliances. The only way to know for certain is to read your own policy or call your carrier, since coverage terms vary by insurer and by state.
