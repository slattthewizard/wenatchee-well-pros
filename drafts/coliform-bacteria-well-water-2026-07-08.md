## What a Positive Coliform Bacteria Test Actually Means

Coliform bacteria are a broad family of organisms that live in soil, plant matter, and the guts of warm-blooded animals. Most types found in nature are harmless to drink. The reason labs test for them isn't because coliform itself makes people sick. It's because coliform is easy to detect and it tends to show up whenever a pathway exists for surface contamination to reach your water.

Think of it like a smoke detector. The beeping isn't the danger. It's the signal that something is present that shouldn't be, and it's telling you to go find out what.

Most private well tests report on two or three categories, and the wording on your lab slip matters a lot:

- **Total coliform positive, E. coli negative.** The most common result. Something, usually surface water, soil, or organic debris, is getting into your well. It's a real problem, but not an emergency.
- **Total coliform positive, E. coli positive (or fecal coliform positive).** This means fecal contamination, from animal or human waste, is reaching your water. This is the version that requires same-day action.
- **Total coliform negative.** Clean result. No action needed beyond your normal testing schedule.

If your report shows E. coli, stop drinking and cooking with the water right now, switch to bottled water, and don't wait to see if a second test comes back different. That level of contamination carries real risk of gastrointestinal illness, and for kids, elderly family members, or anyone with a weakened immune system, it can get serious fast. ## Total Coliform vs. Fecal Coliform vs. E. coli: Why the Wording Matters

Lab reports use these terms almost interchangeably in conversation, but they mean different levels of concern. Knowing the difference keeps you from either panicking over a minor result or brushing off a serious one.

| Result Type | What It Indicates | Urgency Level | Typical Next Step |
|---|---|---|---|
| Total coliform positive, E. coli negative | Surface water or debris intrusion; source is usually environmental | Address within days | Inspect wellhead, shock chlorinate, retest |
| Fecal coliform positive | Warm-blooded animal waste reaching the well | Address immediately | Stop drinking water, inspect for septic/animal source, shock, retest |
| E. coli positive | Confirmed fecal contamination, active health risk | Same-day action | Switch to bottled water, call a professional, shock and retest |
| Repeat positive after shock chlorination | Ongoing pathway, not a one-time event | Address before drinking resumes | Full wellhead and casing inspection |

Total coliform alone, without E. coli or fecal coliform, is by far the most common scenario on private wells around here. It usually traces back to something mechanical: a cracked well cap, a missing vent screen, or a casing seam that lets rain or snowmelt trickle down into the casing. It's annoying, but it's rarely a health crisis on its own.

This is also how the EPA's own rules treat it. The federal Revised Total Coliform Rule uses total coliform as an indicator test precisely because it's a reliable early warning for the presence of other, harder-to-test-for pathogens. You can read the full standard at the [EPA's page on the Revised Total Coliform Rule](https://www.epa.gov/dwreginfo/revised-total-coliform-rule).

## Where Coliform Bacteria in Well Water Comes From Around Here

North Central Washington throws a few specific risk factors at private wells that folks in other regions don't deal with as often.

**Spring snowmelt and irrigation season.** When the Columbia River valley thaws out and orchard irrigation ramps up, ground saturation goes way up. Water that would normally soak into soil and get filtered instead runs across the surface, and any weak point at a wellhead becomes a direct path into the casing.

**Older or buried wellheads.** A lot of rural properties from Cashmere to Chelan still have wells set in pits or buried below grade, a common construction style decades ago that's now known to be a contamination magnet. If your wellhead sits below ground level, that's often the whole story right there.

**Livestock and septic proximity.** Washington code requires at least 100 feet between a well and a septic drainfield, but older properties were sometimes grandfathered in closer, and corrals or pastures uphill from a well can carry runoff straight to it.

**A cracked or unsealed well cap.** This is the single most common cause we find. A gap the size of a pencil is enough for insects, rodents, or surface water to get into the casing. We covered this in detail in our guide to [well cap replacement](/blog/well-cap-replacement/).

The Washington Department of Health tracks coliform as one of the most frequently detected issues on private well tests statewide, and their guidance on interpreting bacteria results is worth a read if you want the full technical picture: [doh.wa.gov](https://doh.wa.gov/community-and-environment/drinking-water).

## What to Do in the First 24 Hours After a Positive Result

Your response should match your result. Here's the practical order of operations.

**If E. coli or fecal coliform is present:** Stop drinking and cooking with the water immediately. Use bottled water. Don't bathe infants in it. Call a professional the same day, especially if you can't quickly identify an obvious cause like a recently opened well cap.

**If total coliform alone is present:** You don't need to panic, but don't ignore it either. Boiling water for one minute will kill coliform bacteria if you need drinking water in the short term, which is the opposite of the nitrate situation, where boiling makes things worse. Then move on to finding and fixing the source.

**Either way, inspect the wellhead first.** Before you do anything else, walk out and look at the cap, casing, and surrounding grade. Cracks, gaps, standing water near the wellhead, or a cap that isn't sitting flush are the most common culprits and often the cheapest to fix.

**Retest to confirm.** Labs occasionally produce false positives from sampling technique, especially if the collection bottle or tap wasn't handled correctly. One positive result is a reason to act, but if the fix seems too easy, a second sample confirms you're chasing the right problem. A basic lab test runs $25 to $50 if you collect the sample yourself. ## Fixing It: Shock Chlorination, Prevention, and When It's Not Enough

For the majority of total coliform positives, especially the kind Denise had, the standard fix is shock chlorination: dosing the well with a strong chlorine solution, letting it sit for 12 to 24 hours, then flushing it out. Done correctly, it costs about $30 in supplies and takes a weekend.

We've written a full step-by-step walkthrough of the process, including bleach dosing by well depth and exactly how long to wait before retesting, in our guide to [shock chlorination for wells](/blog/well-chlorination-shock/). If you're comfortable with basic wellhead work and the cap isn't damaged, that guide has everything you need.

Shock chlorination is a one-time disinfection, though, not a permanent fix. It kills what's in the water today. It does nothing about a cracked cap, a buried pit, or a septic system too close to the wellhead, the way Tom and Linda's pit was letting creek water in with every storm. If your well tests positive again after a proper shock, you don't have a bacteria problem anymore. You have a pathway problem, and that calls for a physical inspection, not more bleach.

### Preventing Repeat Positives

Carlos, who lives on a small orchard near Chelan, shocked his well three times over two summers, and it kept coming back positive for total coliform every few months. By the time he called us, he'd spent close to $150 on bleach and test strips with nothing to show for it. A wellhead inspection found the culprit in about ten minutes: a missing vent screen that let rain and insects straight into the casing. A $180 cap and screen replacement, followed by one final shock chlorination, ended it. He hasn't had a positive test in over a year.

A few habits keep most wells out of this cycle entirely:

- **Inspect your wellhead every spring**, before irrigation season saturates the ground. Look for cracks, gaps, and standing water pooled against the casing.
- **Test annually**, and always after any work that opens the well, a new pump, pressure tank swap, or cap replacement.
- **Know your setbacks.** Keep septic drainfields, livestock, and fertilizer storage well clear of the wellhead.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it safe to drink well water with a positive coliform test?

It depends on which result you got. If E. coli or fecal coliform is present, stop drinking the water immediately and switch to bottled water until the problem is fixed and a retest comes back clean. If only total coliform is positive, boiling the water for one minute makes it safe to drink short-term while you find and fix the source.

### How do I get rid of coliform bacteria in my well water?

Shock chlorination is the standard fix for most cases: a strong chlorine dose, a 12 to 24 hour wait, then a thorough flush. It costs about $30 in supplies for a typical residential well. If the well tests positive again afterward, the real problem is usually a physical entry point, like a cracked cap or damaged casing, that needs to be found and repaired before bleach will hold.

### How long does it take to fix coliform bacteria in well water?

A straightforward case, like a cracked cap causing total coliform without E. coli, usually resolves in about two weeks: a day to inspect and repair the wellhead, a day to shock chlorinate, several days to flush and let chlorine clear, then a 7 to 14 day wait before the confirming retest. Cases involving a buried well pit or septic contamination take longer, since physical repairs are involved.

### Can coliform bacteria in well water make you sick?

Most coliform bacteria found in nature won't make a healthy adult sick on their own. The real danger is what a positive result signals: a pathway that could also be letting in E. coli, viruses, or other pathogens. That's why any positive result, especially one that includes E. coli or fecal coliform, should be treated as a signal to act, not ignored just because you feel fine today.
