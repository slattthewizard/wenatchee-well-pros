# Sand Point Well: Where a Driven Well Actually Works

If you have heard the term "sand point well" from a neighbor, an old property listing, or a hardware store clerk, you are hearing about one of the simplest ways to get water out of the ground. No drill rig, no rotary bit, just pipe driven straight down until it hits water. It is also one of the most location-dependent methods there is. Where the ground cooperates, it works well. Where it does not, no amount of hammering changes the geology underneath your feet.

## What a Sand Point Well Actually Is

A sand point well, also called a driven well or drive point well, is built by joining a pointed screen section, the "drive point," to the bottom of a length of small-diameter pipe, then driving that assembly straight into the ground with repeated blows until it reaches water-bearing sand or gravel. The [U.S. Geological Survey](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/groundwater-wells) describes the method plainly: a small-diameter pipe is driven into soft ground such as sand or gravel, with a screen at the bottom to filter out sediment.

The pipe itself usually runs 1 1/4 or 2 inches in diameter, assembled from 4 or 5 foot sections with threaded couplings. The drive point at the bottom, typically a 2 or 3 foot screened section with a hardened steel tip, is what actually punches through the soil and lets water in once it reaches a saturated layer. There is no borehole in the usual sense. The pipe is the well.

That makes a driven well fundamentally different from a dug well, which is a hand-excavated pit lined with stone, tile, or concrete, and from a modern drilled well, which uses a rotary or cable-tool rig to bore a hole and set casing that can run 1,000 feet deep or more. A driven well sits at the shallow, low-cost end of the spectrum. It also sits at the shallow, exposed end of the water-quality spectrum, which matters more than most people expect.

## How a Driven Well Goes Into the Ground

The process is straightforward where it works. The drive point and first pipe section go in, then a driving cap or coupling takes the hammer blows, whether from a hand maul, a slide hammer, or mechanical driving equipment, one section at a time. As the point advances, more pipe is threaded on top. Periodically the crew stops and bails or pumps the pipe to see whether it is producing clean water yet, or still pulling up wet sand.

Depth is limited by the method itself, not just patience. The [EPA puts driven wells at roughly 30 to 50 feet deep](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/learn-about-private-water-wells), with hand driving usually at the shallow end of that range and weighted hammers or driving equipment needed to go further. Past that, the friction of the pipe against the surrounding soil and the limits of the driving force involved make the method impractical, regardless of what is underneath.

Once the point is producing, the pump sits above ground. A driven well's narrow pipe diameter rules out a submersible pump, so the setup is almost always a shallow well jet pump, which pulls water up by suction and works to roughly 25 feet of lift, or in older and off-grid setups, a hand pump. Priming and troubleshooting a shallow jet pump is its own topic; our guide on [how to prime a well pump](/blog/how-to-prime-a-well-pump/) covers what a jet pump needs to hold suction.

Washington also has a specific construction rule for the top of a driven well. Under [WAC 173-160-271](https://apps.leg.wa.gov/wac/default.aspx?cite=173-160-271), the upper section needs an oversized hole at least 4 inches larger in diameter than the casing pipe, extending a minimum of 6 feet below the surface. That annular space has to stay at least half full of bentonite or bentonite slurry while the pipe is being driven, and the rest of the space up to grade gets sealed with neat cement grout, neat cement, or bentonite. That seal is what keeps a shallow pipe from becoming a direct path for surface water to reach the aquifer.

## Where Driven Wells Work: Shallow, Clean Sand and Gravel

The method only works in loose, unconsolidated material: sand, fine gravel, or a sand-and-gravel mix, with no cobbles, boulders, hardpan, or rock in the way. The USGS is direct about this: driven wells are built in areas of sand and gravel and can only reach shallow water. That is not a limitation you can drill or hammer around.

The settings that actually favor a driven well are river-bottom alluvium, old floodplain deposits, sandy stream terraces, and glacial outwash, anywhere loose granular soil sits over a water table that is close to the surface. A few things to check before assuming a piece of ground will support one:

1. Pull a nearby well log through Ecology's well report records to see what a neighboring well actually hit going down.
2. Probe or hand-dig a test hole to see what the first several feet of soil actually look like, not what the surface soil looks like.
3. Find out how deep to a stable, year-round water table, not just a wet spot after a rainy week.
4. Rule out shallow bedrock or a dense clay layer, either of which stops a drive point cold.

|---|---|---|
| Ground it needs | Loose sand or gravel, no rock | Any formation, including basalt |
| Typical depth | Up to about 50 feet | Often several hundred feet |

## Why Most of Chelan, Douglas and Grant Counties Are the Wrong Ground for One

This is the part that matters most for anyone reading this in orchard country or the foothills around Wenatchee, East Wenatchee, Cashmere, Leavenworth, Chelan, Quincy, or Moses Lake. Much of the region sits over basalt bedrock from the Columbia Plateau lava flows, or over dense glacial till, neither of which a drive point can penetrate. You cannot hammer a well point through rock. Where the surface soil looks sandy, it is often only a thin layer over hardpan or basalt a few feet down, not the many feet of clean granular material a driven well needs.

Depth to water is the second problem. Static water levels on the benches and in the foothills often sit well past the 30 to 50 foot ceiling of a driven well, and they drop further in the hot, dry summer months when irrigation demand and falling groundwater levels stack up. A driven well that barely reaches water in a wet spring can come up dry by August, right when a household or an orchard needs it most. Our guide on [how deep wells run in this valley](/blog/how-deep-are-wells-wenatchee/) lays out why depth here varies so much block to block.

There are pockets where the geology cooperates, mainly low terraces close to the Wenatchee or Columbia River where alluvial sand and gravel run deep enough and the water table sits high for at least part of the year. Even there, a driven well is a shallow, seasonal source rather than a dependable year-round supply, and it is not sized for orchard irrigation demand, which needs far more flow than a 1 1/4 or 2 inch pipe can deliver.

## The Real Risk: Shallow Water Is Exposed Water

A driven well's biggest practical drawback is not depth. It is water quality. Because the screen sits only a few feet to a few tens of feet down, and the sanitary protection is a grouted seal around a narrow pipe rather than a fully cased and grouted deep well, there is a much shorter path between the surface and the water you are pumping.

The EPA is specific about how that path works: [runoff and snowmelt](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/potential-well-water-contaminants-and-their-impacts) can wash microorganisms into a well system or carry them down through the soil, nitrate from fertilizer, septic systems, and animal waste reaches groundwater the same way, and organic chemicals from spills or waste disposal follow the same surface-to-groundwater route. In farm and orchard country, a shallow well sitting anywhere near a septic drainfield, a barn, a chemical storage area, or a sprayed field is drawing from the same shallow layer those things drain into.

None of that means a driven well is automatically unsafe. It means the water deserves a real look before anyone drinks it, and another look after anything that changes the ground around it. At minimum, test for coliform bacteria and nitrate, and retest after heavy runoff, spring snowmelt, or flooding near the wellhead. ## What Washington Law Actually Requires

Washington regulates who can build a well and how it has to be sealed, and both pieces apply to a driven well the same as any other. Under [WAC 173-162-050](https://apps.leg.wa.gov/wac/default.aspx?cite=173-162-050), a landowner can personally construct a well, including a driven well, on land they own or lease for farm or single-family residential use without holding a well driller's license, but only up to one well every two years, and only for their own property. Everyone else, and anyone constructing a well more often than that, has to be licensed under chapter 173-162 WAC.

The exemption from licensing does not exempt anyone from the construction standards. Whoever drives the pipe still has to meet the sealing requirement in WAC 173-160-271 covered above, along with the fee and notification requirements that apply to any well in the state. Skipping the sealing standard is not a shortcut; it is the difference between a driven well that stays sanitary and one that is an open channel for surface water.

## If You Already Have One: Living With a Driven Well

Driven wells sometimes predate the current owner entirely. They can turn up on older acreage, cabin lots, and outbuildings, sometimes forgotten until a pump quits or someone notices a narrow pipe sticking out of the ground that does not match the main well serving the house. A few things are worth checking on an existing driven well. The top of the pipe should sit above grade with a tight, intact cap, not broken, buried, or capped with something improvised. If the seal at the surface looks disturbed or missing, treat that as a sanitary problem to fix, not just cosmetic damage. And because the small pipe diameter rules out ever dropping in a submersible pump, a driven well that is falling short in late summer usually is not a candidate for a bigger pump. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I install a sand point well myself in Washington?

On your own land, for your own farm or single-family use, yes, up to one well every two years, without needing a well driller's license. That exemption comes from WAC 173-162-050. It does not exempt you from the construction rules. The well still has to meet the state's sealing standard for driven wells, including the oversized upper hole packed with bentonite and grouted to the surface, plus the standard fee and notification requirements that apply to any well in Washington.

### How deep can a sand point well go?

The EPA puts driven wells at roughly 30 to 50 feet deep, with hand-driven points usually at the shallow end and weighted hammers or driving equipment needed to go deeper. That ceiling is set by the driving method itself, not just effort. Once you hit bedrock, dense clay, or hardpan, or once the water table sits deeper than that, driving a point stops working no matter how much force you apply, and a drilled well becomes the only real option.

### Is water from a driven well safe to drink?

It can be, but it needs testing before you assume so. Because the water comes from just a few feet to a few tens of feet down, it sits much closer to surface contamination sources than a deep drilled well does. The EPA lists runoff, septic systems, fertilizer, and spills as common routes into shallow groundwater. Test at minimum for coliform bacteria and nitrate, and retest after any flooding, heavy runoff, or snowmelt event near the wellhead, before treating the result as settled.

### What pump goes on a sand point well?

Because the pipe is only 1 1/4 or 2 inches across, a submersible pump will not fit down it. Instead, a shallow well jet pump sits above ground and pulls water up by suction, which works to roughly 25 feet of lift, or in older and off-grid setups, a hand pump does the job manually. If a driven well is at or near its depth limit and cannot keep up, the fix is not a stronger jet pump. It is usually a properly cased drilled well sized for a submersible pump instead.
