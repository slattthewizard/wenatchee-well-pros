# Well Water Smells Like Sewage? Here's What It Actually Means

Say you fill a glass at the kitchen sink and it smells like a septic tank instead of water. Not the sharp rotten-egg smell most people expect from a well. Something heavier, more like sewage or a musty basement. It's an unsettling smell to catch in your own drinking water, and it deserves a clear-headed look instead of a guess.

The good news is that a sewage smell usually has a traceable source, and you can rule several of them out yourself in the next ten minutes. The more serious possibility, that your septic system is actually reaching your well, is less likely but real, and it's the one you don't want to talk yourself out of testing for.

## Sewage Smell and Rotten Egg Smell Are Not the Same Problem

Before you go any further, get the smell itself right. A sharp, sulfur, rotten-egg odor is hydrogen sulfide gas, usually from sulfur bacteria living in the well or the plumbing, and it's a different animal from what you're describing. If that's closer to what you're smelling, our guide to [well water that smells like rotten eggs](/blog/well-water-smells-rotten-eggs/) covers that cause and what fixes it, and none of it applies here.

What you're after is a heavier, organic, "outhouse" smell, sometimes described as musty, swampy, or like a septic tank. That smell points toward decaying organic matter or actual sewage-related bacteria rather than sulfur gas. The two get confused constantly because both are unpleasant and both come out of a faucet, but the cause, and the risk, are different enough that it's worth being honest with yourself about which one you actually have.

## Check the Drain Before You Blame the Well

Often, a sewage smell in a bathroom or kitchen has nothing to do with the water at all. It's the drain. Here's the order we'd check it in:

1. **Fill a glass directly from the faucet and step away from the sink before you smell it.** Sink and tub drains hold a trap of water that blocks sewer gas from the drain line. If that trap runs dry, it doesn't work, and the smell you're catching is coming up out of the drain, not out of the tap.
2. **Check a fixture that gets used rarely**, like a guest bathroom or a basement floor drain. Those traps dry out fastest, and a dry trap can easily pass for a sewage smell coming from the water.
3. **Run the garbage disposal and smell the sink drain itself.** Trapped food waste rots in the disposal housing and smells exactly like sewage.
4. **Smell water drawn straight from an outside spigot near the wellhead**, away from any drain. If it's clean out there, the smell is in your house plumbing or fixtures, not your water supply.
5. **Pour a cup of water down any rarely used drain** to refill a dry trap, then wait a day and see if the smell in that room is gone.

If the smell disappears once you isolate it to one fixture, or it goes away after refilling a dry trap, you've found it, and it isn't a well problem.

## Rule Out the Water Heater Too

If the sewage-like smell only shows up in hot water and the cold water from the same tap smells clean, the water heater is the more likely source, not the well. A degrading anode rod or bacteria growing in a tank that runs cooler than it should can produce an off odor in hot water specifically. We've covered that mechanism, which usually shows up as a sulfur smell rather than a true sewage smell, in our piece on [hot water that smells like sulfur from the anode rod](/blog/hot-water-sulfur-smell-anode-rod/). The test is simple: smell the cold tap, then the hot tap, from the same fixture. If only the hot water smells off, start there before you spend time or money on the well itself.

## If the Smell Really Is in the Water Itself

Once you've ruled out a dry trap, the disposal, and the water heater, and the smell is in cold water drawn straight from an outside spigot, the source is somewhere in the well, the wellhead, or the pressure system. A few things can produce an organic, sewage-like odor that isn't hydrogen sulfide:

- **Iron bacteria.** These organisms are usually known for orange slime and clogged screens, but according to [Penn State Extension's guide to iron and sulfur bacteria](https://extension.psu.edu/iron-and-sulfur-bacteria-a-slimy-problem), the smell they produce can read as musty, oily, or sewage-like rather than the classic rotten-egg odor. We cover the slime and clogging side of this in our [iron bacteria in well water](/blog/iron-bacteria-well-slime/) article, and it's worth a look if you've also noticed orange residue in a toilet tank.
- **Standing water or decaying organic material around the wellhead.** A well cap that's cracked, unsealed, or missing a screen can let leaves, insects, or small animals into the casing, and a well pit that collects standing water gives the same thing a place to sit and rot right above your water supply.
- **Biofilm in the pressure tank or plumbing**, especially if the system has sat unused for a while, like a seasonal property or a fixture nobody runs.

None of these are things to diagnose by smell alone. They're reasons to get a water sample tested, which is the next step no matter which of these turns out to be the cause.

## Why This Can Be a Septic Problem, Not Just a Well Problem

Here's the possibility that actually matters. A sewage odor is one of the few well symptoms that can mean wastewater from a septic system, a neighbor's system, or an old failed drainfield, is reaching your well. This isn't the most common explanation, but it's the one you have to rule out before you write the smell off as cosmetic.

Washington sets minimum separation distances between a septic system's drainfield and a drinking water well for exactly this reason: keep wastewater from traveling into the groundwater a well draws from. We've laid out what those distances are and how they get measured in [our guide to septic and well separation distance in Washington](/blog/septic-well-separation-distance-washington/), so we won't repeat the numbers here. What matters right now is the "why": a well sitting too close to a drainfield, an old or failing septic system upslope, or a shallow well in ground that doesn't filter wastewater well, can all put actual sewage-related bacteria into a water supply. The Chelan-Douglas Health District's [on-site septic program](https://www.cdhd.wa.gov/on-site-septic-program) is the local authority on drainfield siting and failing-system complaints in Chelan and Douglas counties, and it's the right call if you suspect a specific system nearby is the source.

You can't confirm or rule out a septic connection by smell. You confirm it with a water test.

## Test Before You Drink Anything Else

Here's the honest version, described plainly rather than dramatically: until you know what's in the water, don't keep drinking it. Use bottled water for drinking, cooking, brushing teeth, ice, and anything for a baby. Water you suspect may carry sewage-related bacteria isn't worth the risk while you wait for a lab result.

The [EPA's guidance for private well owners](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/protect-your-homes-water) lists a change in your water's odor, color, or taste as a reason to test, and Washington's Department of Health is plain that if you own a private well, testing it is your responsibility. The [CDC's guidelines for testing well water](https://www.cdc.gov/drinking-water/safety/guidelines-for-testing-well-water.html) recommend testing at least once a year for total coliform bacteria and nitrate (along with total dissolved solids and pH) as a baseline, and testing again any time taste, smell, or color changes. Coliform bacteria live in soil, on plants, and in the guts of people and animals, and while they're typically harmless themselves, they get into water the same way disease-causing germs do, so a positive result means the pathway that let them in could also be letting in something that isn't. Washington's own guidance from the [Department of Health on testing your water](https://doh.wa.gov/community-and-environment/drinking-water/contaminants/testing-your-water) recommends testing every year for the same two, coliform and nitrate.

For a sewage-smell complaint specifically, ask the lab for:

| Test | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Total coliform / E. coli | Whether bacteria from soil, animal waste, or human waste has reached your water |
| Nitrate | A marker for septic and fertilizer contamination reaching groundwater, and a real health concern at higher levels, particularly for infants |

A state-certified lab runs both from one sample. We've written a full walkthrough of [what to test for and when in Wenatchee](/blog/well-water-testing-wenatchee/), including how to take the sample correctly so the result actually means something, and a companion piece on [what a positive coliform result actually means](/blog/coliform-bacteria-well-water/) once you have it in hand. ## If the Test Comes Back Positive

A positive coliform result doesn't automatically mean your septic system is the cause, and it doesn't automatically mean the well itself has failed. It means something is letting outside contamination in, and the job now is finding where.

If the entry point turns out to be the wellhead itself, a cracked casing, a bad seal, a well cap without a proper screen, the fix comes first and disinfecting the well comes right after it. Chlorinating a well that still has an open door just buys a clean test before the next contamination. We've covered the disinfection process start to finish in our guide to [shock chlorination for a well](/blog/well-chlorination-shock/). If the well retests clean afterward, you've likely found and fixed a wellhead problem. If contamination keeps coming back after disinfection, that's a stronger sign the source is ongoing, like a drainfield too close for the ground to filter it, and that's a conversation with your health district about the septic system, not another round of chlorine in the well.

## Keeping Surface Water and Sewage Away From Your Well

Once your water tests clean, the goal is keeping it that way. A few things go a long way:

- A well cap that's properly vented and screened, not cracked, loose, or missing a screen entirely.
- No standing water pooling around the wellhead, especially after spring runoff, since pooled water is exactly what finds its way in through a bad seal.
- Knowing where your septic drainfield sits relative to your well, and not assuming an older system still meets today's separation standards.
- Retesting for coliform and nitrate every year, not just when something smells wrong. A yearly baseline is what lets you catch a slow change before it turns into a sewage-smell phone call.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it dangerous to drink well water that smells like sewage?

It can be, which is why it's worth taking seriously instead of waiting it out. A sewage-like smell can come from a harmless source like a dry drain trap, but it can also mean bacteria from a septic system or decaying organic matter has reached your water. Until a coliform and nitrate test comes back clean, use bottled water for drinking, cooking, brushing teeth, and anything for a baby, and don't put off testing.

### How is a sewage smell different from a rotten egg smell in well water?

A rotten egg smell is sharp and sulfurous, caused by hydrogen sulfide gas from sulfur bacteria or naturally occurring sulfur in groundwater. A sewage smell is heavier and more organic, closer to a septic tank or a musty basement, and points toward decaying matter, iron bacteria, or in rarer cases actual contamination reaching the well. If you're not sure which one you have, the section above walks through how to tell them apart.

### Can a septic system really contaminate a well?

Yes, though it's not the most common explanation for an odd smell. It happens when a well sits too close to a drainfield, when an older septic system predates current setback rules, or when a system is failing and wastewater isn't being filtered through enough soil before it reaches groundwater. Washington sets minimum separation distances for exactly this reason. A positive coliform or nitrate test, combined with a nearby septic system, is the pattern that points here rather than to the well itself.

### What should I do right now if my well water smells like sewage?

Check the obvious things first: run water from an outside spigot away from any drain, smell hot versus cold from the same tap, and see if a rarely used drain trap has run dry. If the smell is in the water itself, stop drinking it and get a sample tested for total coliform, E. coli, and nitrate at a certified lab. Use bottled water for drinking, cooking, and brushing teeth in the meantime, and don't guess at a treatment fix before you know what the test actually shows.
